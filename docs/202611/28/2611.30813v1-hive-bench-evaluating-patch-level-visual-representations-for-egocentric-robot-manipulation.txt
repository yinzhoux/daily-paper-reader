ABSTRACT
Pretrained vision is a common start for robot learning, but most comparisons still score
one pooled vector from a third-person image. Bimanual policies instead read dense patch
tokens from cameras on the robot. We introduce HIVE-Bench: one shared flow-matching
policy reads 20 encoders from seven families on 24 egocentric bimanual tasks, across
adaptation, task sharing, and sweeps of data, model size, and layer. Keeping the patches
matters. Averaging each view to one token removes most of the success, adaptation helps
every pair we test, and one shared policy keeps most of the success of separate policies at
a fraction of the updates. The same tokens are then scored with statistics, state and object
readouts, published vision scores, and an inverse-dynamics readout. That readout of the
commanded motion is one of the strongest measurements available before a policy is trained.
Recognition, depth, and correspondence do not transfer reliably. Fine-tuning can improve
the action readout even when other measurements move the other way, and no statistic is
stable enough to replace a rollout before any deployment Codebase & Benchmark Zoo.

1 Introduction
Robot manipulation policies increasingly reuse pretrained visual encoders instead of learning vision from
scratch, which transfers poorly across objects, robots, and viewpoints [1–5]. The candidates come from
very different signals, including self-supervision, language alignment, 3D reconstruction, robot data, and
vision–language models [6–10]. Choosing among them still means training and rolling out a policy.
A benchmark with a shared policy can reveal which representations transfer, and whether inexpensive
measurements explain their successes and failures across different training conditions.
Earlier evaluations often read a pooled third-person feature and relate scene reconstruction to control [2, 3, 11–
15]. Bimanual policies do not use that interface. They attend to dense patch tokens, which keep the local
detail that separates nearby grasps [16–19], and they see through head and wrist cameras that move with the
robot and are often occluded [20–23]. We therefore ask how encoders compare when one policy reads their
patch tokens from onboard cameras, and which properties of those tokens track the resulting success.
We introduce HIVE-Bench (Figure 1), to our knowledge the first comprehensive benchmark of patch-level
representations for egocentric bimanual manipulation that jointly studies control and diagnostic correlations
across training regimes. A flow-matching action head [24, 25] reads 20 encoders from seven families on
two egocentric bimanual suites [22, 26]. We train it frozen and fine-tuned, on one task and on a shared
suite, and we sweep data, model size, and the extracted layer, because a ranking from a single regime is easy
to over-read. The same tokens are then scored in several ways: token statistics, state and object readouts,
published vision benchmarks, and an inverse-dynamics readout (IDM) that recovers the commanded motion.
Those runs support three simple conclusions. Keeping the patch tokens, rather than averaging each view into
one, accounts for most of the success of the encoders we compare this way. Fine-tuning improves every
encoder we adapt, and one policy shared across tasks keeps most of the success of separate policies at a
fraction of the updates. The measurements are the second half of the study. Several of them correlate with
success somewhere, but none is stable enough to prove which encoder a robot should use. IDM is among
the strongest readouts, especially on RoboTwin, yet even its ranking misses the best encoder and fails to
predict gains from adaptation. Our goal is to map these relationships and their limits; finding measurements
that improve on IDM remains an open research problem. Our contributions are:
■An egocentric patch-level benchmark: one shared policy reads the dense patch tokens, not a pooled
vector, of 20 encoders from onboard cameras on two bimanual suites under one shared update budget,
through documented token interfaces (Section 3).
■Evaluation from many angles: single-task and multi-task training, frozen and fine-tuned encoders,
demonstration and model-size scaling, extraction layers, pooling, and optional robot state, with the action
head and optimizer held fixed (Section 4).
■A study of what tracks success, and where it stops: token statistics, action and state readouts, and
published vision scores, compared across suites, task sharing, and adaptation, with the cases where a
frozen ranking fails to predict improvement kept separate from the cases where it holds, and with IDM
among the strongest metrics (Sections 5 and 6).

3 HIVE-Bench
HIVE-Bench compares encoders by the closed-loop success of one shared patch-conditioned policy,
and asks which measurable properties of the tokens go with that success. The environments that sup-
ply the tasks and the policy that reads them are introduced separately below. Appendix A gives the
implementation and the training recipe.
3.1 Environments and protocols
We use 12 RoboCasa-GR1 kitchen tasks, performed by a humanoid whose 29-D action adds difficulty [25,
26, 49], and 12 domain-randomized RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, performed by two arms with grippers [22, 50]
(Figure 2; Table 23). RoboCasa covers object transfer and cabinet, drawer, and microwave interactions.
RoboTwin covers tool use, handover, placement, and articulated objects under randomized clutter, lighting,
and textures. The suites differ in robot, action, cameras, tasks, and data at once, so differences between them
are observational. Default policies see only the current images. One ablation adds robot state (Section 4.5).

Multi-task training (MT) fits one language-conditioned policy to the twelve tasks of a suite. Single-task
training (ST) fits a separate policy to each RoboCasa task. The closed-loop comparison covers 20 frozen
encoders under MT, 17 of them under ST, and five fine-tuned encoders on RoboCasa ST and on RoboTwin
MT (Table 2). Further controls vary the number of demonstrations, the checkpoint size, the extraction layer,
mean pooling, the state input, and the learned queries of the action head. Every configuration in the main
comparison is trained with one seed and evaluated with three evaluation seeds of 50 rollouts per task. A suite
score is the mean of the twelve task scores, and its deviation propagates the per-task evaluation deviations. A
three-seed RoboCasa MT study, including its stability analysis, is reported in Appendix C.7.
3.2 Encoders and the token interface
The 20 encoders span seven families (Table 6): a supervised ViT [51]; the self-supervised MAE, DINOv2,
DINOv3, and V-JEPA 2.1 [6, 7, 52, 53]; the geometry-oriented SPA, VGGT-Ω, and LingBot-Vision [9, 54, 55];
the vision–language CLIP, SigLIP, SigLIP2, and InternViT [8, 56–58]; the robot-oriented VC-1 and
Voltron [2, 27]; the multi-teacher Theia, RADIOv2.5, and C-RADIOv4 [14, 28, 29]; and three VLMs,
Qwen3-VL and its derivatives DepthVLM and Xiaomi-Robotics-1 [10, 59, 60]. Table 3 lists sizes and
token counts. Appendix B documents checkpoints and preprocessing.
The policy reads every patch token of every view. Class and register tokens are removed, and there is no
extra feature normalization at the policy interface. Views are stored at 224 × 224, giving 196 or 256 tokens
per view for patch size 16 or 14. V-JEPA 2.1 resizes to 384 pixels and produces 576 tokens, and each VLM
contributes 64 final-layer image tokens. Those VLM states do not depend on the instruction, and we do not use
Xiaomi-Robotics-1’s action head. Two interfaces mix views before the policy reads them: VGGT-Ω receives
RoboTwin’s three views as one input, and a VLM’s image tokens of later views can attend to earlier ones. We
therefore compare an encoder under a documented extraction interface, not in isolation (Appendix B).

The head regresses the flow-matching velocity toward the normalized 16-step action chunk. The flow
time is drawn from Beta(1.5, 1), with eight noise draws per sample, and inference integrates 15 Euler
steps on RoboCasa or 10 on RoboTwin. Action heads span 138.669–156.478M parameters. Appendix A
gives complete downstream counts and the flow objective.
Each policy is trained for 100k AdamW steps with a global batch of 128 on four GPUs, in BF16, with
warmup and cosine decay. The budget is equal per policy, not per task, so twelve single-task policies
consume twelve times the updates of one multi-task policy on the same suite. The action head uses a peak
learning rate of 10−4. Fine-tuning updates the encoder at 10−5. Other trainable projections use 3 × 10−5
on RoboCasa MT and 10−4 otherwise. Frozen runs update the head and its projections only. Table 3
reports training FLOPs and step time, and Appendix A gives the complete recipe.
3.4 Diagnostics and statistics
The diagnostics describe each encoder from head-camera frames, before asking which of those descrip-
tions agree with closed-loop success (Appendix E).
■Token statistics need only frames: patch similarity, norms, covariance rank, spatial frequency, and their
change through an episode. Norm-histogram entropy [14] bins each frame’s patch norms into 30 bins.
Features come from the early frames of every fifth demonstration.
■IDM and state probes use four-layer, width-256 transformers and a learned query; only input projections
grow with encoder width. The IDM probe reads frames 16 steps apart and predicts the standardized
command displacement 𝑎𝑡+15 −𝑠𝑡. A single-frame control predicts that same target from 𝐼𝑡, and the state
probe decodes 𝑠𝑡. An object probe covers four RoboTwin tasks. Because the IDM target contains 𝑠𝑡, part
of it is readable from one frame (Appendix E.2).
■Published vision scores are collected for the checkpoints that report them under a shared protocol,
covering classification, segmentation, depth, correspondence, and label propagation (Section 5.2).
Associations use Spearman’s 𝜌, a permutation test, a 95% bootstrap interval, and a Benjamini–Hochberg
correction within each cohort. Primary cohorts are the vision encoders; readouts also include the VLMs.
Family deletion and paired before–after comparisons test whether an association depends on related
encoders or on adaptation (Appendix E.3).
These measurements are cheaper than training the policy. Token statistics need only demonstration frames,
the probes train for four epochs on frozen tokens, and the vision scores come from published evaluations
rather than new robot runs. Section 5 then asks which of them agrees with closed-loop success, and which
of those agreements disappear when the benchmark or the training regime changes.

4 What Makes a Visual Representation Useful for Control?
We first examine the policy interface, then encoder choice, task sharing, scale, and additional inputs.
Success rates are percentages; changes are in percentage points.
4.1 Preserve spatial tokens, then adapt the encoder
Mean pooling discards much of the information the policy uses.
Replacing each view’s patches
with their mean and retraining the same head reduces DINOv2 from 32.28% to 9.94% and DINOv3
from 31.22% to 6.44% on RoboCasa MT (Figure 4a). The loss appears in 23 of 24 encoder–task pairs
(Appendix C.2). Scene identity can survive pooling while local distinctions needed to act disappear;
evaluating individual patches exposes that difference.
Fine-tuning improves success, with returns that depend on the encoder.
Jointly adapting five encoders
and the head improves all ten benchmark pairs (Figure 4b): gains span 1.11–9.44 points on RoboCasa ST and
2.22–26.06 on RoboTwin MT. VGGT-Ω rises from 50.00% to 76.06% on RoboTwin, whereas DINOv2 moves
from 49.39% to 51.61%. The five retain their frozen ordering there; on RoboCasa, DINOv3 overtakes DINOv2
and VC-1. A strong starting point and a large return to adaptation are distinct advantages (Appendix C.2).
4.2 Multi-task performance across encoders
Strong encoders emerge from several pretraining families.
We next hold the patch-conditioned interface
fixed and compare the full encoder suite under MT (Table 3). VGGT-Ω leads RoboCasa at 35.39%,
followed by C-RADIOv4 at 34.78% and DINOv2 at 32.28%. The three VLMs lead RoboTwin at 51.44–
53.67%.
Cross-benchmark ranks agree only moderately (𝜌= 0.43): VC-1 outperforms V-JEPA 2.1
on RoboCasa but trails it on RoboTwin.
Pretraining family alone is too broad a label to settle the
choice; the target setting changes which strengths matter.
Cost also changes the choice. DINOv2 comes within 3.11 points of VGGT-Ω on RoboCasa at 54.8 versus
113.3 TFLOPs per step, while the VLMs lead RoboTwin at 195.3 TFLOPs per step. A shared optimizer
schedule therefore does not make the encoders equally expensive to train, and the cheaper run can be
the practical choice when the success gap is only a few points.

One shared policy preserves most of the success of task-specific policies.

4.4 More demonstrations help more consistently than more parameters
Additional demonstrations improve every curve.
We retrain policies on nested demonstration subsets
with the same update budget. Increasing demonstrations per task from 50 to 1,000 adds 24.44–28.16
7


points across RoboCasa MT encoders. All RoboTwin curves improve from 50 to 500; SigLIP2 gains
34.94 points and overtakes DINOv3 (Figure 5a,b). On matched RoboCasa tasks C1–C4, doubling 500

The value of model scale depends on the family.
Within-family sweeps show a less uniform return.
DINOv2 improves from Small to Base, then plateaus on RoboTwin and declines on RoboCasa; DINOv3
Base is its family’s strongest RoboCasa checkpoint. V-JEPA 2.1 instead rises from 18.67% to 36.22% on

(Figure 5c,d). Scale helps when the additional capacity transfers to the policy.
4.5 Input and extraction choices have different returns
Robot state and finer image grids give smaller gains.
Two controls test whether missing proprioception
or a coarser grid explains the encoder gaps. A state token improves DINOv2 and RADIOv2.5 by 0.39–2.97
points and preserves their ordering (Figure 6a,b). More than doubling DINOv3’s tokens, from 196 to
400, adds 1.17 and 4.17 points. Both effects are smaller than retaining patches in the first place, so the
image stream, not a missing state token, carries most of the gap.
An intermediate layer can recover much of a VLM’s RoboCasa deficit.
Replacing final-layer features
with layer 16 tests whether useful information is easier to read earlier in the network. Qwen3-VL and Xiaomi
gain 8–11 points on RoboCasa and change by less than a point on RoboTwin (Figure 6c). The useful layer is

5.1 Action readout is informative, but shares a static component
IDM is one of the strongest readouts of closed-loop success.
We ask which cheap measurement
best ranks frozen encoders before a policy is trained. Among vision encoders, two-frame IDM error
correlates with RoboTwin MT success at 𝜌= −0.75 (95% interval [−0.90, −0.40], 𝑞= 0.020), and
removing any pretraining family leaves 𝜌between −0.88 and −0.70. The association keeps the expected
sign on the tasks we test. IDM is thus a strong correlate on RoboTwin, although even this association
does not reliably identify the best encoder for control.
Matching probe capacity preserves the state association.
With the same width-256 transformer, state
error correlates with success at −0.44, −0.39, and −0.62 across the three vision cohorts. Including VLMs
leaves associations of −0.53, −0.50, and −0.50 (Table 4). Object error behaves differently: its RoboTwin
correlation changes from −0.54 to −0.05 with VLMs. Recovering robot configuration and object position
therefore give different assessments of the same representations.
A future frame improves prediction, but does not isolate motion.

later observation, rather than isolating motion understanding.

ImageNet linear-probe accuracy
gives 𝜌= 0.07 on RoboCasa and 0.38 on RoboTwin.

5.4 Combining diagnostics and selecting candidates
More diagnostics help selectively.
Nested family-held-out prediction tests whether diagnostics complement
one another (Table 5). IDM lowers RoboTwin MAE from 10.91 to 6.73 points, and state or entropy adds
no further benefit there. Adding every measurement at once loses to the best smaller subset in every
setting. Extra measurements help only when their information transfers, so the gain comes from keeping
the measurement that is new in that setting, not from collecting a longer list.
A useful screen need not identify the best encoder.
The five lowest-IDM encoders retain 53.22%
RoboTwin success, 0.45 points below the best, using one quarter of the runs. The same screen falls below
random selection on both RoboCasa regimes (Table 20). A shortlist can therefore sit close to the best encoder
and still miss it, which is why the remaining candidates are checked with policy runs.
The benchmark matters more than task sharing.
Profiles agree much more closely within RoboCasa
(𝑟= 0.95) than between the two benchmarks (𝑟= 0.65), so changing the robot and the views changes
which properties accompany success. Sharing the tasks barely changes that profile, and a diagnostic chosen
on single-task data remains informative when those tasks are trained together.
6 Discussion and Conclusion
HIVE-Bench connects dense egocentric representations to the policies that use them. Preserving patches,
adding demonstrations, and adapting features improve control, and task sharing broadens coverage
cheaply. IDM is one of the strongest guides to which frozen encoder will succeed, but no measure-
ment is stable enough to prove that choice.
Three physical tasks preserve DINOv2’s lead over DINOv3 and CLIP (Appendix D). The other evidence
uses one head and two suites, so more tasks and seeds are still needed.
The open question is how to improve on IDM while keeping closed-loop success as the final measure.

C.5 Learned query tokens in the action head
The default DiT prepends 32 learned query tokens to the 16 noisy action tokens. These queries supply
additional latent positions through which self-attention and cross-attention can exchange information;
the action decoder reads the action-token positions.
They are part of the action head, distinct from
class or register tokens produced by an encoder.
Removing the 32 queries reduces RoboCasa MT success from 32.28% to 27.78% for DINOv2 and from 31.22%

sitions improve success by 4.50 and 7.72 points. The learned queries help the common head use visual context.

E.2 Readouts and controls
IDM and state readouts use a 𝐷→256 input projection, a four-layer pre-norm transformer with four attention
heads and feed-forward width 512, and a learned query. The readout applies LayerNorm and a two-layer
width-256 head with GELU. Dropout is 0.1. Parameters in the transformer are shared in architecture, not in
learned weights, across encoders; only the input projection grows with encoder width. This removes the
quadratic growth in state-probe capacity that would occur if the transformer used native feature width.
26


The two-frame input is [𝑞; 𝑍𝑡; 𝑍𝑡+16], with within-frame sinusoidal positions and frame-segment embeddings.
The single-frame control is [𝑞; 𝑍𝑡]. Both predict the same standardized target 𝑎𝑡+15 −𝑠𝑡and report mean
squared error per coordinate. The state probe predicts standardized 𝑠𝑡. Targets use training-set statistics.
Input length changes from 1 + 𝑁to 1 + 2𝑁in the two-frame comparison, so its error reduction combines
a later observation with additional attention computation; it does not isolate motion sensitivity.
Probes train for four epochs using AdamW, learning rate 5 × 10−4, weight decay 10−4, cosine decay to
1% of the peak, batch 256, clipping at 1, and seed 42. The episode split is 1,000/500 train/held-out
on RoboCasa and 400/100 on RoboTwin, stratified across tasks. The lowest held-out error selects the
epoch and is the reported value, making it a validation error. Diagnostic encoders remain frozen even
when the checkpoint was previously fine-tuned with the policy.
The object probe reads the first head-camera frame and predicts the camera-frame 3D location of the task
object on four RoboTwin tasks: bottle, hammer/block, alarm clock, and laptop. It uses a native-width four-layer
transformer and reports coordinate-averaged absolute error in meters, with 400 training and 100 held-out
episodes per task. Its capacity and task coverage differ from the width-256 action/state probes. All readouts use
the head view; RoboTwin policies additionally see wrist views and, for some interfaces, features that mix views.
E.3 Statistics and analysis cohorts
Spearman correlation is Pearson correlation between mid-ranks, preserving ties. A two-sided permutation
probability uses 100,000 permutations of success across encoders, with (1 + #{|𝜌𝑏| ≥|𝜌|})/(100001).
Cohorts of at most eight use all permutations. Bootstrap intervals use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of
10,000 encoder resamples; resamples with zero rank variance are discarded. Permutations use a fixed seed
indexed by sample size, and bootstrap seeds use a CRC-32 hash of the analysis name.
The primary family covers all corrected token/readout metrics: 24 on RoboCasa and 25 on RoboTwin
(including object error). Benjamini–Hochberg correction is applied within each cohort. Single-frame
IDM is analyzed as a control, with a separate three-readout correction available alongside the primary
results. The vision-only cohorts contain 14 encoders on Casa ST and 17 on each MT suite; adding
VLM readouts gives 17 and 20. Position-dependent statistics have one fewer encoder because MAE is
excluded. The same RoboCasa diagnostic measurements are paired with ST and MT success. Both
settings therefore share encoders and diagnostic inputs.
Family deletion removes all encoders in one of the seven groups of Table 6 before recomputing 𝜌. The
12 task-level associations reuse the same suite-level diagnostic and correlate it with each task’s success.
Encoders can also share teachers across these groups, so these checks assess sensitivity to composition
rather than establish independent replications. Partial Spearman correlation regresses the ranks of IDM
error and success separately on state-error ranks, then correlates the residuals.
Paired adaptation analyses use DINOv2, DINOv3, VC-1, SigLIP2, and VGGT-Ω, each with corrected frozen
and fine-tuned success scores. Pooled correlations combine these with the frozen vision cohort; each adapted
checkpoint shares its initialization with a frozen one. VGGT’s unchanged frequency entries are excluded
from frequency-change analyses. No policy-fine-tuned vision-benchmark scores are used.
Family-held-out prediction.
For each outer fold, ridge regression holds out all encoders in one pretraining
family. Each feature is standardized using only the training fold. Inner family-held-out validation selects
the penalty from {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100} by mean absolute error; the intercept is unpenalized. Predictions
are evaluated in success points. All models use the same complete-case cohort, excluding VLM token
statistics and MAE’s temporal norm entropy. This gives 13 encoders for Casa ST and 16 for each MT
suite.
The mean baseline predicts the outer training mean.
