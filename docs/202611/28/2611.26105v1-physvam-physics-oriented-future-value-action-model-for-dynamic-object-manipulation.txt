Abstract
Predicting future scene or object states does not necessarily reveal how robot–object inter-
actions will advance or hinder a task. This challenge is particularly pronounced in dynamic
robotic manipulation, where objects continue moving during perception and execution. Ef-
fective action generation therefore requires not only modeling object dynamics but also
anticipating how future interactions will shape task progress. To this end, we propose Phys-
VAM, a Physics-oriented Future Value Action Model that represents anticipated interac-
tions through their effects on future task progress. This value-based formulation provides
task-directed foresight without reconstructing future visual states. Conditioned on current
observations and historical object and robot motion, PhysVAM jointly learns action gener-
ation and multi-horizon future-value prediction. The value branch learns horizon-speciﬁc
latent representations through future task-value prediction from successful and failed trajec-
tories. These future-value representations are integrated into the action policy through gated
cross-attention, guiding action generation toward interactions that facilitate task success.
Extensive simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate superior task success, with
absolute success-rate improvements of 27.0 and 27.5 percentage points over the strongest
baselines in simulation and real-world tasks, respectively.

Introduction

ronments, where objects continue to move during robot perception and action execution [1, 2]. Suc-
cessful manipulation therefore requires actions to remain coordinated with evolving robot–object
interactions. Changes in object motion and contact timing can alter whether an intended interaction
will succeed before its execution is complete. A central challenge is thus to learn predictive repre-
sentations that anticipate how future interactions may affect task progress and guide timely action

Recent approaches pursue visual future modeling through future video generation [3, 4] and visual-
feature prediction [5, 6]. These methods provide predictive context by modeling how scenes and
objects may evolve. However, as illustrated in Fig. 1(A-a), generated futures may exhibit geo-
metric distortions and inconsistent contact predictions, compromising their reliability for physical
interaction [7]. More fundamentally, predicting future visual or object states alone does not ex-
plicitly characterize how anticipated robot–object interactions will advance or hinder task progress.
These limitations motivate a future modeling objective that directly reﬂects the task consequences
of physical interactions.
We approach future modeling from a task-value perspective, representing anticipated robot–object
interactions through their effects on future task progress. Rather than reconstructing future vi-
sual states, this perspective models how task progress is expected to evolve. Existing value-based
approaches have demonstrated the usefulness of task evaluation for policy improvement [8–10].
However, current-value assessment does not explicitly characterize how task progress may evolve
through subsequent interactions. For instance, a favorable current value may still be followed by
a missed grasp as the target moves before gripper closure. Predicting task values across multi-
ple future horizons instead provides temporally structured supervision for learning task-directed
representations that guide action generation, as illustrated in Fig. 1(A-b).
To instantiate this perspective, we propose PhysVAM, a Physics-oriented Future Value Action
2


Model for dynamic object manipulation. PhysVAM leverages current observations and historical
object and robot motion to model evolving interaction context, while jointly learning action gener-
ation and multi-horizon future-value prediction. The value branch predicts task values at multiple
future horizons and learns corresponding latent representations from both successful and failed
trajectories. These future-value representations capture anticipated task progress associated with
robot–object interactions and are integrated into the action policy through gated cross-attention,
guiding action generation toward interactions that promote task success. As illustrated in Fig. 1(B),
the predicted future values can reﬂect changes in task progress as interactions unfold.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We present a task-value perspective on future modeling for dynamic object manipulation,
leveraging multi-horizon future-value prediction to learn task-directed representations for ac-
tion guidance.
• We propose PhysVAM, a physics-oriented framework that integrates historical object and
robot motion understanding with multi-horizon future-value guidance for action generation.
• Extensive simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate improved task success and exe-
cution efﬁciency, with absolute success-rate gains of 27.0 and 27.5 percentage points over the
strongest baselines, respectively. Integrating our value branch into other action models yields
further improvements, demonstrating the transferability of future-value guidance.

Related Work
Vision–Language–Action and World–Action Models.
Vision-language-action (VLA) models
have advanced generalist robot manipulation by learning multimodal policies that map visual ob-
servations and language instructions to robot actions [1, 9, 11–13]. Track4Action [14] distills
action-aligned 3D transition features into learnable queries that condition action generation. Ex-
tending this paradigm, World Action Models (WAMs) [3, 4, 15–17] couple future scene prediction
with action generation to enhance temporal reasoning and robotic control. Recent approaches fur-
ther explore efﬁcient predictive modeling: Fast-WAM [3] leverages video modeling during train-
ing without test-time future generation, while ImageWAM [16] leverages pretrained image-editing
models to capture task-relevant visual transformations without future-video decoding. These ad-
vances demonstrate the beneﬁts of predictive world representations for action modeling, while
primarily focusing on scene evolution rather than explicit multi-horizon task-progress prediction.
Value Models for Robot Learning. Process value learning [9, 10, 18, 19] enables robots to as-
sess task progress and leverage interaction experience for policy improvement. Recent studies have
advanced value estimation through experience-based learning and predictive world representations.
Speciﬁcally, π∗
0.6 [9] employs value-based advantage learning for policy reﬁnement, while ViVa [8]
combines future robot motion prediction with current-state value estimation. WCM [10] strength-
ens value estimation through predictive world representations, and RISE [20] evaluates imagined
future states using a current progress value model for policy optimization. WVM [18] models
value sequences over observed video histories using a coupled video–value architecture. Together,
these methods demonstrate the beneﬁts of predictive representations for task-progress assessment.
In contrast, PhysVAM focuses on directly predicting task progress at multiple future offsets and
incorporating the corresponding query representations into action generation.

Overview

to move during robot perception and action execution. At time step t, the policy takes a language

future value learning. The action branch employs a 1B-parameter Diffusion Transformer, termed

information about the robot’s control behavior. The learned horizon-speciﬁc value-query features

success.
3.2
Historical Motion Encoding
Unlike static manipulation, where current observations are often sufﬁcient for action prediction,
dynamic manipulation requires reasoning about object motion, which may not be reliably inferred
from a single current observation [2, 4]. We therefore explicitly encode historical object motion
and executed robot actions to provide compact temporal context for action generation and value
estimation.
Object Motion.
In dynamic environments, task-relevant objects are often in motion, while the
surrounding background remains largely static. Historical object motion (e.g., trajectories) can pro-
vide compact cues about evolving object dynamics, offering useful temporal context for dynamic
manipulation [2, 4]. Motivated by [2, 24, 26], we leverage 3D object positions from four preced-
ing time steps: Po
t = [pt−4,..., pt−1], where pi ∈R3 to represent historical object trajectories. To
further characterize object motion, we estimate velocities using second-order accurate ﬁnite differ-
ences, with central differences at intermediate time steps and one-sided differences at the temporal
boundaries. The resulting position and velocity features are concatenated into 6D object motion
states and encoded into object history tokens to condition ActionDiT.
Robot Motion.
Robot–object interactions are central to manipulation, as task progress depends
on both object dynamics and the robot’s control behavior [5, 27]. In dynamic scenes, historical
robot actions reﬂect the robot’s recent control decisions and responses to object motion, providing
useful cues for estimating future interactions and task progress [28]. We represent robot motion
history using recent robot actions, Ha
t = {at−L,...,at−1}, where L denotes the history length. These
historical actions are encoded into robot history tokens and provided to ValueDiT, allowing future
value estimation to account for the robot’s recent control behavior alongside the evolving physical
interaction.
3.3

interaction. To anticipate how these interactions will shape future task progress, we introduce a
multi-horizon value branch that predicts future task values and learns horizon-speciﬁc latent repre-

4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Simulation Benchmark. Building upon the closed-loop reactivity setting of DOM [2], we recon-
struct dynamic manipulation scenarios with ten geometrically diverse objects, spanning spherical,
ellipsoidal, irregular, cylindrical, bottle-shaped, and cup-shaped forms. To emphasize high-speed
interactions, we redesign the object velocity distribution: Can, D-Bottle, W-Bottle, Egg, and Cup
scenes are evaluated at v ≥0.4m/s, while the remaining scenes are evaluated at 0.2 ≤v < 0.4m/s.
The resulting dataset comprises 12K successful trajectories and 10K failed trajectories, with the
latter collected from rollouts of PUMA [5], DynamicVLA [2], and FastWAM [3]. Each scene con-
tains at least 1K success and 1K failure trajectories. Following [2], we report task success rate and
task completion time. Task completion time is averaged over all trials and include termination by
success, timeout, or object drop.
Implementation Details. The ActionDiT comprises 30 transformer layers with a hidden dimension
of 1,024, while ValueDiT contains 12 layers with a hidden dimension of 512. Value-query features
from ValueDiT layers 4, 8, and 12 are projected and injected into ActionDiT layers 9, 19, and 27,
respectively. Both models are jointly trained for 24K steps using AdamW with a learning rate of
10−4 and a global batch size of 128. The weight λV is set to 0.1 and history length L is set as
four. We set 10 and 4 denoising steps for ActionDiT and ValueDiT, respectively. More Details are
provided in the Supplementary Material.
Compared Methods.
In simulation, PhysVAM is compared with FastWAM [3], InternVLA-
A1.5 [13], DynamicWAM [4], DynamicVLA [2], and PUMA [5]. Following [2], each method
is evaluated over 20 trials for each scene. All methods are ﬁne-tuned using their ofﬁcial codes and
7


released pretrained weights. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation over three random
seeds.
4.2
Main Results
As shown in Table 1, PhysVAM achieves an average success rate of 58.83%, outperforming the
strongest original baseline, PUMA, by 27.00 percentage points. It achieves the highest success
rate in eight of ten scenes and the shortest average completion time of 8.23 seconds under the
DOM evaluation protocol. FastWAM and InternVLA-A1.5 achieve average success rates below
6%, while DynamicWAM remains limited despite incorporating historical optical ﬂow and kine-
matic cues, highlighting the difﬁculty of translating motion awareness into successful interactions.
In high-speed scenes, DynamicVLA performs well on D-Bottle but remains inconsistent across
other scenes, while DynamicVLA and PUMA achieve only 22.00% and 21.00% success rates,
respectively. In contrast, PhysVAM reaches 57.67%, demonstrating the beneﬁt of combining his-
torical motion context with future-value guidance for dynamic action generation.

4.3
Ablation Studies
Effect of the Value Branch. We evaluate the contribution of future-value guidance to action gen-
eration. As shown in Table 2, removing the value branch reduces the success rate from 58.83% to
50.50%, demonstrating the effectiveness of future-value guidance for dynamic manipulation.
Effect of Value Representations. We replace the latent value representations injected into the
action policy with embeddings of either four predicted scalar values or binary good/bad indicators
obtained by mapping these predictions to {0,1}, following [9]. As shown in Table 2, both scalar and
binary conditioning underperform latent conditioning, suggesting that latent value representations
capture richer task-relevant information than explicit value scores and provide more effective action
guidance.
Effect of Multi-Horizon Value Prediction. We vary the number of value queries and their super-
vision targets while keeping the future-value ﬂow-matching objective unchanged. Under a shared
overall future-value target (averaged by four targets), increasing the number of queries from one to
four improves the success rate from 37.83% to 50.50%. Assigning distinct horizon-speciﬁc targets
to the four queries further improves performance to 58.83%. These results indicate that multiple
queries beneﬁt value learning even under shared supervision, while horizon-speciﬁc targets provide
additional gains by capturing task progress across different future horizons.
Effect of Future Progress Prediction. We replace the future-offset query targets with current-
value targets (h = 0), keeping the progress deﬁnition, query count, and injection scheme unchanged.
This reduces the success rate from 58.83% to 46.50%, a 12.33-percentage-point drop. Current-
value supervision focuses on present progress, whereas future-offset targets encourage horizon-
speciﬁc representations of how upcoming interactions may advance or hinder the task. These results
support our value-based future modeling formulation, highlighting the beneﬁt of anticipating task
progression for dynamic action generation.

Interaction-Sensitive Future-Value Predictions. Beyond task performance, we examine whether
future-value predictions reﬂect interaction-dependent changes in task progress. We focus on the
shortest prediction horizon (h = 4) and evaluate value changes over a ±16-step window centered
on gripper closure. Across 220 grasp attempts from 200 simulation episodes, predicted values in-
crease by 0.254 for successful lifts and decrease by 0.185 for missed grasps on average, yielding
a Lift–Miss difference of 0.439. Predicted values increase in 96/119 lift attempts and decrease
in 77/101 missed attempts, consistent with the progress gains and setbacks associated with these
interactions. Notably, across 33 episodes containing both interaction outcomes, the mean within-
episode Lift–Miss difference remains positive (+0.319), further supporting sensitivity to local in-
teractions beyond episode-level outcome labels. These results provide quantitative evidence that
future-value predictions respond to evolving robot–object interactions. Further analyses and more
visualizations are provided in the Supplementary Material.
Effect of Object Motion History. We compare different object-motion representations in Ta-
ble 3(a), while keeping the robot-action history ﬁxed. Without object-motion history, the success
rate drops to 17.17%, highlighting the importance of object dynamics for manipulation. Incorporat-
ing historical RGB observations or object positions improves performance to 22.33% and 30.67%,
respectively, indicating the advantage of explicit spatial trajectories over implicit visual histories.
Moreover, position-derived velocity achieves a success rate of 33.67%, comparable to 34.00% with
oracle velocity, suggesting that effective motion cues can be obtained without privileged velocity
information. Combining object positions and estimated velocities further increases performance
to 58.83%, substantially outperforming either representation alone. This improvement highlights
the complementary roles of spatial position and motion velocity in anticipating target displacement
and coordinating dynamic grasps.
Effect of Robot Motion History. As shown in Table 3(b), removing only historical robot actions
reduces the success rate from 58.83% to 45.17%, indicating that object-motion information alone
is insufﬁcient for effective dynamic manipulation. Historical robot actions provide complementary

temporal context for coordinating robot movements with evolving object dynamics.
Effect of History Length. As shown in Fig. 3, we vary object and robot motion history lengths
independently, ﬁxing the other at four frames. Increasing either history from two to four frames
improves success from 39.83% to 58.83% and from 43.67% to 58.83%, respectively, highlighting
the importance of temporal context for object dynamics and robot coordination. Extending either to
six frames reduces success to 49.67% and 46.00%, respectively, suggesting redundant information.
Finlay, we set the length of history frames as four. Further hyperparameter analyses, temporal
action reweighting, and per-object results are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we propose PhysVAM, a Physics-oriented Future Value Action Model that instan-
tiates value-based future modeling for dynamic manipulation. By integrating historical physical
motion modeling with multi-horizon future-value prediction, PhysVAM learns task-directed latent
representations to guide action generation toward task success. This formulation characterizes an-
ticipated robot–object interactions through their effects on task progress, providing task-directed
foresight without reconstructing future visual states. Extensive simulation and real-world exper-
iments demonstrate the effectiveness of PhysVAM in improving task success and execution efﬁ-
ciency, while gains across other action models further support the transferability of future-value
guidance.
12


Looking ahead, there are three promising directions for future work: 1) Toward Value-Guided Self-
Improvement. Our framework relies on ofﬂine trajectory supervision. Future work could leverage
future value predictions to identify interaction setbacks, guide recovery and exploration, and pro-
vide progress-aware feedback for online reinforcement learning. This could enable iterative policy
improvement through continuous interaction. 2) More Complex Dynamic Manipulation. Future
work could explore more object motions, multi-object interactions, and longer-horizon tasks requir-
ing sustained manipulation. 3) Value-Guided World-Action Models. PhysVAM predicts future task
values without explicitly modeling future scene dynamics. Integrating multi-horizon value guid-
ance with predictive WAMs could combine scene-dynamics prediction with task-directed action
generation, while maintaining efﬁcient inference for real-time manipulation.

A.2
Implementation Details
The value loss weight λV is set to 0.1 and linearly warmed up over the ﬁrst 2K steps. Action DiT and
ValueDiT are jointly trained for 24K steps using AdamW with a learning rate of 10−4 and a global
batch size of 128 on four NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPUs. Training requires approximately 48 GPU-
hours. During inference, PhysVAM employs asynchronous action-chunk generation, achieving a
latency of 0.322 seconds per chunk on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. For τ in Eq. (3), we set
τ = 16, corresponding to half the action-chunk length, to provide gradual temporal variation in the
value targets while retaining sensitivity to the distance from each temporal anchor.
Inspired by prior work [29, 32], we condition ActionDiT on horizon-speciﬁc value representations.
We extract the hidden states of the value queries from ValueDiT blocks 4, 8, and 12, project them,
and inject them into ActionDiT layers 9, 19, and 27, respectively. At each injection layer, the action
hidden states attend to the corresponding value features through a gated residual update (Eq. (7)).
A layer-speciﬁc gating network takes the action hidden states, pooled value features, and denoising
timestep features as inputs and produces token- and channel-wise gates. The same network predicts
the scale and shift applied to the normalized attention queries.

B.4
Effect of Value Loss Weight
We evaluate the effect of the value loss weight λV. As shown in Fig. 10, PhysVAM achieves the
highest success rate of 58.83% at λV = 0.1, with an average execution time of 8.23 s. Perfor-
mance remains stable for λV ∈{0.1,0.2,0.4}, while smaller or larger weights reduce the success
rate. Finally, we chose λV = 0.1 in all experiments to balance action generation and future-value
learning.
B.5
Detailed Ablation Results
Tables 6 and 7 provide per-object results for the value-guided action generation and motion history
ablations reported in Tables 2 and 3, respectively.
Detailed Value Ablation Analysis. As shown in Table 6, different value designs exhibit con-
siderable performance variations across objects. Although some variants achieve higher success
rates on individual objects, PhysVAM obtains the highest average success rate and shortest execu-
tion time. Notably, multi-horizon future-value learning provides stronger overall performance than
single-value prediction and current-value assessment, highlighting the beneﬁt of explicitly model-
ing future task progress for dynamic manipulation.
Detailed Motion History Analysis. As shown in Table 7, motion representations exhibit varying
effectiveness across object categories. Removing robot motion history reduces the average success
rate from 58.83% to 45.17%, with particularly pronounced degradation on the potato, lemon, and
can. Meanwhile, object position and velocity histories provide complementary information, with
their combination achieving the highest average success rate. Notably, PhysVAM achieves the best
or tied-best results on eight of the ten object scenes, demonstrating the beneﬁt of jointly modeling
object and robot motion for dynamic manipulation.
