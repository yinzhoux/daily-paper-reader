Abstract
Hierarchical reinforcement learning enables se-
quential tasks to be represented as composi-
tions of separately trained skills.
This pa-
per evaluates demonstration-free hierarchical
pipelines for MiniGrid navigation and robotic
manipulation,
comparing their training re-
quirements and task performance with flat re-
inforcement learning baselines.
The evalua-
tion concerns complete pipelines with task-
specific observation, reward and control struc-
tures. In navigation, two parameterised skills
are reused across unseen FourRooms layouts
under manually specified high-level plans. In
manipulation, reaching, grasp alignment and
lifting are trained separately, with downstream
skills initialised from states collected during
predecessor rollouts.
Across unseen naviga-
tion layouts, the hierarchical pipeline sub-
stantially outperforms the evaluated flat DQN
baselines while requiring fewer training in-
teractions.
In manipulation, the hierarchi-
cal chain requires fewer training steps than
flat SAC and exhibits lower observed varia-
tion across seeds.
The frozen Pick and Lift
skills are further reused for stacking by train-
ing an additional Place skill. Code and videos
are available at https://madeleinenouri.
github.io/acra-skill-composition/.
Keywords: Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning, Skill
Composition, Robotic Manipulation

Introduction
Tasks requiring an extended sequence of decisions can be
decomposed into a sequence of primitive behaviours. Ex-
amples include transitions between rooms in navigation
and sequences of reaching, grasping, lifting, transporting
and placing in manipulation. Flat reinforcement learn-
ing treats the full sequence as a single control problem,
which can lead to poor sample efficiency when explo-
ration must discover a long chain of behaviours from a
sparse terminal reward [Hutsebaut-Buysse et al., 2022].
Hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) offers a
structured alternative in which the task is decomposed
into reusable skills, each operating over a shorter horizon
with a localised reward signal, and a high-level controller
that sequences the skills at execution time [Sutton et al.,
1999; Bacon et al., 2016]. Some skill-chaining methods
improve composition reliability by aligning upstream ter-
mination distributions with downstream initiation distri-
butions using expert demonstrations as a reference [Lee
et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024]. Another approach uses
engineered rewards instead of demonstrations [Kim et
al., 2023; Kwon et al., 2024]. Trained skills can also be
reused as components of new tasks [Hutsebaut-Buysse
et al., 2022]. This paper evaluates demonstration-free
skill pipelines in navigation and manipulation, examin-
ing training requirements, task performance and reuse of
the trained policies.
The contributions of this paper are:
1. An empirical evaluation of demonstration-free hier-
archical pipelines for discrete navigation and contin-
uous manipulation, in which each downstream skill
is trained on reset states covering its predecessor’s
success states.
2. A comparison of pipeline performance and training
interactions with the chosen flat baselines, counting
cumulative training steps across all skills.
3. An evaluation of skill reuse on previously unseen
navigation layouts without retraining, and exten-
sion of manipulation to a stacking task through an
additional Place skill.

Related Work
2.1
Skill Chaining and Transition
Alignment
Temporal abstraction is commonly formalised through
the options framework [Sutton et al., 1999], in which
each option comprises an initiation set,
an intra-
option policy, and a termination condition.
Option-
Critic [Bacon et al., 2016] learns such options end-to-
end. Konidaris and Barto [Konidaris and Barto, 2009]
introduced skill chaining, training each new skill to reach
the initiation region of the next, and a later extension
learns neural initiation classifiers to select among op-
tions [Bagaria and Konidaris, 2020].
These methods
identify feasible handoff states but do not constrain the
termination distribution of upstream skills.
When independently trained skills fail to compose,
a line of work explicitly aligns their interface.
Lee et
al. [Lee et al., 2019] learn a transition policy that bridges
adjacent skills, Byun and Perrault [Byun and Perrault,
2022] cast this as distribution matching between up-
stream terminations and downstream initiations, and
Chen et al. [Chen et al., 2023] chain dexterous manipu-
lation policies via learned transition controllers. Policy
sequencing [Clegg et al., 2018] trains each new skill from
terminal states produced by the preceding skill. Adver-
sarial methods instead regularise the upstream termi-
nation distribution directly: T-STAR [Lee et al., 2021]
uses a GAN-style discriminator targeting the down-
stream skill’s initiation distribution, and SCaR [Chen et
al., 2024] extends this with bi-directional regularisation.
These adversarial methods depend on expert demonstra-
tions as the reference distribution for alignment.
2.2
Reward-Engineered Task
Decomposition
The most closely related demonstration-free approaches
replace demonstrations with engineered rewards and in-
dependently trained Soft Actor-Critic [Haarnoja et al.,
2018] subpolicies. Kim et al. [Kim et al., 2023] decom-
pose Robosuite pick-and-place into reaching and grasp-
ing subtasks, encoding part of the contact alignment
specification in the upstream reaching reward and exe-
cuting the grasp through rule-based gripper logic. Kwon
et al. [Kwon et al., 2024] extend the same template to
door opening, block stacking, and nut assembly.
Our manipulation pipeline belongs to this family, with
separate policies for reaching, grasp alignment and lift-
ing.
In Kim et al.’s pick-and-place and Kwon et al.’s
block-stacking pipelines, the reaching reward encourages
poses suitable for scripted grasping. Our pipeline instead
trains grasp alignment as a separate policy and separates
lifting from placement, while retaining scripted closure
and initial lifting. This decomposition allows us to eval-
uate extension to stacking with the existing Pick and
Lift policies frozen and only Place newly trained.

Method
This section describes the skill decomposition and execu-
tion procedure. The details specific to each environment,
including state and action spaces, reward functions and
success criteria, are provided in Section 4.
3.1
Problem Formulation
A task is a Markov decision process (MDP) M =
(S, A, P, r, γ) with states S, actions A, transition ker-
nel P(s′ | s, a), reward r, and discount γ ∈[0, 1). A
flat agent learns a single policy π : S →A maximising
Eπ[P
t γtr(st, at)].
We represent task execution as a sequence of K skill
invocations, τ1 →· · · →τK.
The sequence may in-
voke the same parameterised policy multiple times with
different task parameters. Following the options frame-
work [Sutton et al., 1999], each skill is a triple τi =
⟨Ii, πi, βi⟩, where Ii ⊆S is the initiation set (the states
from which τi begins), πi is the intra-skill policy (which
may use a skill-specific observation and action space),
and the termination condition is the indicator of a suc-
cess set Gi ⊆S, βi(s) = 1[s ∈Gi]. A high-level con-
troller h selects which skill is active. Here h follows a
prescribed sequence of skill invocations. Navigation uses
a manually specified plan for each layout, while Pick and
Lift uses a fixed reaching, grasp alignment and lifting se-
quence. In general, h may be a learned policy over skills
or a symbolic planner.
A skill may be parameterised, πi(a | s, g), where g
is a task parameter such as a target location or direc-
tion, so that a single policy represents multiple related
behaviours. We use parameterised skills in the discrete
domain and unparameterised skills in the continuous do-
main.
3.2
Skill Training and Execution
Reliable execution requires each skill to terminate in
states from which its successor can begin. Following pol-
icy sequencing [Clegg et al., 2018], downstream skills are
trained from reset states chosen to cover successful pre-
decessor handoffs. Reset distributions are specified from
task geometry or defined by uniform sampling of suc-
cessful predecessor terminal simulator states.
We assess composition performance through condi-
tional in-chain success rates and comparisons with stan-
dalone skill performance.
At test time, the high-level controller h executes τ1
from s0 ∼d0. A monitor evaluates the active skill’s ter-
mination condition, and when βi(s) = 1 control transfers
to τi+1. The chain succeeds if τK reaches GK.

Results.
Both subpolicies converge within ∼50,000 steps in re-
turn and episode length (Fig. 2(a,b)), so skill training
costs ∼100,000 steps in total, whereas the flat baseline,
at its full 500,000-step budget, reaches only 0.020 on pre-

outs), more than an order of magnitude above the 0.020

baseline succeeded only when the goal was in the initial
room.
To test the effect of doorway coordinates, we trained
a vanilla flat DQN (5-dimensional observation, no door
features) under the identical protocol and compared it to
the flat DQN with doorway coordinates across 20 paired

ference 0.010, 95 % CI ±0.026, paired t-test p = 0.43) or
asymptotic training return (p = 0.99).

Hierarchical Decomposition and Transfer.
The task is decomposed into three sequential skills, each
trained as an independent SAC policy with its own re-
ward, action filter, and termination condition:
• Reach: Move the end-effector toward the cube un-
til the gripper–cube distance d falls below dthr =
0.04 m. The action filter disables rotation and locks
the gripper open.
• Grasp: Align the gripper with the cube and estab-
lish contact between both finger pads and the cube.
Alignment is measured by two cosine terms, ca be-
tween the gripper approach axis and the direction to
the cube, and cf between the finger closing axis and
the nearest cube face normal.
Upon meeting the
alignment trigger (d < 0.025 m, ca > 0.5, cf > 0.7),
a rule-based close-and-lift sequence executes. Suc-
cess requires both finger pads in contact with the
cube and a confirmed lift of > 5 mm.
• Lift: Raise the grasped cube to a target height, with
a curriculum advancing through 3, 5, and 10 cm.
The action filter holds the gripper closed.
Skills are trained sequentially using Algorithm 1 on full
MuJoCo simulator states.
Each skill is evaluated every 5,000 steps over M = 30
deterministic episodes. Training stops once its evalua-
tion success rate reaches η = 0.95 on three consecutive


evaluations.
The reported per-skill step counts corre-
spond to these stopping points. Pools are collected by
running N = 500 deterministic episodes of the trained
policy and keeping the complete simulator state when
the success condition is triggered. Initial states for down-
stream skills are sampled uniformly from the pool.

Training Details.
Both the flat baseline and the hierarchical skills use
SAC [Haarnoja et al., 2018] with two hidden layers of
256 units each, learning rate 10−4, batch size 256, dis-
count factor γ = 0.99, target smoothing τ = 0.002 (0.005
for reach), one gradient step per environment step, and
gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 0.5. Learning
starts after 10,000 transitions. The replay buffer capac-
ity is 100,000 transitions for the skill policies and 300,000
for the flat baseline.
The entropy coefficient is auto-
tuned for reach (default initialisation) and grasp (ini-
tialised at 0.1), and fixed at 0.01 for lift. The flat baseline
uses auto-tuned entropy initialised at 0.1. The episode
horizon is 200 steps.
The grasp policy uses pink ac-
tion noise [Eberhard et al., 2023] with σ = 0.1, sequence
length 200, and spectral exponent 1.0. Observations are
normalised via running statistics, clipped at 20.0 and
synchronised every 1,000 steps between the training and
evaluation environments. Raw actions pass through a
skill-specific filter for scaling, deadzone thresholds, ex-
ponential moving average smoothing, and gripper over-
rides.1
The lift skill advances through target heights of 3, 5,
and 10 cm. The target height increases when the rolling
success rate over the last 100 training episodes reaches
0.80, at which point the replay buffer is flushed to re-
move transitions whose rewards were computed against
the previous height target.
The flat baseline uses the
same three targets with an advancement threshold of
0.60 and keeps its replay buffer across stages. All re-
ported lift and full-chain results are at the 10 cm target.
The flat baseline is a single SAC agent trained end-
to-end with rflat over the full seven-dimensional action
space and the same sequence of target heights, for up to
1,000,000 steps. The flat baseline’s stopping criterion is
assessed at the final 10 cm target. The hierarchical skills
are trained sequentially with the reset pools described
above (per-skill training steps in Table 2). Fifteen seeds
are run per method. The full chain success is evaluated
on 30 fixed, unseen placements sampled from the train-
ing distribution (450 rollouts total), and per-skill success
every 5,000 steps over 30 rollouts.

Skill reuse in stacking.
We extend Pick and Lift to stacking by training an ad-
ditional Place skill for each of the 15 policy seeds. The
Reach, Grasp and Lift policies and their observation nor-
malisation statistics remain frozen.
We use a modified Robosuite Stack environment. The
upstream policies retain their original observations of
cube A, without observing cube B. Cube A is sampled
within the Lift training range of ±3 cm and cube B
within ±8 cm, subject to a minimum separation con-
straint. Cube A has a fixed half-width of 2 cm, compared
with 2.0–2.2 cm during Lift training.
Place is trained using SAC from successful Lift states
collected in Stack. Its 22-dimensional observation con-
tains gripper and object states, relative displacements
and a release flag. It controls three translational actions
scaled by 0.3, with rotation disabled and the gripper
closed until release.

5
Discussion
The navigation results demonstrate reuse of parame-
terised policies across previously unseen layouts without
additional training, with higher success and fewer train-
ing steps than the evaluated flat DQN baselines. This
transfer uses a manually specified high-level plan for each
layout. The flat baseline succeeded only when the goal
was located in the initial room, indicating limited per-
formance on tasks requiring inter-room traversal. The
addition of doorway coordinates yielded no statistically
significant improvement under the evaluated protocol,
suggesting that access to these coordinates alone was in-
sufficient to overcome this limitation within the allocated
training budget.
In manipulation, the hierarchical chain required fewer
training steps than flat SAC and exhibited lower ob-
served variability in final success across seeds, whereas
flat SAC achieved a higher mean final success rate.
Grasping accounted for the majority of chain failures.
Conditional in-chain success rates were close to the cor-
responding standalone skill success rates, consistent with
limited additional performance loss during composition.
The stacking extension achieved 0.716 success by com-
bining the frozen Pick and Lift policies with an addition-
ally trained Place skill. Failures were distributed almost
equally between the upstream chain and placement.
6
Conclusion
This paper evaluated demonstration-free hierarchical
pipelines for simulated navigation and manipulation. In
navigation, two parameterised skills achieved a success
rate of 0.555 on previously unseen FourRooms layouts
following approximately 100,000 training steps, com-
pared with 0.020 for the flat DQN with doorway coor-
dinates after 500,000 steps. In manipulation, the chain
achieved 0.858 success following approximately 195,000
cumulative training steps, compared with 0.914 for flat
SAC, with approximately 292,000 training steps aver-
aged over the 14 of 15 seeds that reached the success
criterion. Reusing the frozen Pick and Lift policies with
an additionally trained Place skill achieved 0.716 stack-
ing success.
Several directions remain open. In navigation, these
include automated high-level planning, transfer beyond


the FourRooms environment and evaluating a flat DQN
with the same prescribed subgoals and intermediate re-
wards as the hierarchical pipeline. In manipulation, fu-
ture work can include improving skill reliability and in-
vestigating flat SAC’s higher mean success through ad-
ditional grasp-specific rewards and ablations of control
constraints and training procedures. Learning skill selec-
tion, handoff conditions, termination criteria and retry
decisions from interaction can reduce reliance on manu-
ally specified execution components. Further studies can
explore reuse with different predecessors and execution
orders through retraining or learned transition policies.
Additional comparisons can include reward-engineered
and transition-policy methods, alongside execution-time
evaluation under matched conditions. A further direc-
tion is automatic expansion of the skill set, in which a
planner composes existing skills and triggers the training
of additional skills to support new transitions.
