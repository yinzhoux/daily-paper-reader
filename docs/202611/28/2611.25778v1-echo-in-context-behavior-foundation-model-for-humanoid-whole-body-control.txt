Humanoid behavior foundation models (BFMs) acquire diverse skills through large-scale motion
imitation and massively parallel reinforcement learning, yet broad behavioral capability does not
ensure precise execution across physical conditions. Without distinguishing the underlying dynamics,
policies may favor conservative compromises over environment-specific precision. We introduce ECHO,
an in-context BFM that turns interaction experience into environment-specific control, separating
general behavioral capability from dynamics-specific execution. Mixed and Annealed Sampling first
establishes a behavior prior in a single pretraining stage by balancing targeted practice with broad
motion coverage. Building on this prior, Context Representation and Residual Adaptation learns
structured dynamics representations from interaction histories to guide residual corrections to the
frozen prior, enabling adaptation without test-time parameter updates. Extensive experiments show
that ECHO outperforms all baseline methods across diverse motions and physical conditions. Moreover,
deployment on the Unitree G1 demonstrates whole-body teleoperation spanning extreme acrobatics
and terrain interaction, alongside payload adaptation in manipulation and whole-body control. Page

Introduction
For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them.
Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics
Humanoid robots are expected to execute diverse whole-body behaviors accurately, responsively, and robustly
in human-centric environments. Large-scale motion imitation and massively parallel reinforcement learning
(RL) have advanced this goal through behavior foundation models (BFMs) (Luo et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2025;
Qi et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026; RoboParty Lab Team, 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Cheng et al., 2026; Ma et al.,
2026; Li et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026b). Yet a broad behavior repertoire alone does not
ensure precise execution across physical conditions: the same reference motion may require different control
responses as payload, friction, or actuator dynamics vary. Without adequately distinguishing these dynamics,
a policy trained across environments may learn conservative compromises that favor broad reliability over
environment-specific tracking precision (Mehta et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2026b). The key challenge is therefore
to enable a BFM to understand the underlying dynamics across environments and adapt to different physical
conditions.
Recent work in robotic manipulation has demonstrated in-context adaptation through human demonstra-
tions (Shah et al., 2025) or retrieved interaction experience (Chen et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2025a), without
updating the model weights at test time. These advances motivate equipping humanoid BFMs with the ability

to learn environmental dynamics from interaction context and tailor whole-body actions accordingly. As a
BFM executes diverse behaviors, its actions and the resulting state transitions provide evidence about how
the underlying dynamics shape its response. By encoding this evidence into structured dynamics represen-
tations, the model can learn control-relevant properties of the current environment and use them to guide
subsequent actions. Interaction histories thus serve not merely as records of past execution, but as context for
environment-specific action corrections. This allows a shared behavior prior to pursue the same objective with
control responses tailored to different physical conditions, adapting its execution without test-time parameter
updates.
To this end, we introduce ECHO, an in-context behavior foundation model for humanoid whole-body control
that separates general behavioral capability from dynamics-specific execution. Specifically, we first train
a motion-tracking prior, ECHO-PRE, using Mixed and Annealed Sampling (MIAS). By balancing targeted
practice with broad motion coverage, MIAS enables a single policy to jointly learn everyday motions, highly
dynamic skills (Wang et al., 2026c), contact-rich floor behaviors, and terrain motions within one pretraining
stage (Table 1). we introduce Context Representation and Residual Adaptation (CRA), which learns structured
representations of the underlying environmental dynamics from the prior’s interaction histories. CRA combines
multi-step dynamics prediction with physical neighborhood soft alignment to capture control-relevant physical
information across behaviors. A context-conditioned residual policy then uses these representations to generate
action corrections tailored to the learned dynamics, adapting the frozen prior’s execution.
We evaluate ECHO from four fundamental perspectives: (1) tracking fidelity, covering challenging high-
dynamic skills (Figure 1 (a)) and terrain motion (Figure 1 (b)); (2) in-context adaptation, assessing the benefit
of interaction context under nominal and heavily randomized dynamics; (3) representation quality, examining
whether representations distinguish physical environments across motions; and (4) real-world applicability,
demonstrating whole-body teleoperation and payload adaptation on the Unitree G1 (Figure 1 (c)). Extensive
experimental results show that MIAS strengthens the general behavior prior, while CRA learns physically
informative context that supports more precise execution across varying dynamics without test-time parameter
updates.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce ECHO, an in-context behavior foundation model that separates general behavioral compe-
tence from environment-specific execution, enabling a single whole-body behavior prior to retain broad
capabilities while adapting to varying physical dynamics.
• We develop two complementary components: Mixed and Annealed Sampling (MIAS) for acquiring a broad
whole-body repertoire in a single pretraining stage, and Context Representation and Residual Adaptation
(CRA) for turning interaction histories into physically grounded context that guides dynamics-specific
corrections to the frozen prior.
• Extensive experiments show that ECHO improves tracking under substantial dynamics variation, learns
context representations that remain physically informative across motions, and transfers to real-world
whole-body teleoperation and payload adaptation.

Related Work
Behavior Foundation Models.
Behavior foundation models learn reusable whole-body control priors.
BFM Zeng et al. (2025) supports diverse control interfaces through generative behavioral representations,
while BFM-Zero Li et al. (2026) uses unsupervised RL to unify motion-, goal-, and reward-conditioned control
in a shared latent space. SONIC Luo et al. (2026), Scaling BFM Zeng et al. (2026), Humanoid-GPT Qi
et al. (2026), and HoloMotion-1 Chen et al. (2026b) explore complementary data, model, and training scaling
strategies to improve tracking and generalization, while Mimiclite RoboParty Lab Team (2026) emphasizes
efficient generalized motion tracking. Beyond tracking, related systems support reactive motion planning (Chen
et al., 2026c), terrain-aware execution (Wang et al., 2026e), and environmental interaction (Cheng et al., 2026).
HEFT Liu et al. (2026a) and Extreme-RGMT Ma et al. (2026) further extend capabilities to heavy-payload
teleoperation and highly dynamic skills, respectively. Our ECHO complements these advances by combining
broad skill acquisition via MIAS with dynamics-specific residual adaptation through CRA.
In-Context Learning and Dynamics Adaptation.
Algorithm Distillation Laskin et al. (2022) and Prompt-
DT Xu et al. (2022) demonstrate adaptation through learning histories and demonstration prompts, respectively,
without test-time parameter updates. In robotics, earlier work combines universal policies with online system
identification to adapt control to unknown dynamics (Yu et al., 2017). RMA Kumar et al. (2021) and
dual-history policies Li et al. (2025) exploit proprioceptive histories for dynamics adaptation, while DWL Gu
et al. (2024) uses denoising reconstruction for implicit system identification. LocoFormer Liu et al. (2025)
leverages cross-trial Transformer memory to adapt locomotion to unseen morphologies and dynamics. For
humanoid control, Any2Track Zhang et al. (2025b) combines world-model-based dynamics representations
with layer-wise adapters in a frozen tracker, while HoRD Wang et al. (2026a) integrates history-conditioned
RL with online distillation. FADA Xie et al. (2026) separates planning from inverse dynamics and adapts
the latter using target-domain rollouts. In contrast, ECHO combines multi-step dynamics prediction with
cross-motion physical neighborhood soft alignment to learn structured context representations, then trains an
RL-based residual policy (Johannink et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2026d) conditioned on the learned in-context
representation with both the encoder and behavior prior frozen.

Method
As illustrated in Figure 2, we first formulate In-Context BFM under unobserved environmental dynamics
(Sec. 3.1). We then introduce MIAS to pretrain ECHO-PRE, balancing broad motion coverage with targeted
practice on difficult segments (Sec. 3.2). Building on this frozen prior, CRA enables effective in-context
adaptation by inferring environmental dynamics from interaction history and translating the resulting context
into action corrections tailored to the current environment (Sec. 3.3).

3.2
Learning a General Behavior Prior via MIAS
In-context adaptation across diverse whole-body behaviors requires a sufficiently general behavior prior. We
first train the behavior prior πθ, termed ECHO-PRE, in a single stage with MIAS, using motion tracking
as the proxy task. MIAS combines failure rewind, uniform exploration, and failure-adaptive sampling to
balance broad motion coverage with targeted practice on difficult segments, and gradually reduces sampling
concentration to consolidate the learned repertoire.
Adaptive weight.
We partition reference motions into temporal bins and estimate their difficulty using
empirical failure rates fi (Luo et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2026). We prioritize bins with observed failures through
a length-weighted, temperature-scaled distribution:
epi ∝ℓi exp(fi/T) 1{fi > 0},
(5)
where ℓi is the number of valid frames in bin i and T > 0 controls sampling sharpness. To prevent excessive
concentration on individual segments or motions, we cap and redistribute probability mass at both levels,
yielding the adaptive distribution pA. Counting rules, the no-failure fallback, and probability caps are detailed
in Appendix A.
Mixed and annealed sampling.
Difficult segments warrant targeted practice without indefinitely restricting
motion coverage. MIAS therefore mixes three sampling branches and progressively relaxes the concentration
of the adaptive branch.

In-Context Adaptation via CRA
CRA first learns a context encoder from the prior’s rollouts using dynamics prediction and physically grounded
representation objectives, then freezes the context encoder and trains a residual policy to translate context
into action corrections.
Interaction context.
Let sp
t denote noisy proprioception and at the applied action. At decision time t, the
context Ht contains only completed, valid transitions preceding that decision. We instantiate the context
with short- and long-term histories:
Ht = (St, Mt),
zt = Eφ(Ht),
(8)
The short-term history St preserves fine-grained recent responses, while the long-term memory Mt provides
complementary dynamics evidence from earlier interactions across motions. Complete chunks are retained in
Mt across reference-motion changes and episode resets within the same physical session, whereas St is cleared
at these boundaries. The physical context remains fixed within a session, and a new session clears all memory.

Experiments
4.1
Set up
We use the Unitree G1 for both training and evaluation. We evaluate ECHO extensively in simulation,
including tracking fidelity (Section 4.2), in-context adaptation (Section 4.3), and in-context representation
analysis (Section 4.4). We further validate ECHO on the real robot in Section 4.5.
To enable fair comparison with existing BFMs while evaluating in-context ability under broader dynamics
variations, we design two physical randomization settings. Necessary Domain Randomization (NDR) contains the
minimum set of DR required for sim-to-real transfer and is designed to remain close to the settings commonly
adopted by existing baselines. We pretrain ECHO-PRE only under NDR to acquire the general behavior
prior.
We further introduce Heavy Domain Randomization (HDR), which extends NDR with substantially larger
dynamics variations, including additional payloads on the wrists and lower legs. Starting from the same frozen
ECHO-PRE backbone, we train CRA under NDR and HDR, resulting in ECHO-N and ECHO-H, respectively.
The detailed randomization ranges of Nominal environment, NDR and HDR are summarized in Table 7.
We train ECHO using PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) in Mjlab (Zakka et al., 2026) for behavior pretraining and
in-context adaptation, leveraging more than 1,200 hours of filtered high-quality motion data. The composition
of the training dataset is illustrated in Figure 5.
4.2
Evaluation of Motion Tracking Fidelity
We evaluate the contributions of MIAS and CRA to motion tracking. To assess MIAS, we compare ECHO-PRE
with HEFT (Liu et al., 2026a), SONIC v1.1 (Luo et al., 2026), and ECHO-PRE w/o MIAS, which uses uniform
motion sampling. To assess CRA, we compare ECHO-N against its pretrained ECHO-PRE backbone. Our
evaluation covers two complementary benchmarks: AMASS-2000-Random tests generalization to everyday
motions from AMASS, which is excluded from ECHO’s training data, while BONES-SEED-100-HighDynamic
tests challenging motions within the training distribution. All evaluations use nominal dynamics without
domain randomization. We report success rate (SR) and motion-tracking errors, with dataset construction
and metric definitions detailed in Appendix C.
As shown in Table 2, MIAS improves tracking on both benchmarks relative to uniform sampling, and ECHO-
PRE compares favorably with the external baselines. CRA further reduces tracking errors, showing that
interaction context improves execution precision even under nominal dynamics.

4.3
Evaluating the ability of using context
In this section, we compare ECHO with representative adaptation methods for legged robot control. We
consider three baselines. RMA (Kumar et al., 2021) learns a privileged teacher conditioned on ground-truth
dynamics parameters and subsequently distills its adaptation capability into a student policy; we report both
the RMA-Teacher and RMA-Student for comparison. Any2Track Zhang et al. (2025b) learns a latent dynamics
representation from interaction history and regularizes the representation with a world-model prediction
objective. We further design a Vanilla baseline, which uses the same residual-policy architecture but receives
only the observations available to ECHO-PRE, without any latent dynamics representation.
For a fair comparison, all methods share the same frozen ECHO-PRE backbone and residual-policy architecture,
and are trained under the same HDR setting. We evaluate all methods on AMASS-2000-Random under both
the nominal environment and the HDR environment. The evaluation metrics are identical to those used in
Section 4.2.
As shown in Table 3, ECHO-H achieves performance comparable to RMA-Teacher in the nominal environment,
without requiring privileged dynamics parameters at inference. Under HDR, ECHO-H achieves the best results
on four of the five metrics, improving SR by 0.95 percentage points and reducing global MPJPE by 3.0%
relative to RMA-Teacher. ECHO-H also outperforms RMA-Student across all HDR metrics and consistently
improves over Vanilla in both environments. In contrast, the Any2Track baseline provides no consistent
gains over Vanilla, suggesting that introducing a history-derived latent alone does not guarantee effective
adaptation in this setting. Together, these results suggest that ECHO learns control-relevant representations of
environmental dynamics that the residual policy can effectively exploit to improve tracking under substantial
dynamics variation.
4.4
Analyzing the learned context representations
We examine whether CRA learns environment representations that remain consistent across motions while
retaining physically meaningful information. We collect trajectories by rolling out ECHO-PRE across 40
LAFAN motions in 1,024 HDR environments and evaluate all variants on 325,193 shared valid history windows.
We evaluate environment identification, cross-motion retrieval, separation, and linear readout of payload mass
and friction. See Appendix D for details.
As reported in Table 4, ECHO achieves 97.478% mean environment identification accuracy and 93.872% mean
cross-motion retrieval mAP, with mean linear-readout R2 scores of 0.8357 for payload mass and 0.9208 for
friction. These results indicate that the learned representations capture environment information consistently
across motions while keeping physical properties linearly accessible.

mass R2 from 0.8357 to 0.4777 and friction R2 from 0.9208 to −0.0132. This contrast highlights the predictor’s
contribution to making physical properties linearly accessible, beyond merely distinguishing environments.
Scaling interaction context.
Our context encoder supports longer interaction histories by aggregating addi-
tional long-term chunks into a fixed-size memory representation. We fix the short-term history at 1 second
(50 steps) and compare a short-only baseline with total context budgets of 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 9 seconds. Each
variant is trained separately for 5,000 updates using the prediction loss and soft-alignment loss. Evaluation
uses shared, unaveraged history windows from 40 LAFAN (Harvey et al., 2020) motions, and we report means
over three evaluations. Figure 4 shows consistent improvements with longer context: from 50 to 450 steps
(1 to 9 seconds), mean linear environment identification accuracy increases from 91.967% to 98.403%, and
cross-motion retrieval mAP from 75.778% to 96.165%. Payload mass R2 increases from 0.5037 to 0.8618,
showing improved linear readability of physical properties. These results demonstrate that our method can
scale its interaction context to learn more informative environment representations with better cross-motion
consistency. Complete results and protocol details are provided in Appendix D (Table 8).
4.5
Real-world experiments
We demonstrate ECHO’s diverse whole-body behaviors through real-time teleoperation, including crawling,
highly dynamic motions, and terrain traversal (Figure 1, Figure 6, and the accompanying video). As shown in
Figure 7, in-context adaptation enables ECHO-H to learn context-dependent control under HDR and maintain
precise tracking with added payloads. Even with 10 kg of payloads distributed across its hands and legs (three
2 kg loads and one 4 kg load), ECHO-H can still stand on one leg, squat, and bend forward. For quantitative
evaluation, we execute four reference motions and compute MPJPE-L from joint positions recorded during
deployment. We compare ECHO-N with ECHO-PRE, HEFT, and SONIC v1.1 without additional payloads,
and ECHO-H with Vanilla and RMA-Student under a 2 kg load on each lower leg. As reported in Table 9,

ECHO-N and ECHO-H achieve the lowest mean tracking errors across all four motions in their respective

5
Conclusion
We presented ECHO, an in-context behavior foundation model that separates general behavioral capability
from dynamics-specific execution. MIAS acquires a broad whole-body repertoire, while CRA learns physical
dynamics from interaction context to guide residual corrections to the frozen behavior prior. Experiments
and real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 demonstrate improved tracking, whole-body teleoperation, and
adaptation to added payloads. These results show that interaction context enables a shared behavior prior to
adapt to its physical environment, combining behavioral breadth with dynamics-specific precision without
test-time parameter updates.

D
Context Representation Evaluation
Sampling and splits.
Each environment–motion pair contributes up to eight independent sessions, with
one latent extracted after 500 warm-up steps. ECHO variants are evaluated after 5,000 training iterations;
the default encoder produces a 64-dimensional context from at most 350 completed interactions. Following
grouped evaluation (Roberts et al., 2017), we split motions into five folds, keeping each motion’s windows
together. For classification, three folds fit the probe and one selects hyperparameters; the probe is then
refitted on all four non-test folds. Retrieval uses these four folds as the gallery. Physical readout additionally
splits environments into five folds, yielding 25 tests that exclude both test environments and test motions from
probe fitting and validation. Preprocessing uses training data only. LAFAN motions belong to the encoder’s
pretraining corpus.
Metrics.
We report frozen-encoder linear classification accuracy (Chen et al., 2020), full-gallery mAP (Man-
ning et al., 2008), and the Silhouette score (Rousseeuw, 1987) using environment identities. Retrieval treats
windows from the same environment as positives; both retrieval and Silhouette use Euclidean distances
between original latent vectors without additional normalization. Ridge probes (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970)
predict payload mass and friction. Probe scores pool out-of-fold predictions across valid windows; mass R2
averages the four limb-wise scores, while friction R2 evaluates a single coefficient.
Visualization.
Both panels use the same windows and t-SNE settings: perplexity 5, PCA initialization and
1,500 iterations. The Any2Track comparison is qualitative because inputs, capacity, history length, and
training setup differ. To further evaluate out-of-distribution generalization, we visualize the learned context
representations on the AMASS dataset using t-SNE.

Real world experiments
Broad capability versus contextual adaptation.
Figure 6 demonstrates that ECHO-PRE supports a broad
repertoire of whole-body behaviors with a single pretrained policy, ranging from ground and terrain inter-
actions to highly dynamic aerial motions. The robot seamlessly handles substantial variations in posture,
support conditions, and motion dynamics, including floor-level transitions, platform traversal, and aerial flips,
highlighting the breadth and reusability of the learned behavioral capabilities.
Figure 7 further demonstrates that ECHO can adapt these capabilities to changing physical contexts through
in-context interaction. Under additional wrist and limb payloads, the robot maintains precise manipulation
and diverse whole-body behaviors, including balance, squatting, sitting, and posture transitions. Crucially,
when the payload is attached or removed, ECHO adjusts its control response based on the resulting interaction
history without updating model parameters at test time. This shows that ECHO complements broad pretrained
capabilities with context-dependent dynamics adaptation, enabling precise behavior under previously unseen
physical conditions.
