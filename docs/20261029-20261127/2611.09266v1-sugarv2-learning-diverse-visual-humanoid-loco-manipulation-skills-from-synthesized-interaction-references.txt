Abstract
Scaling up visual humanoid loco-manipulation skill ac-
quisition requires reducing the human effort needed as
tasks and spatial configurations expand, particularly in
demonstration collection and task-specific training de-
sign.
We present SUGARv2, a unified framework for
learning diverse visual humanoid loco-manipulation skills
through interaction reference synthesis and a shared
training pipeline.
To obtain diverse references at low
cost, we adapt reusable task templates to different spa-
tial configurations, synthesizing coordinated robot and
object motions.
To reduce task-specific reward de-
sign and training effort, we use reference-based imita-
tion as a common learning objective across tasks.
We
first train privileged teachers to execute these refer-
ences, then train visual policies through teacher distilla-
tion and reinforcement learning with the same reference-
based mimic rewards, using diverse object instances and
visual domain randomization to support visual general-
ization and sim-to-real transfer.
Across five humanoid
loco-manipulation tasks, SUGARv2 achieves a mean suc-
cess rate of 91.0% in simulation. Deployment on a real
humanoid robot further demonstrates successful sim-to-
real transfer for whole-body manipulation. These results
demonstrate the framework’s potential for learning diverse
visual humanoid loco-manipulation skills. Project Page:

1. Introduction
Scaling up visual humanoid loco-manipulation skill acqui-
sition requires reducing the human effort needed for each
new task and spatial configuration. Existing approaches rely
on manually collected interaction data or task-specific train-
ing designs, making it costly to extend skill learning across
tasks and spatial configurations. HumanoidMimicGen [23]
expands teleoperated demonstrations through whole-body
planning, but depends on initial demonstrations and uses de-
coupled upper- and lower-body control. GRAIL [44] avoids
teleoperation and supports whole-body control, but requires
video generation and motion reconstruction for every spa-
tial configuration, incurring high costs and potentially pro-
ducing physically implausible interactions.
Task-specific
policy learning methods [9, 33, 50] acquire visual loco-
manipulation skills through tailored training designs, re-
quiring further engineering for new tasks. These dependen-
cies make scaling skill acquisition costly in data preparation
and training design (Table 1). Motivated by this, we present
SUGARv2, a unified framework for learning diverse visual
humanoid loco-manipulation skills through interaction ref-
erence synthesis and a shared training pipeline.
For low-cost reference synthesis, we use GPT-6 As-
tra [29] to generate reusable task-level interaction tem-
plates. These templates specify ordered sequences of key
whole-body poses, robot–object and hand–object spatial re-
lations, and intended contact labels. We adapt them to dif-
ferent spatial configurations of the robot, object, and goal to
synthesize robot and object trajectories for policy learning.
To reduce task-specific reward design and training ef-
fort, we use reference-based imitation as a common learn-
ing objective across tasks. We first train privileged teachers
to execute these references under physical dynamics, then
train visual policies through teacher distillation and rein-
forcement learning with the same reference-based mimic
rewards. Teacher supervision is retained during reinforce-
ment learning to guide policy optimization at states visited
by the visual policy. Diverse object instances and visual
domain randomization further support visual generalization
and sim-to-real transfer.
Across five humanoid loco-manipulation tasks, SUG-
ARv2 achieves a mean success rate of 91.0%, outperform-
ing the strongest baseline by 51.4 percentage points. De-
ployed on a real humanoid robot, the visual policies suc-
cessfully perform whole-body manipulation tasks, demon-
strating the framework’s potential for learning diverse real-
world manipulation skills. Our main contributions are:
• We present an interaction reference synthesis pipeline for
humanoid loco-manipulation. By adapting reusable task
templates to different spatial configurations, the pipeline
synthesizes diverse interaction references across tasks, re-
ducing the cost of collecting demonstrations.

2. Related Work
2.1. Synthetic Demonstrations for Policy Learning
Demonstration-based methods synthesize interaction refer-
ences by adapting existing motions [4, 6, 15, 51]. Mim-
icGen [27] adapts object-centric demonstration segments
to new configurations. HumanoidMimicGen [23] extends
demonstration adaptation to humanoids using explicitly an-
notated skills and separate planning for locomotion and sta-
tionary manipulation, which constrains whole-body coordi-
nation. Generative approaches instead synthesize interac-
tion motions from learned priors [3, 13, 16, 19, 20, 41, 47].
GRAIL [44] generates interaction videos conditioned on 3D
assets, reconstructs human and object motion, and retargets
the reconstructed motions to humanoid robots.
Beyond generating diverse motions, interaction refer-
ence synthesis must account for task requirements and
scene geometry.
Prior work uses contact relations
and scene-adapted keyframes to specify interaction con-
straints [1, 21, 54]. Conditional motion models generate
continuous motion from trajectories, joint constraints, or
sparse keyframes [2, 17, 31, 34, 45]. Building on condi-
tional motion generation, SUGARv2 adapts reusable task-
level interaction templates to different spatial configura-
tions, uses the adapted key poses to guide whole-body mo-
tion generation, and constructs coordinated object trajecto-
ries to form interaction references for policy learning.

2.2. Motion Tracking and Interaction Imitation
Motion tracking connects kinematic references to physical
execution [7, 25, 30]. Physics-based character controllers
can infer motion from sparse conditions [37], while hu-
manoid tracking controllers track motion references and
enable whole-body teleoperation [8, 10, 22, 55]. Scaling
motion data and controller pretraining further broadens the
range of motions that can be executed [26, 56].
Object interaction additionally requires coordinating
body motion with object dynamics [24]. The SkillMimic
series [38, 53] learns diverse interaction skills through a
unified imitation formulation and extends skill coverage
from sparse and noisy demonstrations.
InterMimic [48]
uses physics-based teachers to refine imperfect interaction
demonstrations and distills their behavior into a general pol-
icy. Subsequent work extends this direction through gener-
ative control and the augmentation of robot interaction mo-
tions [49, 57]. Reference-based interaction learning also en-
ables humanoid robots to acquire manipulation skills by im-
itating coordinated human and object motions [39, 42, 43].
These works show how reference-based learning can sup-
port interaction skills without a separately designed task
reward for each behavior.
Building on this, SUGARv2
uses synthesized interaction references to define a common
learning objective for visual loco-manipulation across tasks.
2.3. Visual Humanoid Loco-Manipulation
Imitation learning trains visual humanoid manipulation
policies from demonstrations collected through whole-body
teleoperation [5, 8].
Humanoid vision-language-action
models draw on teleoperated demonstrations, human data,
and heterogeneous robot data to learn diverse skills [12, 14,
28, 46]. Synthetic demonstrations also support visual pol-
icy learning by expanding training data without collecting
additional robot demonstrations [4, 18, 40].
Reinforcement learning provides another route to learn-
ing visual loco-manipulation skills through interaction with
the environment. Some approaches train privileged poli-
cies with task-specific rewards and then transfer their be-
havior to visual policies [52]. VIRAL [9] trains visual poli-
cies to imitate privileged teachers through online DAgger
and behavior cloning. Reinforcement learning can further
refine visuomotor behavior [11, 33, 50]. Extending these
approaches to new tasks requires additional reward design
and tuning. SUGARv2 uses synthesized interaction refer-
ences to define a common learning objective across tasks.
A shared reference-based mimic reward guides both priv-
ileged teacher training and visual policy optimization, re-
ducing task-specific reward design effort.

3.1. Problem definition and framework overview
We aim to learn visual humanoid loco-manipulation poli-
cies that coordinate whole-body motion and object manipu-
lation. Using egocentric images and proprioception, a pol-
icy must complete the task across different spatial configu-
rations. We learn a separate policy for each task.

3.2.1. Task template construction
For a given task, motions across spatial configurations typ-
ically share the same interaction sequence and contact re-
quirements. For example, carrying an object requires es-
tablishing a grasp, transporting the object, and reaching the
goal, while the robot’s stance, turns, and path depend on the
object and goal locations. A task template captures these
shared interaction stages, allowing the same task design to
be reused across configurations.
For each task, we use GPT-6 Astra [29] to generate an
interaction template T consisting of an ordered sequence
of template keyframes. Each template keyframe specifies a
whole-body pose, robot–object and hand–object spatial re-
lations, and intended contact labels. The poses in the tem-
plate provide starting points for adaptation, while the spa-
tial relations guide robot positioning and hand placement in
each configuration. The contact labels specify which hands
interact with the object at each template keyframe.
3.2.2. Task template instantiation
We adapt the template keyframes to each sampled spatial
configuration to obtain instantiated keyframes and their tim-
ing. Starting from the object’s initial pose and the goal po-
sition specified by the spatial configuration, we use the tem-
plate’s hand–object relations and the object’s symmetries to
generate candidate hand targets. We then adapt the robot’s
position, heading, and whole-body pose to these targets un-
der reachability and scene constraints. Among feasible can-
didates, we select the keyframe requiring the least move-
ment and pose adjustment. Once the keyframe poses are
instantiated, we determine their timing from the movement
demands between consecutive poses.
Given the instantiated keyframes and their timing, we
use an off-the-shelf motion generation model [34], addition-
ally conditioned on task text, to generate continuous whole-
body motion. For each configuration, we generate multiple
candidate trajectories, filter out those that violate kinematic
or scene constraints, and rank the remaining candidates to
favor smooth motion with less unnecessary movement.
After generating robot motion, we construct the object
trajectory from the motion of the interacting hands and the
template’s hand–object relations. We correct object posi-
tions so that the trajectory passes through the positions spec-
ified at the keyframes. Using the template’s contact labels
and keyframe times, we assign contact intervals to the inter-
acting hands. Together, the robot motion, object trajectory,
and contact labels form a complete interaction reference for
policy learning in the sampled spatial configuration.
3.3. Policy learning
Building on the synthesized interaction references, our pol-
icy learning pipeline uses reference-based imitation as a
common learning objective across tasks, reducing task-
specific reward design effort. We first train privileged teach-
ers to execute these references, then train visual policies
through teacher distillation and reinforcement learning with
the same reference-based mimic reward.

4. Experiments
Through our experiments, we aim to answer the following
questions: (1) How well does SUGARv2 learn visual ma-
nipulation skills under varied spatial configurations and un-
seen backgrounds? (2) How efficiently can we synthesize
interaction references, and how well do they support visual
policy learning? (3) How does the number of synthesized
references affect visual policy performance? (4) How do
key design choices in our learning framework affect policy
performance? (5) Can the learned visual policies transfer to
a real humanoid robot?
4.1. Experimental Setup
Tasks.
We evaluate five humanoid loco-manipulation
tasks covering pickup, pick-and-place, and object transport.
Tabletop Pickup requires grasping an object from a table-
top and holding it. Floor-to-Table and Same-Table involve
picking up an object from the floor or tabletop, respec-
tively, and placing it into a target basket on a table. Box
Carrying and Basket Transport require carrying the corre-
sponding object while walking to a designated target region.
These tasks demand tightly coordinated whole-body con-
trol, requiring continuous coordination between the upper
and lower body throughout object interaction.
Training and evaluation. We train visual policies across
diverse spatial configurations of robot, object, and goal
within prescribed ranges. We use 100 synthesized refer-
ences per task and train separate privileged teachers and
visual policies with the same pipeline. In simulation, we
report full-task success as mean ± standard deviation over
three seeds, using task-specific criteria.
Baselines. For visual policy learning, we compare with VI-
RAL [9] and GR00T N1.7 [28]. VIRAL combines DAg-
ger with behavior cloning.
GR00T is fine-tuned on im-
age–action pairs from teacher rollouts. For interaction ref-
erence synthesis, we compare with GRAIL [44], which re-
constructs motion from generated videos.

Data scale counts kinematic robot–object trajectories with

4.5. Ablation study
We evaluate the key design choices in our visual policy
learning pipeline through ablations under the same train-
ing budget (Table 4). Without teacher distillation, train-
ing the policy from scratch using reference-based reinforce-
ment learning yields near-zero success on all five tasks un-
der this training budget. Without reference-based reinforce-
ment learning, training through teacher distillation alone re-
sults in lower success than the full framework on every task.
Finally, after the same initialization through teacher distilla-
tion, continuing with reference-based reinforcement learn-
ing alone reduces success across all tasks compared with
retaining teacher distillation.
These results support the design of our visual policy
learning pipeline: teacher distillation provides an effective
initialization, while reference-based reinforcement learning
refines task performance with continued teacher supervi-
sion. Together, these components enable effective learning
of visual loco-manipulation skills.

executions. The policies successfully perform all five tasks
on the real robot, demonstrating sim-to-real transfer for co-
ordinated whole-body motion and object manipulation.
Failure analysis. Execution errors can cause missed grasps,
after which the policy may continue subsequent motions
without securing the object. Recovery from these failures
remains limited, as the policy does not consistently retry
the grasp before proceeding.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we presented SUGARv2, a unified framework
for interaction reference synthesis and visual policy learn-
ing across tasks. We adapt reusable task templates to differ-
ent spatial configurations to obtain diverse interaction refer-
ences at low cost. These references support a unified train-
ing pipeline combining teacher distillation and reinforce-
ment learning with shared reference-based mimic rewards,
reducing task-specific reward design effort. Experiments
across five tasks and real-humanoid deployment demon-
strate the framework’s effectiveness, highlighting its poten-
tial for learning diverse real-world manipulation skills.
Limitations and Future Works. Our reference synthesis
currently focuses on short-horizon interactions in simpli-
fied scenes. Extending it to long-horizon tasks and cluttered
environments will require more scalable and automated in-
teraction reference synthesis. Our visual policies are task-
specific; we aim to use synthesized references to support
multitask vision-language-action policies that follow lan-
guage instructions across diverse interaction tasks.
