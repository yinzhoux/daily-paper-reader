# Abstract 

Planning with world models enables test-time adaptation for embodied agents, offering superior generalization over direct policy learning. However, long-horizon tasks remain hindered by cumulative errors and search complexity. Existing hier-archical methods often use rigid macro-actions or latent skills, leading to brittle representations and limited fine-grained control. We introduce ID-WM (Implicit Dynamics World Models) , a hierarchical world model that decouples state-space reachability from local control. Our framework utilizes a high-level model to sample feasible transitions across the state manifold and a low-level world model as an inverse dynamics solver to ground these transitions into precise actions. This allows the planner to identify reachable subgoals without being constrained by low-level execution details. By prioritizing local physical feasibility over global behavioral patterns, ID-WM synthesizes novel long-horizon plans from short segments, transcending the specific behavioral modes seen during training. We demonstrate its effectiveness across three navigation and manipulation suites, achieving a 38.5% improvement over state-of-the-art policy and diffusion-based planners. Crucially, by leveraging this hierarchical structure to mitigate compound-ing errors, our approach yields a 135% performance gain over single-level world model MPC, particularly in complex, unseen configurations for long-horizon tasks. Videos can be viewed at https://id-wm.github.io .

# 1 Introduction 

World models (WM) [1, 2, 3] have emerged as a powerful paradigm for embodied decision-making [4, 5, 6, 7]. By learning predictive models of environment dynamics, they enable test-time reasoning and the synthesis of novel behaviors for unseen goals by searching in the action space [8, 9, 10, 11]. However, planning with WMs faces two fundamental challenges: 1) search complexity grows exponentially with planning horizon, and 2) prediction errors accumulate over long rollouts. Therefore, most WM-based planners are restricted to short-horizon tasks [8, 9], rely on simplified action abstractions [10], or operate in high-level spaces such as language, sacrificing fine-grained control [12]. Hierarchical learning offers a principled solution to this scalability challenge by decomposing long-horizon problems into manageable abstractions [13, 14, 15]. It has been extensively studied in Reinforcement Learning (RL) [16, 17, 18, 19], where hierarchy mitigates the credit assignment problem in long trajectories, and in imitation learning [20, 21], where it enables reusable behaviors. More recently, hierarchical structure has been introduced into diffusion-based planners to avoid directly modeling entire trajectories [22, 23, 24], improving scalability and generalization beyond fixed-horizon training data. How can we enable the same capabilities for task-agnostic world models, enabling long-horizon zero-shot planning for unseen goals? A direct approach would be to simply increase the prediction horizon

However, previous attempts at hierarchical world models typically depend on task-specific priors to function effectively, relying on pre-trained skills and low-level policies [19, 26] or rewards and value functions to verify subgoal feasibility [27, 28]. This dependency arises because planning is fundamentally adversarial: without these anchoring constraints, a powerful optimizer will inevitably exploit deficiencies in a learned world model, discovering "wormholes" or unrealistic transitions [29, 30]. To make hierarchical world models suitable for planning, we must construct a latent space that both condenses trajectory information to minimize search complexity, and remains inherently well-behaved to prevent the planner from exploiting unrealistic transitions. To this end, we introduce ID-WM , a hierarchical world model with implicit dynamics grounded in the insight that effective planning requires decoupling topological reachability from execution details (Figure 1). ID-WM consists of a high-level diffusion-based world model that only implicitly models the dynamics by outputting states that are within a fixed distance away from the current state, functioning as a neighboring state generator , and a deterministic low-level model conditioned on both current state and action, acting as an inverse dynamics solver . Conditioned on the current state and a Gaussian noise, the high-level model leverages the generative power of diffusion to map a tractable unit Gaussian latent space to the manifold of feasible neighboring states. This formulation allows us to treat the noise as an implicit high-level action, enabling the planner to search over a well-behaved distribution instead of complex, discontinuous skill spaces to reason purely about "where to go," while the low-level model recovers the precise executable actions for "how to get there." We summarize our contributions as follows: • We introduce ID-WM , a hierarchical world model that decouples reachability from control, eliminating the need for task-specific value functions or pre-trained policies. • We propose a corresponding hierarchical planning algorithm that bridges high-level abstrac-tion with local dynamics, enabling robust, long-horizon decision-making. • Across three navigation and manipulation environment suites, ID-WM shows 38.5% im-provement for reaching arbitrary goals against powerful hierarchical offline reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and diffusion planning methods, and crucially, a 135% improve-ment over single-level world model and planning regimes. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Hierarchical Learning 

Hierarchical learning has long been a foundational pursuit in artificial intelligence, decomposing long-horizon problems into manageable abstractions [13, 14, 15]. This paradigm spans policy 2architectures [31], reinforcement learning [16, 18, 27, 32], imitation learning [20], and planning [28, 33]. Existing work focuses on both behavioral (skills/options) [14, 19, 26, 34, 35, 36, 37] and 

subgoal-centric approaches [17, 28, 38]. However, these methods typically rely on coupled priors, such as rewards, learned value functions or pre-trained low-level policies [28, 32] to ensure feasibility, limiting generalization beyond the training distribution. In contrast, we eliminate this dependency, enabling robust hierarchical reasoning purely through implicit dynamics even when reliable value functions and policies are absent. 

2.2 World Models for Embodied Planning 

World models [1, 2, 3] have emerged as a foundational paradigm for embodied decision-making, enabling agents to reason about the future consequences of their actions. Recently, the field has shifted towards developing general-purpose world models [5, 8, 10, 39, 40, 41] capable of capturing broad environmental dynamics, diverging from traditional model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) where learning task-specific reward or value functions is paramount [42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. Once these dynamics are learned, inference-time planning algorithms like the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) [49] or gradient descent—are employed to derive behaviors zero-shot [8, 9, 11, 50]. However, the effectiveness of these approaches is often constrained to short horizons due to the computational complexity of search and the accumulation of prediction errors over time. While various strategies have been proposed to mitigate these issues, ranging from robust gradient-based optimization [30, 51] and efficient sampling routines [52] to temporal approximations for longer action sequences [10], they remain fundamentally flat in their temporal reasoning. Consequently, they struggle with long-horizon task solving, remaining susceptible to local minima and limited in their scalability to complex behaviors. Concurrently, HWM [25] builds a hierarchical latent world model by increasing the high-level frameskip and learning macro-actions; in contrast, ID-WM searches over the diffusion noise space, which is smooth and well-conditioned by construction. 

2.3 Diffusion Models and Planning 

Diffusion probabilistic models [53, 54, 55] excel at capturing complex, multimodal distributions via iterative denoising. Their efficacy is proven across high-dimensional modalities, including pixels [40, 41, 56, 57], language [58], and robot actions [59, 60]. Recently, diffusion models have been proposed as zero-shot planners for embodied tasks [61, 62, 63, 64]. These methods model entire trajectories directly, limiting generalization to out-of-distribution initial-goal pairs and constraining planning horizons to those seen during training. While hierarchical formulations mitigate these constraints, they rely on task-specific value functions [22, 23, 32], successful task trajectories [65], or assumed access to low-level primitives [24]. In contrast, we redefine diffusion as a local generator 

rather than a global planner . By delegating long-horizon reasoning to search, our method composes local jumps into novel trajectories without requiring task-specific priors. 

# 3 Hierarchical World Models with Implicit Dynamics 

Overview and Problem Formulation. We consider a goal-conditioned planning problem within a Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by the tuple M = ( S, A, P), where S is the continuous state space, A is the action space, and P(st+1 |s≤t, a ≤t) represents the transition dynamics. Following prior world modeling methods [8, 10, 11], we learn environment dynamics from a task-agnostic offline dataset without any reward labeling via a world model, and plan to reach arbitrary unseen goals at test time. More specifically, an agent is provided with an initial state o0 and a goal state og at test time. The objective is to execute a sequence of actions (a0, a 1, . . . , a T −1) such that the distance between the agent’s final state oT and the goal og is minimized. Unlike prior work, we do not assume a fixed task horizon T [8], focusing instead on enabling the agent to perform autonomous long-horizon reasoning and planning.

# 4 Experiments 

Our experiments are designed to answer the following key questions: • Subgoal Generation: Can ID-WM reliably generate diverse and feasible reachable states? • Generalization: Can ID-WM solve long-horizon planning tasks to reach arbitrary, zero-shot goals at test time? • Scaling: How does the performance of ID-WM scale across environments of increasing complexity? • Visual Planning: Is ID-WM effective for long-horizon planning directly from high-dimensional visual observations?

4.5 How does ID-WM scale with task complexity? 

We investigate whether ID-WM scales to tasks of increasing complexity across two dimensions: environment size and goal horizon. 

Scaling with Maze Size. We evaluate ID-WM across four mazes of increasing difficulty: U-Maze (3 × 3), Medium Maze ( 6 × 6), Large Maze ( 7 × 10 ), and Giant Maze ( 13 × 13 ) (Figure 7a). Each dataset contains 2,000 random 100-step trajectories. Given a fixed dataset size, data coverage naturally becomes sparser as maze size increases. While all methods achieve near-perfect success on the U-Maze, baselines degrade significantly in larger environments (Figure 7a). In contrast, ID-WM maintains the highest performance on the Large Maze. We hypothesize this is because ID-WM combines local dynamics modeling with high-level planning, effectively "stitching" together regions of sparse data. Unlike GCBC or Diffuser (which model entire trajectories simultaneously) or HIQL (which requires dense data for global value functions), ID-WM does not rely on global trajectory coverage. Furthermore, while H-Diffuser uses abstraction, it lacks an explicit dynamics model, resulting in brittle plans requiring expensive, 9high-frequency replanning. By grounding its hierarchy in explicit local dynamics, ID-WM generates robust, scalable plans. 

Scaling with Goal Horizon. Building on the PushT planning benchmark introduced by Zhou et al. [8] (which evaluates goals 25 steps away), we increase complexity by sampling goals from the validation set that are d ∈ { 25 , 50 , . . . , 150 } steps away. We compare ID-WM against a single-level world model baseline that performs full-horizon CEM search and replanning at every step. While the total number of executed actions is identical, ID-WM 

achieves a near-perfect success rate across all horizons as shown in Figure 7b). This confirms our main PushT results (Table 1), demonstrating that ID-WM maintains precise goal-reaching capabilities even when goals are over 150 steps away. 

4.6 Vision-based Planning with ID-WM 

To evaluate ID-WM in high-dimensional observation spaces, we test it on a vision-based PushT task. We use the state-based PushT dataset augmented with four 90 ◦ rotations for spatial robustness. Observations ( 3 × 64 × 64 images) are encoded into 4 × 8 × 8 latents using a pretrained Stable Diffusion VAE [66]. We compare against AVDC [64], a language-conditioned video planner that generates all subgoals simultaneously (details in Section 6.4). As shown in Figure 8, ID-WM generates reliable, sequential subgoals that ensure steady progress. In contrast, AVDC fails to produce meaningful plans, likely because the initial-goal pairs are out-of-distribution. This highlights the generalization advantage of iterative subgoal search over simultaneous generation—which relies heavily on trajectory-level dataset coverage—even within compressed visual spaces.

10 5 Conclusion 

We present ID-WM , a hierarchical world modeling and planning framework that decouples tem-porally extended subgoal generation from local reachability. By treating diffusion noise as implicit actions, we constrain the planning search space to a unit Gaussian, ensuring reliable subgoal gener-ation. ID-WM significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines and demonstrates high-quality vision-based subgoal generation. Ultimately, grounding hierarchical abstraction in explicit local dynamics represents a promising step toward robust, long-horizon decision-making. 

Limitations. First, ID-WM ’s reliance on search-based optimization within a diffusion latent space incurs higher inference times than reactive policies or deterministic world models. Future work will explore amortized or gradient-based planning to mitigate this bottleneck. Second, while our hierarchy prevents planning complexity from scaling with trajectory length, it still scales with state-space dimensionality. Integrating compact, structured representation learning could further streamline this planning process for highly complex environments.

6.1 Environments and Dataset Generation 

We evaluate ID-WM on three navigation and manipulation environment suites as shown in Figure 3. The environments are Wall, PushT, and Maze, with task horizon ranging from hundreds to thousands of environment steps. a) Wall: Introduced in Zhou et al. [8], this is a simple 2D navigation environment that consists of two rooms separated by a wall with a door. The agent’s goal is to navigate from a random starting location on one side of the wall to a random location on the other side, requiring the agent to pass through the door. We use the same dataset as in Zhou et al. [8], which consists of randomly generated 1920 trajectories of length 50. b) Maze: This environment is introduced in Fu et al. [67], where a force-actuated 2-DoF ball navigates to an arbitrary location in a maze. In this work, we consider maze layout of four sizes, U ( 3 × 3), Medium ( 6 × 6), Large ( 7 × 10 ), and Giant ( 13 × 13 ), visualized in Figure 7 a). We generate 2000 random trajectories of length 100 for each maze layout. c) PushT: Introduced by Chi et al. [59], this environment consists of a pusher agent and a T-shaped block. The objective is for both the agent and the block to reach specified target locations from random initial positions. We evaluate two variants of this task: in Fix Goal , the target state is a fixed configuration (the green T); in Random Goal , the target is randomly sampled from the environment’s state space. Both settings are significantly more challenging than the formulation in Zhou et al. [8], where the goal is provided at a known, fixed horizon of 25 steps. In contrast, completing the task in our settings often requires hundreds of steps, rendering standard single-level planning infeasible. Following [8], we train on noisy, replayed trajectories. We further evaluate our method in a data-abundant regime by applying random translation and rotation augmentations to the existing dataset. 

6.2 Task Visualizations 

For the Wall, PushT, Giant Maze, Large Maze, Medium Maze, and U-Maze environments, we randomly sample 50 initial and goal states from each environment’s state space (visualizations in Figure 9). For PushT, the random-goal setting samples both the initial and goal states randomly from the environment, whereas the fixed-goal setting samples a random initial state while using the environment’s fixed goal location. For the Wall environment, to ensure sufficient task difficulty, the initial and final object locations are constrained to lie on opposite sides of the wall. For the Giant Maze, Large Maze, Medium Maze, and U-Maze environments, we follow the random state sampling procedure used in OGBench: we sample arbitrary free cells for the ball and add continuous uniform noise around the corresponding grid-cell centers. 

6.3 Data Augmentation Data augmentation for PushT. For PushT we additionally apply geometric data augmentation to the offline dataset at each training step. The state vector contains end-effector position and velocity 

(xdot , y dot , ˙xdot , ˙ydot ), T-block position (xT , y T ), and T-block orientation (sin θT , cos θT ). One of three augmentation types is sampled per step: • Displacement (40%): all positions shifted by a random integer offset (δx, δy ) chosen so that both the end-effector and the T-block remain within the screen boundaries [50 , 450] and 

[100 , 400] respectively. • Rotation (30%): all positions and the T-block angle rotated by θ ∼ U (0 , 2π) about the scene center; velocities and actions are rotated accordingly. • Displacement + Rotation (30%): a half-range displacement combined with a small rotation 

θ ∼ U (0 , 30 ◦).

6.8 Steps-to-goal metric 

Success rate measures whether a goal is reached but not how efficiently. To quantify trajectory efficiency, we report the average number of environment steps to success for all methods on PushT-Aug (Random Goal) and Medium Maze in Table 5, computed over successful trajectories only (N/A if no trajectory succeeded). ID-WM is the most efficient method on PushT-Aug and the most efficient among value-function-free methods on Medium Maze. HIQL’s learned value function yields substantially shorter maze paths, reflecting a trade-off: the planning cost in Section 3.2 is the distance between the last imagined state and the goal, which optimizes for reaching the goal rather than for path efficiency. Within the same framework, a self-supervised value function could be substituted for this cost to improve path optimality.

6.11.6 Diffuser [61] 

Diffuser frames offline goal-conditioned planning as a trajectory denoising problem. A temporal U-Net processes a full trajectory τ = ( s0, a 0, . . . , s H−1, a H−1) of planning horizon H, and iterative reverse diffusion over T steps is guided by goal conditioning to produce trajectories that reach a specified goal state. At inference, the current state is inpainted at the start of the trajectory and the goal state at the end; the remaining timesteps are filled by denoising. All environments share the same base configuration. Only the planning horizon H is adjusted (in Table 16). Detailed implementation choices for inference are expanded in Section 6.11.8. 

6.11.7 Hierarchical Diffuser [23] 

H-Diffuser [23] extends Diffuser with a two-level hierarchy. A high-level diffusion model plans jumpy subgoals spaced J timesteps apart, producing a compressed sequence of length ⌈Hhl /J ⌉ that covers a larger temporal horizon at lower computational cost. A low-level diffusion model then plans a dense trajectory of length Hll between consecutive subgoals, conditioned on the current state and the subgoal as the terminal constraint. Hyperparameters shared with Diffuser are listed in Table 17. Environment-specific hyperparameters are listed in Table 16. Detailed implementation choices for inference are expanded in Section 6.11.8. 

6.11.8 Baseline Tuning for Diffuser & H-Diffuser 

The original Diffuser paper uses two execution strategies depending on the environment: for D4RL locomotion tasks, only the first action of each plan is executed and the model replans at every step (execute one action + replan ); for maze2d and Kuka block-stacking, the full planned trajectory is executed open-loop without replanning ( execute plan ). H-Diffuser adopts the same two strategies at the subgoal level. To enable a fair comparison with our method, we additionally evaluate both models under a third strategy in which the full plan is executed and the model then replans ( execute plan + replan ). 6.12 High-Level World Model Training Convergence 

We further analyze the training-time convergence behavior of the high-level world model, extending the qualitative study in Figure 4. While Figure 4 visualizes the final learned neighboring-state distribution, Figure 11 shows how this distribution emerges over the course of training. Specifically, we fix validation observations s0 and periodically sample predicted future states from intermediate checkpoints of the high-level WM. The first row shows the initial observation s0

sampled from the validation set. The lower rows visualize generated future states ˆs∆ = fhi (s0, z, ∆) 

for horizons ∆ ∈ { 1, 3, 5}, where each prediction is obtained by sampling latent noise vectors 

z ∼ N (0 , I ). Each column corresponds to a different number of training steps on a logarithmic scale: 20, 60, 200, 600, 2000, 6000, and 20000. As training begins, the predicted distributions are highly dispersed and often fail to preserve the structure of the PushT scene. In early checkpoints, the sampled futures do not consistently respect the local geometry of the object or the physically plausible displacement induced by the agent-object interaction. As training progresses, the generated samples become increasingly concentrated around coherent future configurations. By approximately 200 to 600 training steps, the model begins to capture the coarse reachable region for each temporal horizon, and later checkpoints further sharpen the predicted distribution while preserving meaningful sample diversity.
