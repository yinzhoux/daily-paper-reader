Robotic manipulation policies can improve action prediction by anticipating how objects move and
how their spatial relationships change. World Action Models (WAMs) learn to predict future visual
observations, but their training objectives typically leave the underlying 3D geometric transitions im-
plicit. To alleviate this problem, we introduce Foresight Forcing (F2), a WAM that learns a spatial
transition representation from changes in a pretrained 3D feature space. During training, a frozen
3D foundation model extracts patch-wise feature differences from aligned current and future observa-
tions that provide dense supervision for geometry-aware feature transitions. We distill these targets
into spatial transition queries, which are progressively refined from the current observation, language
instruction, and proprioceptive state, and use them alongside current visual features to generate ac-
tions. At deployment, the policy predicts the queries without future observations or the 3D teacher.
Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin 2.0 demonstrate strong manipulation per-
formance and improved robustness, including 95.01% average success on RoboTwin 2.0 Full. On six
real-world manipulation tasks, F2 achieves a mean task score of 85.8%.

Introduction
Robotic manipulation has progressed from task-specific systems designed for controlled environments towards
generalist policies that operate across diverse objects, instructions, and settings. A general-purpose robot
must interpret task instructions, perceive objects in its surroundings, and execute precise actions under
varying conditions. Recent advances in large-scale robot learning and pretrained vision-language models have
enabled Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies to map visual observations and natural-language instructions
to robot actions [3, 4, 14, 15, 48]. However, action labels do not directly supervise the visual outcomes of those
actions. To incorporate this information into policy learning, World Action Models (WAMs) build on visual
generative models and couple action learning with world modeling objectives [2, 6, 45, 47]. The resulting
visual prediction task provides an additional training signal alongside action supervision and enables robot
policies to benefit from pretrained generative representations.
Most WAMs learn how a scene evolves by predicting future RGB observations or visual latents.
These
objectives encode task-relevant spatial relationships alongside object appearance, background, and viewpoint,
leaving the geometric transitions that matter for control implicit. Geometry Forcing shows that pretrained
3D features can improve the geometric consistency of video generation [40], and geometry-aware WAMs have
explored geometric supervision for manipulation [19, 24, 46].
For robot control, the 3D changes needed
to advance the task provide a natural target for this supervision. Placing an object requires changing its
pose relative to a target, while opening a drawer requires changing the configuration of an articulated object.
Modeling such transitions gives the policy a way to anticipate how the scene needs to change when generating
actions.
Therefore, we introduce Foresight Forcing (F2), a WAM that fuses correspondence-aligned transitions in 3D
geometry feature space of foundation models into spatial transition queries for action generation, rather than
directly predicting explicit 3D motions (Figure 1). During training, a frozen VGGT teacher [32] extracts
patch features from an aligned observation pair, and their temporal differences serve as dense targets for the
queries. A shared updater progressively refines the queries after each double block of the visual backbone
using information from the current observation, language instruction, and proprioceptive state. We supervise
the queries to match the direction and scale of the teacher targets and feed the refined queries directly to the
action expert. At inference, the policy predicts the queries from its current inputs without access to future
observations or the teacher. We evaluate F2 on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-world
manipulation tasks to assess manipulation performance, robustness, and inference eﬀiciency. F2 achieves
state-of-the-art performance across simulation and real robot.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• Transition-centric geometric supervision. We introduce correspondence-aligned 3D teacher feature differ-
ences as supervision for spatial transition learning for action generation in a World Action Model.
• Teacher-guided spatial transition learning for action generation. We introduce dedicated latent queries that
a shared updater progressively refines across the visual backbone. During training, we align the refined
queries with the frozen 3D teacher targets in direction and scale. The queries directly condition the action
expert.
• Manipulation performance and robustness.
Evaluations on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin 2.0
demonstrate strong manipulation performance and improved robustness, with state-of-the-art average
success rates on RoboTwin 2.0 Full and Clean-to-Random. On six real-world tasks, F2 achieves a mean
task score of 85.8%, exceeding ImageWAM and π0.5 by 15.6 and 2.3 percentage points, respectively.

Related Work
World Action Models.
World Action Models (WAMs) jointly learn action generation and future visual
prediction through autoregressive video-action modeling, shared latent representations, compact future condi-
tions, or image-based prediction [2, 6, 18, 30, 45, 47]. Visual prediction provides supervision for control, even
when future images are not generated during execution. Building on ImageWAM, we study a complementary
signal: the spatial transition between current and future observations.

3D Representations for Visual Generation.
Feed-forward 3D models recover spatial structure and
dense features from images. DUSt3R and MASt3R establish pairwise geometry and correspondences [16, 36];
subsequent models extend inference to multiple views and image streams [20, 32–34, 39, 42]. REPA aligns
diffusion representations with pretrained visual features [44], while geometric features guide consistent video
generation and image editing [23, 25, 40]. We use a frozen VGGT to encode paired observations and supervise
the policy with correspondence-aligned differences between its current and future features.
Geometry in Action Learning.
Existing action models incorporate geometry either as an observation
or predicted spatial representation, or as supervision for learned features. The former includes geometric
inputs, point flow, trajectories, future pointmaps, and spatially structured actions [9, 11, 12, 26, 35, 41, 46].
The latter uses geometric objectives or distilled priors to shape policy representations [17, 19, 24]. Foresight
Forcing instead supervises dedicated spatial queries with correspondence-aligned temporal differences in 3D
teacher features. The queries are predicted from current inputs and directly condition the action expert.

Method
World Action Models (WAMs) jointly predict future observations and robot actions. Building on this frame-
work, F2 introduces spatial transition queries that encode task-relevant geometric changes over the action
horizon. During training, a frozen 3D teacher supplies feature differences between aligned current and fu-
ture observations as dense targets. The queries condition action generation alongside current visual features;
neither future observations nor the teacher is required at deployment (Figure 2). We formulate the task in
Sec. 3.1, construct the geometric transition target in Sec. 3.2, explain how the queries learn from it in Sec. 3.3,
and detail training and deployment in Sec. 3.4.

3.4
Training Strategy and Deployment
Reference-condition dropout.
Within the MoT attention mask, the Action Expert attends to the in-
struction, proprioceptive state, action tokens, and Z∆across blocks while staying masked from future tokens.
Attention to Hcur is enabled with probability q = 0.5 during training, exposing the Action Expert to both
reference-assisted and transition-only conditioning. At inference, future tokens, VGGT, and distillation heads
are omitted, and both Hcur and Z∆remain visible. Complete attention masks and token-visibility rules are
provided in Appendix A.
Optimization.
We jointly optimize future-image prediction, action generation, and geometric transition
alignment:

gradually increases the weight of geometric supervision during training. Details are provided in Appendix A.

Experiments
We evaluate manipulation performance, robustness to distribution shifts, the contributions of individual
components, real-world applicability, and inference cost.
4.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmarks.
We evaluate F2 on LIBERO [22], LIBERO-Plus [10], RoboTwin 2.0 [8], and real-world
manipulation tasks. LIBERO is a single-arm simulation benchmark; we evaluate its Spatial, Object, Goal,
and Long suites, each containing 10 tasks. LIBERO-Plus tests robustness across seven perturbation dimen-
sions: object layout, camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language instruction, lighting, background, and
observation noise. RoboTwin 2.0 comprises 50 dual-arm manipulation tasks in simulated tabletop scenes,
including object placement, stacking, tool use, and articulated-object interaction. We evaluate both its Full
and Clean-to-Random settings under clean and randomized scenes. For real-world evaluation, we compare
three methods on six manipulation tasks.

Models and evaluation.
Following ImageWAM [47], we train F2 with geometric transition supervision
added to ImageWAM after fine-tuning on the corresponding benchmark demonstrations.
For LIBERO,
LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin Full, we do not use robot pretraining.
For RoboTwin Clean-to-Random,
we use robot pretraining on InternData-A1 [31] before fine-tuning on clean demonstrations. As a control,
we also continue fine-tuning ImageWAM without geometric supervision under the same settings; evaluation
shows no consistent improvement, with slight decreases in some settings. We compare F2 with ImageWAM
and representative VLA and WAM methods using task success rates. Training configurations are provided
in Appendix A.
4.2
In-Distribution Performance
We first examine whether F2 maintains strong standard manipulation performance when the evaluation
follows the training distribution.
LIBERO.
Table 1 reports success on the four standard suites. Each suite contains 10 tasks, and we evaluate
each task over 50 trials. F2 achieves an average success rate of 98.5%, compared with 98.4% for ImageWAM
and matching the leading methods at the reported precision. As these methods approach the success-rate
ceiling, standard LIBERO offers limited headroom for distinguishing their performance.
RoboTwin 2.0.
In the RoboTwin 2.0 Full setting, F2 achieves the highest clean, randomized, and average
success rates in Table 2, without robot-policy pretraining. It reaches 94.90% and 95.12% in the clean and
randomized settings, respectively, improving over ImageWAM by 1.70 and 1.56 percentage points. Its average
success is 95.01%, compared with 94.55% for Flex-π. The gains over ImageWAM in both settings suggest that
spatial transition supervision benefits manipulation across the evaluated scene conditions. Per-task results
are reported in Appendix B.

Its improvement over DINOv3 suggests that geometry-oriented teacher features can provide useful spatial
supervision for manipulation, while the result with π3 shows that the choice of geometric teacher also matters.
How do query design and action conditioning affect performance?
Finally, we examine how the
transition signal is represented and used for action generation. The frame-matched budget of N = 392 queries
(LIBERO’s 14×28 two-view token grid) performs best among the tested sizes, while both 200 and 512 queries
perform worse, showing that simply increasing the budget does not guarantee improvement. This comparison
varies the query budget and does not isolate the effect of spatial correspondence from other changes in the
representation. With the query grid in place, removing the shared updater while retaining native masked
attention (leaving Z∆self-attention-only, without access to current visual or instruction tokens) reduces
success from 84.5% to 81.7%, supporting the complete updater design for integrating current visual and
conditioning states. This comparison tests the updater as a whole, including its learnable computation and
parameter sharing across blocks. A fixed reference keep probability of q = 0.5 outperforms schedules that
anneal q to zero by the midpoint or end of training, supporting the retention of reference-assisted examples
alongside Z∆.

4.5
Real-World Evaluation
We evaluate physical manipulation on six tasks: cup stacking, towel folding, egg transfer, breakfast prepara-
tion, tool stowing, and size sorting. We compare π0.5 [3], ImageWAM [47], and F2, each jointly trained on

Success criteria.
Cup stacking, towel folding, egg transfer, and breakfast preparation require full-task
completion. Tool stowing and size sorting use item-level success rates. Each tool-stowing trial contains four
tools, while each size-sorting trial contains 15 blocks and is scored by the fraction correctly sorted.
F2 achieves an average score of 85.8%, compared with 83.5% for π0.5 and 70.2% for ImageWAM. Whereas
ImageWAM reports strong real-world performance on its original suite [47], its lower scores here reflect
differences in task suites, demonstrations, and evaluation protocols. In particular, several of our tasks require
consecutive manipulation steps, during which the policy must respond to changing object configurations.
The learned transition representation may provide useful spatial information for these tasks. Although F2
is trained without robot pretraining, its results are competitive with the pretrained π0.5 on several tasks.
Further evaluation should focus on generalization.

4.6
Inference Efficiency
We assess the inference cost of spatial transition conditioning in Table 6. Under the same measurement
settings, F2 takes 195.01 ± 2.99 ms per 16-action chunk, compared with 187.41 ± 4.80 ms for ImageWAM.
This modest 4.1% increase accompanies the manipulation improvements reported above. The small overhead
indicates that the additional query pathway largely preserves the runtime eﬀiciency of the underlying WAM.

The optimized ImageWAM and F2 achieve 80.20 ± 3.74 ms and 99.53 ± 3.26 ms per chunk, respectively. For
F2, this reduces the inference latency by approximately 49%, supporting more responsive action generation
during real-world execution.
5
Conclusion
F2 learns spatial transition queries from correspondence-aligned changes in pretrained 3D features. These
queries provide the Action Expert with a representation of anticipated spatial change, connecting geomet-
ric supervision during training to action generation from current observations. Experiments on LIBERO,
LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and six real-world tasks show strong manipulation performance and improved
robustness. Together, the results suggest that supervising how a scene changes in 3D feature space is a
useful complement to future-image prediction in world action models. Studying how spatial transition learn-
ing generalizes across broader tasks, embodiments, and environments is an important direction for future
research.

A.3
Teacher and Spatial Layout
The frozen VGGT aggregator outputs patch features of channel width D = 2048. Camera and register tokens are
stripped prior to bilinear interpolation onto each camera’s region in the student token grid. Our layout supports
horizontal and vertical multi-view concatenations as well as the RoboTwin three-view mosaic (a top head view above
two side-by-side wrist views). Image crops, teacher features, flow-validity masks (Sec. A.1), and transition queries
share identical spatial bounding boxes within the mosaic.
Feature subtraction and geometry-loss reductions are
computed in FP32 to preserve small feature differences across large teacher activations.
A.4
Distillation Details

convolutional layers (each followed by a SiLU activation) and a 1 × 1 output convolution. The scale head Sθ is a

For Figure 4(b), we freeze the trained policy and fit a lightweight two-layer MLP

A.6
Data Augmentation
During training, we apply synchronized image augmentations across frames within each camera view, including re-
sized cropping, rotation, color jitter, gamma adjustment, and Gaussian noise. Under the RoboTwin Clean-to-Random
setting, following PatchWAM [37], we additionally apply stronger appearance augmentations comprising photometric
jitter, Gaussian blur, AdaIN-style feature-statistic perturbation, and Fourier amplitude modulation. Spatial transfor-
mations are shared between student and teacher inputs so geometric correspondence is preserved, whereas appearance
augmentations are applied solely to the student inputs.
A.7
Training–Inference Visibility
Figure 7 illustrates the native MoT attention masks used during training and inference. Within the native MoT blocks,
transition queries Z∆perform self-attention only, preventing any leakage from future-image tokens during training;
they instead aggregate current visual and conditioning features through the shared updater Uθ using identical current-
only context at both training and test time. At inference, future-image tokens are absent and the Action Expert
attends jointly to current visual states Hcur and transition queries Z∆.

C.2
Task Success Criteria
Cup stacking.
The robot unstacks a nested pile of cups into a horizontal row and then stacks them into a pyramid.
A trial succeeds if the entire sequence finishes within the time limit. Minor execution errors (e.g., missed grasps, tipped
cups, or overlapping placements) are permitted if the policy autonomously recovers and completes the pyramid within
the allotted time; otherwise, the trial fails.
Towel folding.
The robot folds a flat towel in half, folds it in half a second time, and presses the folded cloth flat.
A trial succeeds only if all three steps are completed cleanly; failure at any stage counts as a failed trial.
Egg transfer.
The robot transfers eggs from a source carton into designated slots in a destination tray. A trial
succeeds when all eggs are properly seated in the tray; rolling eggs or visibly misplaced placements count as failure.
18


Breakfast preparation.
The robot sequentially places a slice of bread, a fried egg, a second slice of bread, a
banana, and a carrot onto a plate. A trial succeeds if the full assembly completes within the time limit, including
autonomous recovery from intermediate mistakes; any incomplete sequence counts as failure.
Tool stowing.
Each trial presents four articulated tools to be placed into fitted organizer slots in a toolbox. We
score the fraction of tools stowed successfully; if a tool fails, it is marked as a failure and an operator places it so
the policy can proceed to the remaining tools. The reported score is the total number of stowed tools divided by 60
across 15 trials.
Size sorting.
Each trial contains 15 blocks to be sorted by size into a large box and a small box. We report the
mean fraction of correctly sorted blocks across 15 trials. A block counts as a failure if the robot fails to grasp it,
selects the wrong box, or drops it outside the target box.
D
Attention Analysis
We apply an identical diagnostic procedure to ImageWAM and F 2 on randomized RoboTwin observations (Figure 5),
holding the current RGB inputs, proprioceptive state, instruction, and sampling seed fixed within each pair to compare
the two methods under matched inputs.
For Figure 5, we adapt attention rollout [1] with gradient-weighted attention propagation [7], combining head-averaged
positive attention–gradient products with additive residual transport across the image backbone, shared query updater,
and Action Expert. Attribution targets the first action’s left-gripper component at the final denoising step with earlier
action latents held fixed. Routes are grouped by their entry edge into the action stream and normalized over all three
camera views (only the head view is displayed).
