## Abstract 

Robot demonstrations provide more than actions to im-itate: they reveal how actions change the world and how those changes contribute to task success. Recent world–action models (WAMs) extend vision–language– action (VLA) models with the first signal by jointly pre-dicting actions and future observations, yet this predictive ability serves only as an auxiliary loss for policy learn-ing. Meanwhile, most policy improvement methods learn dynamics and values in separate models, increasing train-ing overhead and limiting representation sharing between these complementary objectives. We introduce DexWAC , a unified world–actor–critic model that jointly learns an ac-tor, an action-conditioned world model, and a bootstrapped critic in a single robot pretraining stage. Our key insight is that visual prediction encourages control-relevant features, while value supervision promotes task-relevant representa-tions; together, they shape a representation suited to pol-icy learning. Built on an image-editing backbone, DexWAC predicts, for each action chunk, the visual latent and propri-oception at its endpoint together with its reward and contin-uation, so the world model alone efficiently yields complete imagined transitions without generating intermediate video frames. After adapting the world model and critic on real rollouts, we improve the pretrained actor through either PPO on imagined rollouts or critic-guided best-of-N se-lection. DexWAC surpasses the large-scale pretrained VLA and WAM baselines in simulation and achieves 75% aver-age success on two real-world tasks, compared with 45% for π0.5. Its world model is more accurate and faster than video-based baselines, and ablations confirm that learning dynamics and value together improves the policy. 

## 1. Introduction 

Large-scale learning from demonstrations has enabled robot policies to operate across tasks and embodiments, offer-ing a scalable path toward general-purpose physical in-telligence [2, 13, 28]. Yet imitating actions alone leaves much of the supervision in these demonstrations unused. A demonstration reveals not only which action an expert chose, but also how it changes the world [44] and how those changes contribute to task success [48]. Data collection and policy execution also yield suboptimal trajectories, failed attempts, and unstructured play, which are often underused by imitation-based policies. Exploiting this supervision can help a policy understand both what its actions can change 

and which factors matter for the task [24]. Recent world–action models (WAMs) jointly predict ac-tions and future observations [1, 10], but use this prediction mainly as an auxiliary objective for policy learning. Mean-while, methods to improve pretrained policies often intro-duce separately trained world models [9, 50] and reward or value models [18, 43]. For a generalist policy, these mod-els must themselves generalize across the same broad range of tasks and embodiments as the policy [20]. Pretraining them separately at this scale can duplicate computation and relearn overlapping representations. It also keeps the pre-dictive and value objectives from jointly shaping the fea-tures the actor uses [5]. These observations motivate two questions: Can a single pretraining stage jointly acquire an actor, a world model, and a critic? And can their comple-mentary objectives constrain a shared representation that improves policy learning? 

We therefore introduce DexWAC (Fig. 1), a unified World–Actor–Critic model for dexterous manipulation that learns all three components in a single pretraining stage. To share one representation across these components, we adopt a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture [22] that couples a generative world model backbone with a compact actor–critic expert. The backbone encodes the lan-guage instruction, current visual observations, and propri-oception as a shared prefix of attention keys and values (K/V). Through action imitation, the actor learns to use these com-plementary control- and task-relevant features to generate expert action chunks (Fig. 4). To make imagination efficient, we build the world model on an image-editing backbone and align its predictions with the actor’s action chunks: it predicts only the visual latent and proprioception at each chunk endpoint rather than inter-mediate video frames, and these predictions serve as context for the next imagined chunk. After pretraining, we adapt the world model and critic on successful and failed rollouts of the pretrained actor. We then freeze the world backbone and use it to generate imagined experience, on which the critic is further trained for model-based policy optimization [14]. We instantiate this stage with two methods: PPO [36] reg-ularized by behavior cloning on expert actions, and critic-guided best-of-N (BoN) selection inspired by MAC [29], where an action-value critic trained on imagined transitions ranks candidate chunks from the frozen actor. We evaluate DexWAC on two simulation benchmarks, DexJoCo [40] and RoboTwin 2.0 [4], and on two contact-rich manipulation tasks using a physical dexterous robot. Joint pretraining alone yields a policy that reaches 65.5% success on DexJoCo, compared with 57.9% for π0.5 [13], and the best average success on RoboTwin 2.0 (88.1%). Its world model achieves the best prediction quality on all six image-quality metrics, reducing wrist-view LPIPS by 38% while running 8.5× faster than the strongest baseline. Using only 50 executions per task for adaptation, PPO in imag-ination raises DexJoCo success to 67.8%, surpassing the large-scale pretrained WAMs, and improves bimanual suc-cess from 47.8% to 52.9%. On a physical robot, DexWAC outperforms π0.5 on two contact-rich tasks (50% vs. 20% and 100% vs. 70%). Ablations further show that removing either world prediction or value supervision reduces policy success. In summary, our contributions are as follows: 

1. We propose DexWAC, which jointly trains an actor, a world model, and a critic with shared representations in a single pretraining stage. 2. We evaluate the pretrained policy in two simulations and on two contact-rich dexterous manipulation tasks with a physical robot, and demonstrate the benefits of joint supervision through ablations. 3. We adapt the world model and critic to execution data, evaluate world-model accuracy and efficiency, and demonstrate their utility for multi-task policy improve-ment through imagined PPO and critic-guided BoN. 22. Related Work 

2.1. Unified Foundation Models 

Unified foundation models learn shared representations across multiple objectives, enabling mutually beneficial transfer. Transfusion [51], Show-o [45], and BAGEL [6] demonstrate the benefits of jointly learning multimodal un-derstanding and generation. Embodied models extend this paradigm to action generation, often leveraging visual gen-erative priors [10, 49]. UWM [52], Cosmos 3 [28], and Motus [1] unify understanding, future prediction, and ac-tion generation, but primarily use dynamics prediction as auxiliary supervision. LeWAM [7] uses the learned dy-namics at test time to evaluate sampled actions, but learns neither rewards nor values. FACT [31] and Cosmos Pol-icy [15] further incorporate value prediction into world– action modeling. However, they supervise value prediction with trajectory-derived normalized progress labels, rather than learning a temporal-difference critic for policy im-provement. Shared policy–value representations have also been explored in reinforcement learning (PPG [5]) and lan-guage agents [21]. DexWAC extends this unification to joint learning of action generation, action-conditioned world modeling, reward prediction, and temporal-difference value estimation through shared representations. 

2.2. Policy Improvement for Pretrained Policies 

Pretrained policies can fail on states poorly covered by their demonstrations, motivating further learning from subopti-mal and failed executions. Online RL addresses this gap through environment interaction [3, 39], but real-world ex-ploration is slow and costly, especially across multiple tasks and embodiments. Offline RL learns from existing data, but unreliable value estimates can destabilize policy opti-mization and lead to policy collapse [16, 17]. V-GPS [26] and SeeQ [37] instead learn critics from diverse robot data and use them to rank candidate actions at test time, steer-ing behavior without directly updating the policy. World models offer an alternative by learning environment dy-namics and generating imagined experience for policy op-timization and value learning [8, 11, 29, 50]. However, achieving broad dynamics coverage often requires separate world-model pretraining and adaptation, introducing addi-tional training costs [9]. DexWAC instead adapts the dy-namics, reward model, and critic learned during joint pre-training and reuses them for policy optimization, reducing the need for a separately trained world model.

## 4. Method 

DexWAC proceeds in three stages (Fig. 1). Joint pretraining learns, from the same demonstrations, an actor for action generation, a world model for predicting the visual and pro-prioceptive consequences of actions, and a critic for state-value estimation (Sec. 4.1). Next, we adapt the world model and critic on real rollouts of the pretrained actor, so that they remain accurate on the states the policy actually visits (Sec. 4.2). Finally, the adapted components improve con-trol, either by updating the actor in imagination or by rank-ing its proposals (Sec. 4.3). Hyperparameters and imple-mentation details are given in Appendix A. 

4.1. Learning to Act, Predict, and Evaluate 

We design the pretraining stage so that one representation serves acting, predicting, and evaluating. A Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture [22] couples a genera-tive world backbone θ with a compact actor–critic expert ϕ

(Fig. 2). The backbone encodes the context Ct = ( ℓ, z t, s t)

of instruction, visual latents zt = EVAE (ot) [34], and pro-prioceptive state, and the expert reads its keys and values (K/V). The two thus share features through attention but keep separate weights, which limits interference with the backbone’s pretrained visual priors [6]. Two choices make the backbone a complete simulator for imagination. First, given Ct and an action chunk, it pre-dicts only the visual latent and proprioceptive state at the chunk endpoint t + H [29], so each imagined step needs a single prediction. Second, it predicts reward and continua-tion from the reached state, so each imagined transition, in-cluding its feedback, comes entirely from the world model.

## 5. Experiments 

Our experiments address four questions. (1) Does joint world–actor–critic pretraining yield a policy competi-tive with state-of-the-art embodied foundation models? (2) Does the world model predict action consequences ac-curately and efficiently? (3) Can the adapted world model and critic further improve the pretrained policy? (4) How do value supervision, visual prediction, and reward smoothing contribute to these results? 

5.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. Our main benchmark is DexJoCo [40]. We use eight of its eleven tasks and exclude three that require long-horizon memory, which is outside the scope of this study. The five single-arm tasks are click mouse ,

fold glasses , hammer nail , pick bucket ,and water plant ; the three bimanual tasks are 

bimanual hanoi , bimanual microwave cook ,and bimanual photograph . Together they cover diverse objects, contact interactions, and coordination requirements. We further evaluate on the 50 tasks of RoboTwin 2.0 [4] under clean and randomized settings, and on two tasks with a physical robot (Sec. 5.5). 

Training protocol. On DexJoCo, we jointly pretrain a single language-conditioned DexWAC model across all eight tasks on 800 expert demonstrations for 10,000 opti-mizer updates, with a global batch size of 256. We then adapt a copy of its world model and critic for 5,000 up-dates using the demonstrations and 400 policy rollouts, in-cluding both successful and failed executions. Policy im-provement through imagination also spans all eight tasks, using task-balanced roots to optimize a shared policy. On RoboTwin 2.0, we jointly pretrain across all 50 tasks using 25,000 trajectories from the randomized setting and 2,500 from the clean setting. Detailed hyperparameters and im-plementation settings are provided in Appendix A.

5.3. Action-Conditioned World Modeling 

We evaluate the accuracy and efficiency of the adapted world model on DexJoCo. For a consistent comparison, DexWAC and all baselines are pretrained on the same 800 expert demonstrations, then adapted using these demonstra-tions and 400 policy-rollout trajectories. On held-out trajec-tories, all models predict observations at t + 16 from identi-cal observations and recorded actions. We measure predic-tion quality using LPIPS [47], PSNR [41], and SSIM [42] for head and wrist views, and assess efficiency through end-to-end prediction latency. Table 3 shows that DexWAC achieves the best re-sults across all six metrics with only three denoising steps. Improvements are particularly pronounced in wrist views, where LPIPS decreases from 0.1373 for FACT to 0.0853. The qualitative examples in Fig. 3 further illus-trate better preservation of gripper geometry, object con-tours, and future object positions. As reported in Table 4, DexWAC predicts a chunk endpoint in 0.214 s, approxi-mately 8.5×, 20 ×, and 300 × faster than FACT, Ctrl-World, and VLA-MBPO, respectively. Together, these results show that DexWAC outperforms the evaluated action-conditioned world models in both prediction quality and speed, provid-ing an effective foundation for policy improvement.

We evaluate DexWAC on single-arm and bimanual dexter-ous manipulation with two physical robots. Each task uses 300 demonstrations. ## 6. Limitations 

Achieving sustained and stable policy improvement in contact-rich dexterous manipulation remains challenging. We think difficult credit assignment is one contributing fac-tor [25]. The problem becomes more pronounced when op-timizing a single policy across multiple tasks, where gradi-ents from different skills may conflict [46]. Finally, due to computational constraints, we have not yet evaluated DexWAC with large-scale robot pretraining. Its scalabil-ity to broader datasets, tasks, and embodiments therefore remains to be established. 

## 7. Conclusion 

We introduced DexWAC, an efficient framework that trains an actor, a world model, and a critic together in a single robot pretraining stage. The resulting policy and world model are both competitive with strong pretrained base-lines, while the adapted world model and critic can be di-rectly reused for model-based policy optimization. Exist-ing approaches often require the policy and world model to be pretrained separately at large scale, making it difficult to determine whether a failure comes from the optimiza-tion method or from differences in the underlying models and data. Direct offline policy improvement. Direct application of IQL yields 54.75% overall success, below the pretrained policy’s 68.00%. Optimization with the original VLA-MBPO settings. 

Applying the original VLA-MBPO optimization settings in our setup leads to policy collapse. We attribute this be-havior to accumulated world-model errors during imagined rollouts. Inaccurate contact predictions produce unreliable future states and reward targets, which optimization can ex-ploit rather than improve actual execution. These observa-tions highlight the difficulty of directly transferring exist-ing optimization settings to contact-rich dexterous manipu-lation with limited execution data. 

## D. Reward-Target Smoothing 

We examine whether smoothing the reward targets helps the model recognize successful terminal states. We com-pare a checkpoint trained with binary targets and binary cross-entropy against one trained with Gaussian-smoothed targets and class-balanced mean squared error. Both check-points are evaluated on the same 400 trajectories collected by Motus with evaluation seed 1. These trajectories contain 266 successful terminal observations across eight DexJoCo tasks. A successful terminal observation is counted as detected when its predicted reward exceeds half of the corresponding target: 0.5 for the binary target and half of the normalized Gaussian terminal value for the smoothed target. No thresh-old calibration is performed. As shown in Fig. 5, the checkpoint trained with Gaussian-smoothed targets achieves higher terminal detec-tion rates on all eight tasks. This diagnostic measures re-call on successful terminal states and does not evaluate false positives or downstream policy performance. Because the checkpoints also differ in initialization and other training settings, the comparison should not be interpreted as isolat-ing the effect of reward smoothing alone. ## E. Discussion 

Multi-seed policy improvement. Table 12 reports the complete DexJoCo results across three evaluation seeds. PPO provides the clearest evidence of multi-task policy im-provement: it improves overall success on every seed and raises the mean from 65.50% to 67.75% after 500 updates. Most of the gain comes from bimanual tasks, whose aver-age success increases from 47.78% to 52.89%. However, the improvement is not uniform across tasks. Several skills improve substantially, while others exhibit modest regres-sions. Optimizing the shared policy therefore improves its average performance without guaranteeing retention of ev-ery pretrained skill. 

Over-optimization in imagination. Continued optimiza-tion does not necessarily produce further gains. With only 50 execution trajectories per task, the adapted world model cannot cover every contact outcome that arises during imag-ined exploration. As training proceeds, the policy or critic may increasingly exploit prediction and reward errors, fa-voring imagined outcomes that receive high predicted re-turns but are not physically realizable. This form of model exploitation is especially pronounced for BoN, where max-imizing over multiple proposals amplifies critic ranking er-rors. 

Sensitivity of contact-rich manipulation. These results expose a central difficulty of model-based policy improve-ment for contact-rich manipulation. Small differences in pose, timing, or contact force can turn a successful grasp or insertion into a slip, collision, or missed contact. Such differences may appear minor in predicted observations but correspond to different task outcomes. Errors in imagined dynamics can then propagate into reward and value tar-gets and become amplified through policy optimization or best-of-N selection.
