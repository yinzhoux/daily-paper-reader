Abstract— Bimanual dexterous manipulation requires coordi-
nated arm and hand control, yet specifying such coordination
manually is challenging and often task-specific. In this work, we
explore whether complex bimanual dexterity can emerge from a
simple object-centric learning objective. We train reinforcement
learning policies under a shared object-centric reward, varying
their training environments rather than tuning separate rewards
for each behavior. We find that the policies discover pre-grasp
manipulation, grasping and regrasping, in-hand adjustment
and handover to reach object-pose goals without hand-designed
motion sequences. We then refine the experts on transition states
and use DAgger and reinforcement learning to distill them into a
single policy that composes these behaviors for diverse bimanual
tasks. On real hardware, the same policy succeeds in 61 of 80
trials (76.3%) across four tasks with unseen objects and no task-
specific fine-tuning. In simulated barcode scanning, our policy
achieves 85.0% success, compared with 60.4% for two single-
arm policies without handover. These results suggest that this

I. INTRODUCTION
Bimanual dexterous manipulation systems have the poten-
tial to perform a wide range of everyday tasks: picking up
an object, reorienting it to expose a desired face, and passing
it between hands. Existing systems have demonstrated these
capabilities on individual tasks [1]–[3]. However, specifying
these behaviors is challenging because the robot must decide
not only how to move the object, but also which hand to use
and when to pass it between hands. Existing methods often
specify this coordination through task-specific rewards or skill
sequences. These specifications are difficult to reuse across
tasks, so extending the system to new behaviors requires
repeated engineering.
arXiv:2611.10410v1  [cs.RO]  7 Nov 2026


Object-centric formulations sidestep part of this problem by
specifying tasks purely in terms of the object’s desired state,
leaving the robot free to discover how to bring it about. For
example, SimToolReal [1] trains a single-arm policy to move
procedurally generated tool-like objects to random 6-DoF goal
poses and transfers zero-shot to unseen real tools. Tasks can
then be specified as sequences of object goal poses without
object- or task-specific training. Extending this approach to
two hands over a larger workspace, however, poses a learning
challenge. For goals beyond either hand’s reach, the policy
must discover how to transfer the object before it can complete
the goal. RL can instead settle for partial progress with one
hand without discovering the handover needed to reach the
goal.
The straightforward approach is to add task-specific rewards
that shape hand selection and handover. This can guide
exploration, but requires specifying how the hands should
coordinate for each behavior. New behaviors may then require
new reward terms, weakening the benefit of a reusable object-
goal interface.
In this work, we extend object-centric task specification
to bimanual dexterous manipulation by designing training
environments rather than hand-craft separate rewards for
each behavior. We vary the distribution of object goals
and constrain each hand’s workspace under a shared object-
centric reward. Bimanual behaviors emerge during training,
without explicitly programming how the hands should pick
up, reorient, or pass the object (Fig. 1).
More specifically, we define a reachable workspace for
each hand and train two single-arm experts for object control
within their respective workspaces. Furthermore, we train a
bimanual expert for cross-workspace transfer (Sec. III-B). All
three share the same object-centric representation and reward,
while their training tasks only differ in goal sampling, episode
initialization and termination. For single-arm training, the
object starts on the table and the goals stay within that arm’s
reach. The hand learns to pick up the object, reorient it, and
change its grasp as the goals change. For handover training,
one hand starts holding the object, and the goal is sampled
from the other hand’s workspace, forcing the holding hand to
pass the object to the other hand to reach the goal. A goal is
complete only when the receiving hand contacts the specified
surface region on the object and the giving hand releases
the object. As a result, the giver learns to adjust the object
before passing it, exposing this region for a transfer grasp
rather than a hold grasp (Fig. 5). We distill the experts into
one policy using behavior cloning and RL, after fine-tuning
each expert from states reached by preceding experts. During
distillation, the policy is trained on both individual expert
behaviors and their transitions (Sec. III-C).
Our policy can be prompted with objects: we specify how
the objects should move, and the policy controls the arms and
hands to carry out that motion without task-specific training.
An operator can provide these prompts by moving virtual
objects in VR. We also extract object trajectories from RGB-
D human demonstrations using FoundationPose [4] and use
them as pose waypoints. We evaluate barcode scanning in
simulation with unseen object shapes. On real hardware, we
use object-level teleoperation to test unseen objects on four
tasks: barcode scanning, peg insertion, cleaning, and cooking
(see Fig. 1). We also compare expert-first and end-to-end
training and ablate expert refinement, the distillation loss, and
the training scenes (Sec. IV).
Contributions.
• We design training environments that use object-goal
sampling and workspace constraints to learn single-arm
control and bimanual handover under a shared object-
centric reward, rather than separate rewards for each
behavior.
• We show that pickup, in-hand adjustment, regrasping,
and handover emerge without hand-designed motion
sequences. Before handover, the giver learns transfer
grasps that expose the object region the receiver needs
to grasp.
• We prompt one bimanual policy with object motion to
execute new tasks without task-specific training. We
demonstrate this with unseen real objects in barcode
scanning, peg insertion, cleaning, and cooking.
II. RELATED WORK
Bimanual Dexterous Manipulation. Bimanual systems
with multifingered hands have demonstrated coordinated
behaviors such as twisting lids, tool use, and passing objects
between hands [5]–[9]. Reinforcement learning systems
specify each behavior through task-specific rewards or staged
sub-goals [5]–[7], while imitation systems specify it through
demonstrations collected per task [8]–[11]. In both cases the
specification includes which hand acts and when the object
changes hands, so a new behavior requires new reward terms
or new demonstrations. We instead use a single object-centric
reward and vary only the goal distribution and the workspace
constraints used during training.
Object-Centric Task Specification. A second line of
work specifies manipulation through object-centric task spec-
ification. In-hand reorientation reaches 6-DoF object pose
goals with a single hand [2], [12]–[15], and recent methods
use object trajectories as the learning signal for dexterous
policies [16]–[18]. SimToolReal [1] trains a single-arm policy
to move procedurally generated objects to random goal poses
and transfers zero-shot to unseen real tools, so tasks become
sequences of object goal poses. These formulations assume
one hand and one workspace. With two hands, pose error
alone does not distinguish a handover from a single-hand
carry, and the carry is available whenever the goal is reachable;
SimDuoReal makes the transfer the only strategy that reaches
the goal.
Composing Specialists into One Policy. Training spe-
cialists and compressing them into one network is common
practice: teacher–student distillation folds privileged or terrain-
specific policies into a single deployable one [19]–[22],
and skill-chaining work observes that a policy degrades on
the initiation states its predecessor actually produces, and
regularizes terminal states to close that gap [23], [24]. These
pipelines still define each skill by its own reward or reference

III. METHOD
We propose SimDuoReal, a single bimanual policy for
manipulating one or two objects toward desired 6-DoF poses
(Fig. 2). Through this common goal interface, SimDuoReal
acquires dexterous behaviors such as grasping, in-hand
adjustment and handover, which can be composed to execute
downstream tasks such as coordinated tool use. Our key
idea is that these behaviors can emerge from a shared object-
centric formulation by varying only the training environments
(Sec. III-B). We first train three workspace-conditioned
experts for within-workspace control and cross-workspace
transfer. We then refine these experts at transition boundaries
and distill them into a unified policy using DAgger and
reinforcement learning in scenes spanning individual expert
regimes and their sequential or concurrent composition
(Secs. III-C). At deployment, the unified policy tracks object
trajectories obtained from VR or human video (Secs. III-D),
enabling diverse zero-shot bimanul tool manipulation.

C. Distilling Workspace Experts into a Unified Policy
We combine the three experts’ complementary behaviors
in a unified bimanual policy πU, using the same object-
centric observations and action space as πH. To meet the
requirements of diverse downstream tasks, we design three


training scene distributions covering expert behaviors and their
composition, with routing rules that select the appropriate
experts. Since switching experts can expose them to unfamiliar
states, we first refine them on transition states collected
in end-to-end cross-workspace scenes. The refined experts
then supervise πU through DAgger, alongside reinforcement
learning.
1) Training Scenes and Expert Routing:
a) Training Scenes: All scenes contain two objects,
initially one in each workspace. For objects not assigned for
manipulation, we set their goals to their current poses. At
each episode reset, we sample one of the following three
scene types with probabilities 0.4, 0.4, and 0.2, respectively:
Cross-workspace expert scenes begin with one grasped
object whose goals shift between workspaces, following πH’s
training setup (Sec. III-B.1.b). The other object rests on the
table, unassigned for manipulation.
End-to-end cross-workspace scenes begin with both
objects resting on the table. One is randomly selected
for source-side pickup and manipulation, cross-workspace
transfer, and destination-side manipulation. Goals follow the
cross-workspace schedule (Sec. III-B.1), with Nshift sampled
uniformly from {3, . . . , 7} at each reset and Ndst = 2. The
other object remains unassigned for manipulation.
Concurrent manipulation scenes begin with both objects
resting on the table. Both arms manipulate simultaneously,
each acting on the object in its own workspace. Each object’s
goal advances independently upon completion.
b) Expert Routing: For each object assigned for manip-
ulation, we query the corresponding workspace expert, πL
or πR, when its current and goal positions lie in the same
workspace, and πH when a grasped object must reach a goal
in the other workspace. This rule switches teachers as end-to-
end scenes alternate between within-workspace manipulation
and cross-workspace transfer. For concurrent manipulation,
we combine the arm-specific actions from πL and πR into a
bimanual action. Expert routing is used only during training
and is not exposed to πU.
2) Transition-Aware Expert Refinement: During rollouts
in end-to-end cross-workspace scenes, we observe excessive
and unstable adjustments when experts encounter unfamiliar
states generated by their predecessors. We collect the states
at expert transitions into a pool. For each target expert, we
sample transition states from its valid predecessors to initialize
episodes in its own training scenes. This adapts each expert to
states encountered at behavior transitions. The refined experts
serve as teachers for distillation.
3) Distillation with DAgger and RL: During student
rollouts, DAgger queries the routed teachers on states visited
by πU. We optimize

D. Task Generalization through Object Pose Commands
At deployment, tasks are specified by object-pose goals
that describe the desired object motion. Given object assets
and contact regions, our unified command interface accepts
these goals from diverse sources. We demonstrate two: 1)
VR: An operator moves virtual objects using a Meta Quest
3, streaming poses at 60 Hz to update the active goals. 2)
Human Video: We use FoundationPose [4] to extract object
trajectories from human demonstrations and downsample
them into waypoints τ j = { ˆT j

A), after which the policy advances to the next waypoint.
The interface supports tasks involving one or two objects.
For single-object scenes, we complete the observation with a
dummy object whose goal is set to its current pose. For two-
object waypoint trajectories, goals advance independently for
concurrent manipulation or synchronously for paired motions
such as alignment and insertion.
The unified policy executes these object-pose commands
through pre-grasp manipulation, grasping, in-hand adjustment,
and handover, enabling zero-shot execution of diverse tasks
without task-specific fine-tuning or robot-level commands.

SimDuoReal policy on four real-world tasks without task-
specific fine-tuning. Together, these tasks cover comple-
mentary dimensions of bimanual dexterity, including object
handover, coordinated manipulation of two objects, and
precise object alignment. We conduct 20 trials per task using
unseen objects initialized at randomized poses. When both
objects initialized in same workspace, handover is required.

between barcode and scanner. A trial succeeds when the
scanner detects the barcode.

C. Simulation Analysis and Ablation Study
We use barcode scanning as a controlled simulation
benchmark (Fig. 4c) that follows rule-based trajectories
that interpolates position and orientation linearly, because
successful execution requires grasping an initially ungrasped
object, reorienting it toward the scanner, transferring it to
scanner workspace when necessary, and precisely reaching
the final scan pose. We evaluate each method over 3,072 envs
each with distinct unseen primitive objects and randomized
initial poses across bimanual workspace. Each env contains
K = 7 task waypoints. We report three metrics: success rate
(SR), the percentage of episodes in which the policy reaches
the final task goal; task progress (Prog.), the percentage of
waypoints reached in each episode, averaged over all episodes;
and completion time (Time), the elapsed simulator time from
episode initialization to the final goal, reported as mean and
standard deviation over successful episodes.
1) Why train workspace expert separately: SimDuoReal
separately trains three expert policies before unifying them.
Fig. 6 compares cross-workspace expert training with an end-
to-end baseline trained from scratch on all three workspace
regimes, using three seeds and the same algorithm, environ-
ment count, initial-state distribution, and compute budget.
End-to-end training becomes particularly challenging when
Fig. 6: Expert training vs. End-to-End training.
all required behaviors must be learned jointly from scratch.
This approach also limits extensibility, as incorporating new
skills requires retraining on the expanded task distribution. In
contrast, the expert-based approach allows new skills to be
trained independently and integrated into the unified policy
through distillation.
2) Why do we need handover:
We compare with
SimDuoReal teacher w/o cross-workspace expert, which
transfers objects by placing them in the shared workspace for
the other hand to regrasp. In contrast, SimDuoReal directly
coordinates the two hands to transfer the object without
an intermediate placement. Adding the handover expert, by
chaining all three using a workspace-based routing rule,
even without transition-aware refinement (SimDuoReal w/o
transition-aware refinement), raises success to 79.4% and
progress from 79.6% to 88.1%, while reducing completion
time. Without handover, failures are dominated by timeouts
and drops during placement and regrasping, whereas direct
transfer introduces only a small fraction of handover-specific
failures.
3) Why is transition-aware expert refinement needed:
Although rule-based chaining selects the appropriate expert,
it does not ensure that the succeeding expert is prepared for
the state produced by its predecessor. In particular, the hand
configuration produced by one expert may be poorly matched
to the preferred manipulation configuration of the next.
Despite maintaining a high success rate through the experts’
recovery ability and hand-configuration generalization, the
policy often performs excessive in-hand adjustment and
regrasping. We therefore refine each expert on states collected
at transitions from its predecessors, enabling more efficient
and stable transitions between experts. This improves success
from 79.4% to 84.3% and reduces completion time from 9.1 s
to 8.7 s.
4) How effective is the distillation?: Removing cross-
workspace expert scenes (SimDuoReal w/o expert scenes)
reduces success to 68.3%, primarily due to degraded handover
performance, indicating that composition scenes alone cannot
preserve learned transfer behavior. Removing the RL loss
(SimDuoReal w/o RL loss) yields 81.6% success, below
the refined teachers’ 84.3%. Adding RL helps the student
better capture expert behaviors and surpass the teachers by
encouraging preceding behaviors to produce states better
suited to subsequent manipulation. The unified policy achieves
85.0% success.
V. CONCLUSION
We presented SimDuoReal, a bimanual policy that learns
dexterous arm–hand coordination from a shared object-centric
objective rather than behavior-specific rewards. By varying
only the goal-sampling regime under one object-centric
representation and reward, we train two within-workspace
experts and one cross-workspace expert in which grasping,
in-hand adjustment, regrasping, and handover—including the
transfer grasps that expose the region the receiver needs—
emerge without prescribed motion sequences. We then distill
them into a unified policy that is prompted purely with object


motion from VR or human video. The single policy succeeds
in 61 of 80 real-world trials (76.3%) on unseen objects across
handover, two-object coordination, and precise alignment,
and reaches 85.0% in simulation while matching its refined
teachers, suggesting that object-centric task specification
offers a simple and scalable interface that shifts the burden
from designing how the hands should coordinate to specifying
what should happen to the object.
Limitations remain: SimDuoReal requires accurate object
meshes and multi-view pose tracking; its 6-DoF goal interface
does not directly capture force- or timing-dependent behaviors,
motivating richer contact specifications; and goal trajectories
are externally provided rather than generated autonomously
