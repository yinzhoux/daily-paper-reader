# ABSTRACT 

Predictive world models learn to forecast observations, but accurate short-term predictions do not necessarily produce physically coherent behavior over longer horizons. We investigate how incorporating physical structure can strengthen world models while retaining their underlying predictive objectives. Across token-based models of planetary motion and JEPA-style visual models of mechani-cal systems, we study conserved-quantity supervision, symmetry-aware repre-sentations, temporal consistency, and learned structured dynamics. In planetary motion, angular-momentum supervision improves autonomous next-token fore-casting, while the benefits of temporal regularization depend on the accompany-ing physical objective and representation. In visual models, generating rollouts through supervised energy-based dynamics heads produces more faithful trajec-tories and phase-space evolution. Together, these findings highlight the impor-tance of how physical structure participates in prediction: improvements in an auxiliary physical readout or a local acceleration estimate do not automatically translate into more reliable long-horizon behavior. Evaluations across initial con-ditions and physical parameter shifts further reveal where these benefits persist and where generalization remains limited. Our study supports physical structure as a practical means of improving predictive world models and provides a unified framework for understanding its effects on representations, learned transitions, and autonomous rollouts. The central lesson is that effective physical inductive biases should help models both represent physical regularities and use them to generate future states. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

World models used for planning must predict what happens after observations stop. Small errors can accumulate until a pendulum loses its oscillation or a planet leaves its orbit. Can physical structure help a predictive model sustain the motion of a system while relying on its own predictions? We add physical structure in four ways: supervising physical quantities, building relative geometry into the model, encouraging consistent representation changes over time, and learning mechanical equations for forecasting. These choices guide training or forecast generation. Prior work examines what models represent (Li et al., 2023, Mitchell & Krakauer, 2023) and how consistently they behave (Vafa et al., 2024). We ask how physical guidance can improve the motion they generate. We study two model families. A joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) infers motion from images, progressing from oscillating pendulums and springs to damping and coupled double-pendulum motion. For planetary motion, a separate autoregressive transformer receives positions directly and generates coordinates through next-token prediction (NTP). Together, these studies test physical guidance when the model must infer state and when state is observed. Attribution comes from matched comparisons within each family, rather than from comparing the two modalities or architectures directly. These four forms of structure lead to three main findings: 1arXiv:2611.27149v1 [cs.AI] 20 Nov 2026 Preprint • Physical guidance improves generated trajectories. Learned mechanics improves visual rollouts across four systems. Angular-momentum supervision improves planetary forecasts even though its auxiliary head is unused during generation. • Geometry improves forecasting within NTP. A pairwise adapter improves the matched relative-coordinate model while retaining the next-token objective and decoder. • Temporal benefits depend on the representation and task. Controlled comparisons iden-tify gains in forecasting and force learning, including force-direction transfer under eccen-tricity and stellar-mass changes. 

# 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Learning what changes over time. JEPA learns to predict features of observations, with V-JEPA extending this objective to masked video (Assran et al., 2023, Bardes et al., 2024). Temporal objec-tives guide how those features change. NextLat predicts the next latent, while Temporal Difference in Vision (TDV) uses observed image changes to predict representation changes (Teoh et al., 2025, Daithankar et al., 2026). We adapt TDV to planetary motion. Related work introduces spatial smoothness, stability, and temporal locality into GPT-2-scale models through continuous coordinate regression, noisy contexts, and restricted attention (Liu et al., 2026). Our models retain discrete NTP and its causal context. 

Using physics to guide prediction. Physical information can shape a learning signal or the rule that advances state. Equation-residual penalties (Raissi et al., 2019), differentiable solvers (Hos-seini Dashtbayaz et al., 2025), and physical controls (Gillman et al., 2025) offer different ways to make this connection. Hamiltonian and Lagrangian networks learn functions such as energy and derive motion through their derivatives (Greydanus et al., 2019, Cranmer et al., 2020). Dissipative extensions capture energy loss (Sosanya & Greydanus, 2022). Context-sensitive imagination (R¨ oder et al., 2025) and physical-state recovery from predicted features (Rao et al., 2026) also link physical structure to forecasting. We compare physical targets used during NTP training with supervised mechanics used to generate visual rollouts.

Signed planar angular momentum, for example, is conserved in our orbits yet reverses under reflection. We therefore test conservation supervision and geometry separately. 

Testing what the model can do. Inductive-bias probes assess behavior after controlled adaptation (Vafa et al., 2025). We pair that perspective with autonomous forecasting. Forecasting tests gen-erated motion, force adaptation tests learning from limited labels, and force transfer tests reuse on changed orbits. 

Separating structure from supervision. Botev et al. (2021) find that stronger physical priors do not consistently improve latent dynamics. Gruver et al. (2022) show that coordinates and accelera-tion modeling can explain apparent Hamiltonian advantages. Our shared second-order dynamics and RK4 control tests energy parameterization beyond those factors. Its system- and metric-dependent gains accord with Botev’s findings. Image-based mechanics learns through reconstruction (Toth et al., 2020, Zhong & Leonard, 2020), whereas our decoder is state-supervised. Noether Networks learn conservation within prediction (Alet et al., 2021), and graph simulators propagate particle 2Preprint states (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020). Our contribution is controlled evidence separating transi-tion accuracy, conservation and geometric robustness in predictive models. Appendix E includes a Lag-caVAE comparison.

# 5 EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN 

Visual training and evaluation. Each visual system trains on 200 simulated episodes of 150 images ( 64 × 64 pixels), sampled every ∆t = 0 .05 s. Each training window contains two observed frames and the next image as target. The encoder uses four transformer blocks, eight heads, width 256, and 8 × 8 patches. We evaluate the final epoch: 50 for simple pendulum and spring, 60 for damped pendulum, and 120 for double pendulum. The original simple and damped pendulum comparisons have three independently initialized training runs (random seeds 42–44), and spring and double pendulum one (seed 42). Root mean squared error (RMSE) pools 400 future steps (20 s) over 25 held-out initial conditions, excluding initialization. Angular errors take the shortest difference around the circle. Energy RMSE uses the simulator’s energy function. Acceleration RMSE compares velocity changes per unit time. We report means and sample standard deviations across training runs. Appendix A.6 defines drift metrics. 

Planetary training and data. All variants share 100,000 single-planet trajectories and 15,625 AdamW updates at learning rate 6 × 10 −4. Each update combines gradients from four batches of eight trajectories (effective batch size 32). The transformer has 12 layers, 12 attention heads, width 768, and 999 body-coordinate rows of context. Coordinates use 7,000 bins over [−50 , 50] 

AU plus three special tokens. Relative and pairwise models share integer-bin encoding. Auxiliary weights are 0.1. Velocities and masses are saved in physical-state records alongside coordinates to compute targets and evaluation metrics. They are not input tokens. Validation NTP loss selects the checkpoint. The original three independent training runs use random seeds 1337–1339. The adapter increases parameter count from 108.53M to 109.72M. Absolute TDV uses the earlier auxiliary co-ordinate encoding, so its ranking against corrected relative TDV compares complete configurations. Relative-model contrasts hold encoding fixed. Each system draws two independent masses uniformly between 0.0001 and 0.0101 solar masses, designating the heavier body as its star. Orbit size (semimajor axis) is uniform between 0.3 and 32 AU. Elongation (eccentricity) follows a beta distribution with shape parameters 0.867 and 3.03. The cadence token specifies regular sampling every 0.0189 or 0.486 years, shared by both bodies. 

Planetary forecasting and physics. Each model receives 32 frames and generates 128 on 16 held-out systems, choosing the most probable valid coordinate token at each step. Position MSE averages squared errors over future planet coordinates, then systems, excluding the star. Angular-momentum drift measures change from the last observed state, divided by a fixed physical scale from the true initial orbit. Equation 3 defines this scale, including near-zero angular momentum. Finite differences estimate velocities from predicted positions. Table 4 aggregates training runs. Appendix C gives individual scores and paired uncertainty intervals. 

Sparse force adaptation. Each backbone receives a fresh linear force head and 1,000 full-backbone updates using 25 force labels per orbit, 50 for validation, and 424 for testing. Fits use eight named-planet systems at both cadences, with identical label splits and seed 1337. Figure 3 shows Earth at the finer cadence, with each condition fitted separately. Figure 4(a,b) averages all 16 orbits and reuses the nominal fit under both parameter changes without refitting. Appendices C.5 and C.6 report all nine variants. Observed orbital positions are inputs. Force-vector NMSE divides mean squared vector error by mean squared true-force magnitude. 

Additional matched controls. Appendix E extends visual controls to seeds 42–46 and planetary controls to 1337–1341. Decoded heads retain state-supervised observation models. Equal-input planetary controls share observed-prefix velocities and masses. These additions retain all seeds and remain separate from the original protocols. 

Controlled changes. Paired planetary test sets change one parameter, retaining other random draws. Eccentricities 0.45 and 0.75 lie within training support. Tests span 64 frames, with a separate 400-frame test set. Further tests vary stellar mass, body count, order, and coordinates. Multi-planet systems combine independent star-planet orbits. Visual tests vary initial conditions, 6Preprint rendering, lengths, and masses. Changed parameters are withheld from the head. Forecasts use pretrained checkpoints without adaptation. Appendix D gives all scenarios.

# 7 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Match the structure to the prediction task. Visual mechanics generates the next state directly. Planetary supervision and geometry guide the token predictor that generates coordinates. Both re-quire checking trajectories alongside physical scores. Energy can stay constant at the wrong ampli-tude, and a phase portrait can trace the right curve at the wrong speed. Joint trajectory and physical metrics reveal both errors. 

Scope and next steps. Ordered attention and quantization limit end-to-end equivariance. Matched controls distinguish transition-level visual benefits and geometric robustness from added supervision and capacity (Appendix E). Body-count shifts and parameters ambiguous from two images remain difficult. New visual controls use five seeds. Original Relative+TDV force results use one pretrain-ing seed, while five-seed Plain/AM adaptation is mixed. Force direction is mass-independent at fixed position. Further tests must assess mass-scaling of force magnitude and integration error. Training and data efficiency require matched learning curves. 

# 8 CONCLUSION 

When observations stop, a predictive model must sustain motion using what it has learned. Com-pared with the V-JEPA 2 baseline, learned mechanics improves trajectories, acceleration and en-ergy accuracy across four visual systems. With labels and RK4 matched, energy heads improve simple- and double-pendulum motion, while spring and damped-pendulum gains mainly concern 10 Preprint consistency. Angular-momentum supervision improves forecasting and momentum stability over position-input Plain NTP. Equal velocity/mass inputs retain a nominal forecasting benefit, but not improved conservation. Pairwise geometry improves Relative NTP and rotation robustness over a capacity-matched generic adapter. Temporal benefits depend on the task and representation. Phase-space plots and joint trajectory/physical metrics show these effects. Within-family controls distinguish structure from added capacity and supervision. A learned mechanical head improves visual rollout by advancing the decoded state. An auxiliary objective or geometric adapter improves planetary rollout by changing the token predictor itself. Parameter and horizon tests locate where gains persist. The practical lesson is to connect physical guidance to the computation producing future states, then evaluate the motion it generates. Relative and pairwise models use integer-bin displacements and accumulation throughout training and generation. Instantaneous distance occupancy also differs from the semi-major-axis dis-tribution, since eccentric orbits spend unequal time at different radii. Distance-error comparisons therefore require stratifying by cadence and forecast horizon. Controlled eccentricity interventions are reported separately from broader distribution changes.

We evaluate the author Lag-caVAE architecture (Zhong & Leonard, 2020) on simple-pendulum data across five seeds. Its 520,680 parameters train on five-frame windows with the original reconstruc-tion, spherical KL and 0.01 norm penalty. RGB images are averaged to grayscale and area-resized to 32 × 32 . Adam uses learning rate 0.001 without clipping or weight decay. The native Euler solver and inferred coordinates are retained. Train-only circular sign/offset calibration maps latent angles to physical units using angle labels after training, without model updates. Our heads use a state-supervised decoder and RK4. All methods observe two images. This pipeline comparison complements matched-head attribution.
