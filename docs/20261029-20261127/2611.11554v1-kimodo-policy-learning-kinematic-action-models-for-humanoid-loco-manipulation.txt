Abstract.
Humanoid loco-manipulation requires continuous coordination of high-dimensional
whole-body motion to realize task objectives. While vision–language–action models and world action
models transfer semantic and visual-dynamics priors to robot control, these priors do not necessarily
capture the structure of robot motion. We introduce Kinematic Action Models (KAMs), a paradigm
that treats whole-body motion as a direct pretraining target and uses a pretrained motion generator
as the primary action-generation backbone. KAMs ground learned temporal and cross-body coor-
dination priors through task conditioning, visual observations, and execution feedback. We present
Kimodo-Policy as an initial instantiation, combining a frozen Kimodo text-to-motion backbone with
lightweight ControlNet-based visual conditioning. A root–body–hand hierarchy coordinates locomo-
tion and manipulation, while motion-history conditioning and repeated action-chunk prediction bridge
motion synthesis and closed-loop control. Following large-scale loco-manipulation pretraining, the
policy achieves the highest overall success among compared policies on multi-task HumanoidArena
(72.14%), competitive performance on SIMPLE, and 60% mean success across eight real-world tasks,
with 678M parameters (92M trainable). Ablations support the contribution of the pretrained motion
backbone. Together, these results provide initial evidence for motion generation as a complementary
foundation for humanoid action learning.
1 Introduction
Humanoid loco-manipulation integrates task understanding, visual perception, and high-dimensional whole-
body coordination (Hu et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026a). Beyond the requirements of conventional manipulation,
it demands that robots continuously coordinate their root, torso, arms, and legs throughout an interaction (Xue
et al., 2025). For example, reaching for an object while moving requires a robot to simultaneously adjust its
body position, maintain dynamic balance, and coordinate joint movements according to the object’s location
and contact state. Whole-body motion is therefore an integral part of accomplishing the task, rather than
merely an execution stage that follows task-level decision-making. Even with accurate task understanding
and perception, a robot cannot complete the task without generating temporally and spatially coordinated,
executable whole-body motion. The central challenge of loco-manipulation thus extends beyond determining
what the robot should do to generating the high-dimensional, continuous, and coupled whole-body motion
required for task execution.
Recent advances in vision–language–action models (VLAs) and world action models (WAMs) offer two repre-
sentative approaches to robot action generation. VLAs typically extend the semantic understanding and visual
representations acquired by pretrained vision–language models to robot action prediction (Black et al., 2025a;
Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; NVIDIA et al., 2025). WAMs draw on the visual dynamics learned by
pretrained video generation models to connect future-state prediction with action generation (Li et al., 2026d;
Ye et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). These approaches provide important foundations for task understanding,
environmental perception, and interaction planning. However, pretrained knowledge of visual semantics and
dynamics does not necessarily capture the structural regularities of high-dimensional robot motion. Although
these models can acquire whole-body motion capabilities through action supervision and downstream policy
learning (Jiang et al., 2025), translating visual or semantic representations into coordinated whole-body motion
still requires action representations and dependencies that reflect the structure of the robot’s motion space. This
motivates a complementary question: beyond semantic understanding and visual dynamics, can the structure
of whole-body motion itself serve as a distinct target for generative pretraining?
Motivated by this observation, we introduce Kinematic Action Models (KAMs), a paradigm that uses a pre-
trained motion generation model as the primary backbone for action generation (Fig. 2). The central premise
of KAMs is that, for humanoid robots whose behavior is expressed through high-dimensional whole-body
motion, motion should be a direct target of foundation-model pretraining, in addition to being the final out-
put of a policy. Whereas visual and language pretraining primarily captures visual semantics or the evolution
of visual states, motion generation pretraining directly models the temporal evolution of body configurations,
learning patterns of joint motion, root motion, and coordination across body parts (Rempe et al., 2026; Tevet
et al., 2023). This allows the model to acquire structural priors in a motion space closely aligned with the tar-
get action-generation problem, reducing its reliance on downstream task learning to acquire these basic motion

Transferring pretrained motion generation capabilities to robot control nevertheless requires addressing the
gap between motion synthesis and closed-loop interaction. Conventional motion generation models typically
produce coherent motion sequences from text or kinematic constraints (Rempe et al., 2026; Tevet et al., 2023;
Xie et al., 2024), whereas loco-manipulation requires robots to continually revise their subsequent motion
based on current observations and their own states. The challenge is therefore both to acquire priors that
capture whole-body coordination and to preserve these priors while making action generation responsive to
task requirements and environmental changes. Within this framework, the pretrained motion generator provides
reusable whole-body motion structure, perceptual conditioning grounds motion predictions in the current scene
and task, and motion history together with continually updated action predictions allows the generation process
to incorporate execution feedback. By combining motion structure with task conditioning and closed-loop
feedback, KAMs provide a path from general-purpose motion generation to embodied interaction and control.
To investigate this paradigm, we present Kimodo-Policy, an initial instantiation of KAMs for humanoid loco-
manipulation (Fig. 1). We combine Kimodo’s pretrained text-to-motion backbone (Rempe et al., 2026) with a
lightweight ControlNet-based visual adapter (Zhang et al., 2023) to directly examine whether strong motion pri-
ors can support visually conditioned action generation. The motion backbone remains frozen, while the adapter
injects egocentric visual features to guide its predictions. A three-stage root–body–hand architecture first gen-
erates a root trajectory, then produces body joint motion conditioned on that trajectory, and finally predicts
hand control signals from body features and visual context. Large-scale loco-manipulation pretraining adapts
this architecture to robot interaction, while motion-history conditioning and repeated action-chunk prediction
support closed-loop execution through a whole-body tracking controller (Luo et al., 2026). The resulting policy
contains 678 million parameters, of which 92 million are trainable. Across simulation benchmarks and real-
robot tasks, Kimodo-Policy achieves the highest overall success rate among the compared policies on multitask
HumanoidArena (Wang et al., 2026a), remains competitive on SIMPLE (Wei et al., 2026b), and outperforms
the evaluated baselines on real-world loco-manipulation tasks. Ablation studies support the contribution of
the pretrained motion backbone, while comparisons across motion priors show that the choice of pretrained
motion model affects downstream performance. These results provide initial evidence that motion generation
can serve as a practical foundation for humanoid action models. Our main contributions are threefold:
• We introduce Kinematic Action Models (KAMs), a paradigm built on pretrained motion generators. By
making whole-body motion a direct pretraining target, KAMs transfer temporal and cross-body coordination
priors to downstream control, establishing a complementary foundation for humanoid policy learning.
• We develop Kimodo-Policy, an initial KAM instantiation with a frozen motion backbone and lightweight
visual conditioning. Its root–body–hand hierarchy and motion-history conditioning support closed-loop
loco-manipulation in a compact policy with 678M parameters, of which only 92M are trainable.
• We achieve the highest overall success among the compared methods on multitask HumanoidArena (72.14%),
competitive SIMPLE performance, and 60% mean success across eight real-world tasks, outperforming
the evaluated real-world baselines. Ablations substantiate the contribution of pretrained motion priors to
humanoid control.

2 Related Work
2.1 Whole-Body Control for Loco-Manipulation
Humanoid loco-manipulation integrates spatial grounding (Wang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026b) and nav-
igation planning (Wang et al., 2025a,b) with whole-body coordination (Hu et al., 2026; Xue et al., 2025),
enabling robots to approach interaction targets and manipulate objects while maintaining balance. Human-
VLA (Xu et al., 2024) distills a reinforcement-learned teacher into a vision–language policy. OASIS (Yu et al.,
2026) combines simulated teleoperation with visual randomization, while FetchMan (Rayyan et al., 2026) re-
fines policies learned from synthetic demonstrations through reinforcement learning. Foundation policies such
as GR00T N1 (NVIDIA et al., 2025) and Ψ0 (Wei et al., 2026a) transfer pretrained visual–semantic represen-
tations to robot action experts. LeVERB (Xue et al., 2025) and WholeBodyVLA (Jiang et al., 2025) further
investigate learned latent interfaces and loco-manipulation-oriented controllers that connect task understand-
ing to whole-body execution. These developments highlight the complementary roles of perceptual general-
ization, interaction data, and the control interface. World action models (WAMs) additionally use future visual
or latent-state prediction to inform action learning. MotionWAM (Zheng et al., 2026) conditions whole-body
action generation on intermediate video-denoising features, whereas the WBC-grounded WholeBodyWAM of
Li et al. (2026d) adapts pretrained world–action priors through a unified controller interface and coordination-
aware attention. More recent formulations address distinct aspects of predictive control: 𝜔-0 (Li et al., 2026c)
couples latent visual prediction with whole-body action generation, HWAM (Yang et al., 2026b) jointly pre-
dicts reference actions and their realized body states, and Language-Forcing (Li et al., 2026b) aligns predictive
visual features with task-relevant language supervision. These approaches strengthen semantic grounding, pre-
dictive modeling, and execution awareness. However, pretraining on visual semantics or dynamics does not
by itself provide a generative model of the coupled kinematics required for whole-body control. KAMs ad-
dress this gap by making whole-body motion a direct target of generative pretraining and reusing the resulting
generator as the action backbone. Perception then conditions an existing model of temporal and cross-body
coordination, making coordinated movement a transferable foundation for humanoid policy learning.
2.2 Motion Priors for Loco-Manipulation
Human motion provides supervision for coordination beyond what is available in costly robot demonstrations.
Humanoid-VLA (Ding et al., 2025) first aligns language and motion within an autoregressive language model,
then incorporates egocentric visual context; ZeroWBC (Yang et al., 2026a) adapts a VLM with image–text–
motion data and executes the generated human motion through retargeting and interactive tracking. Predictive
models offer another route. Being-M0.7 (Yue et al., 2026) learns a joint visual–motion prior from paired and
unpaired human data, adapts it to robot trajectories, and conditions a separate action expert on its predictive
representations. The motion-prior-based WholeBodyWAM of Zhang et al. (2026a) first pretrains a motion
expert on heterogeneous whole-body trajectories, then integrates video, motion, and action experts through
joint post-training. These methods demonstrate the value of learning movement structure before target-task
adaptation and of connecting that structure to visual context. The key distinction is how the learned motion
prior enters action generation. Language-model-based approaches learn motion within a semantic backbone,
while predictive-expert architectures align motion representations with a separate robot-action stream. KAMs
reuse the pretrained motion generator itself as the primary action backbone: the model pretrained to generate
coordinated movement also produces the kinematic sequences supplied to control. Visual observations, task
information, and execution history condition this generative process, which accommodates different motion
generators and perceptual encoders, including VLMs. This direct reuse allows robot-interaction learning to
focus on grounding pretrained movement capabilities in the scene and task, with temporal and cross-body
coordination already encoded in the backbone.
2.3 Motion Generation
Motion generation extends beyond text-conditioned synthesis to action-conditioned animation, temporal com-
pletion, spatial control, and interaction with scenes and objects. ACTOR (Petrovich et al., 2021) learns an
action-conditioned variational motion representation; MDM (Tevet et al., 2023) and MLD (Chen et al., 2023)
model motion with diffusion in pose and latent spaces, respectively. MoMask (Guo et al., 2024) generates dis-

crete motion tokens through masked modeling, while DartControl (Zhao et al., 2025a) autoregressively com-
poses diffusion-based motion primitives for online control. Controllability is developed along complementary
axes: OmniControl (Xie et al., 2024) constrains arbitrary joints over time, CHOIS (Li et al., 2024) jointly syn-
thesizes human and object motion under language and waypoint conditions, and MaskedMimic (Tessler et al.,
2024) unifies physics-based character control through motion inpainting with partial motion, text, and scene
inputs. Together, these approaches show that motion models can encode reusable movement structure while re-
sponding to diverse conditioning signals. Kimodo (Rempe et al., 2026) advances this direction with large-scale
motion-capture pretraining, a root–body diffusion hierarchy, and flexible constraints including keyframes, joint
targets, and paths. Its released human and humanoid variants make controllable kinematic generation directly
relevant to robot action modeling. However, controllable motion synthesis alone does not specify how a robot
should revise its actions as the observed scene and executed motion change. Kimodo-Policy addresses this
gap by coupling Kimodo’s frozen generator with visual adaptation, coordinated hand prediction, and motion-
history-conditioned replanning. Each policy query grounds future action chunks in current observations and
anchors them to the robot’s executed motion. Motion generation thereby becomes the mechanism for con-
tinually producing and revising robot actions, establishing a concrete KAM instantiation that preserves the
pretrained backbone while learning its adaptation to interaction.
3 Method: Kimodo-Policy
This section presents Kimodo-Policy, an instantiation of KAMs for closed-loop humanoid loco-manipulation.
We first introduce the pretrained motion backbone and visual conditioning mechanism (Sec. 3.1), then de-
scribe the root–body–hand architecture (Sec. 3.2). We next explain how motion-history conditioning adapts
motion generation to closed-loop interaction (Sec. 3.3), and conclude with the training objective and inference
procedure (Sec. 3.4).
3.1 Preliminaries
Kimodo as a KAM Backbone. Kimodo-Policy instantiates Kinematic Action Models (KAMs) by using a
pretrained motion generator as the backbone for humanoid action generation. We adopt Kimodo-G1 (Rempe
et al., 2026), which generates robot kinematic motion from language instructions and optional motion con-
straints through a hierarchical root–body denoising process. The root stage models global translation and
heading, while the body stage generates articulated motion conditioned on the root prediction. Its motion rep-
resentation jointly describes root motion, joint positions, velocities, orientations, and foot contacts, providing
a structured space for whole-body prediction. Pretraining in this space allows the backbone to acquire tem-
poral and cross-body coordination patterns that can be reused during downstream policy learning. We retain
this pretrained generation structure and keep the backbone frozen, learning additional conditioning modules to
adapt its predictions to robot interaction. Motion generation thus provides the foundation of the policy, while
adapting it to closed-loop control requires grounding its predictions in the current scene and execution state.
ControlNet for Visual Conditioning. To make the pretrained motion generator responsive to the current scene,
we adopt ControlNet-based conditioning (Zhang et al., 2023) for the frozen root and body denoisers. Train-
able control branches process egocentric visual features and inject residual signals into selected layers of the
corresponding denoisers. The injection projections are initialized to zero, so the root–body generator initially
follows its pretrained computation before learning to incorporate visual information. This design separates
the pretrained motion backbone from the parameters learned for visual conditioning, allowing robot demon-
strations to shape scene-dependent motion predictions without updating the backbone weights. Within this
KAM instantiation, the motion generator supplies learned whole-body structure, while ControlNet provides a
trainable interface through which observations guide action generation.

allowing each predicted time step to retrieve task-relevant visual evidence. In parallel, a token-axis MLP maps

HandNet. Hand control has different output semantics from root and body motion: it specifies when each hand
should open or close, rather than a kinematic pose trajectory. We therefore model bilateral hand control with
a dedicated diffusion head. At each denoising step, HandNet jointly processes the current hand state, noisy
future hand controls, future body hidden states, and visual tokens, and predicts two hand-control values for
every future frame. Conditioning on the predicted body and current scene aligns interaction commands with
the evolving whole-body action, while a separate head avoids placing hand labels in the root–body motion
space. The head supports binary open/close states and continuous closure changes. During training, its body
and visual conditioning features are detached, so hand supervision updates HandNet without sending gradients
into the motion denoiser or ControlNet.
3.3 Adapting Motion Priors for Closed-Loop Control
Grounding Language-Aligned Motion Priors. Kimodo’s motion pretraining associates language semantics
with coordinated root and body movements (Rempe et al., 2026), as illustrated by the sequence generated by
the original model in the upper row of Figure 4. This language-aligned motion prior provides the generative
foundation for Kimodo-Policy. Real-world interaction additionally requires the generated motion to account for
the observed scene and the robot’s ongoing execution. We learn visual conditioning from robot demonstrations
to adapt the inherited motion-generation process to the current scene, while motion-history constraints anchor
each prediction to the robot’s executed trajectory. The lower row illustrates the resulting closed-loop policy in
real-world object transfer. Within this KAM instantiation, policy learning therefore centers on grounding an
existing language-aligned motion prior in the environment and execution context, with the pretrained generator
retained as the primary action backbone.

Motion-History Conditioning. Applying Kimodo’s pretrained sequence generator to closed-loop control
requires reconciling coherent motion synthesis with repeated, observation-dependent action selection: the pol-
icy must predict a finite action chunk, execute only part of it, and revise subsequent predictions using updated
observations. We formulate each policy query as constrained motion completion in Kimodo’s kinematic rep-
resentation. The available measured motion history occupies the prefix of a history–future window, and a
feature-wise mask specifies the observed kinematic values. This prefix is restored before each denoising step,
while the unconstrained future is generated as the next action chunk. Trainable ControlNet branches inject
features from the current egocentric image into the frozen root and body denoisers, grounding predictions in
the scene. In the real-world configuration, each query uses up to 100 history frames and predicts 50 future
frames at 30 Hz (approximately 1.67 s); a 15-frame prefix (0.5 s) is executed per update (Sec. 4.2). The re-
sulting measured state history and refreshed image then condition the next prediction, allowing subsequent
chunks to account for execution deviations and environmental changes. The full interaction in Figure 4 there-
fore spans successive prediction–execution updates. This receding-horizon formulation retains the pretrained
motion structure while enabling continual visual and state-based correction.

Inference. At each policy query, we encode the language instruction and current egocentric image, and repre-
sent the available proprioceptive history in Kimodo’s motion space with frame- and feature-wise observation
masks. We initialize the future motion and hand sequences from Gaussian noise and iteratively denoise them
with DDIM (Song et al., 2021). At each step, the observed history is restored, visual features are injected into
the frozen root and body denoisers through ControlNet, the predicted root conditions body generation, and
HandNet predicts hand controls from the current hand state, visual features, and future body features. After
the final denoising step, we invert the motion representation and map the resulting root and joint trajectories,
together with bilateral hand commands, to tracker-compatible references. The low-level whole-body tracker
executes a prefix of the predicted chunk; the policy then incorporates the updated visual observation and pro-
prioceptive state to replan. This receding-horizon loop combines structured motion generation with continual
closed-loop correction.

4.1 Pretraining
Dataset. Here, policy pretraining refers to training the visual conditioning modules and hand head on robot
demonstrations while retaining the frozen, pretrained Kimodo motion backbone. We assemble a 419-hour
pretraining corpus from three publicly released humanoid datasets (Fig. 5). HIW-500 provides teleoperated
Unitree G1 demonstrations collected in real homes. From UniFoLM-WBT, we use the 14 task-specific G1
subsets available when our corpus was assembled: the BrainCo subsets Collect Plates into Dishwasher, Make
the Bed, Pick Up Medicine, and Pickup Pillow; the Dex1 subset Put Clothes into Washing Machine; and the
Inspire subsets Clean the Living Room, Collect Clothes (MainCamOnly), Pick Up Drinks, Pickup Pillow (Main-
CamOnly), Put Clothes into Basket, Put Clothes into Washing Machine, Put Clothes into Washing Machine
(MainCamOnly), Put Drinks into Fridge, and Put Vegetables into Basket. Main-camera-only variants are in-
cluded when available, while UniFoLM-WBT entries released after this snapshot are excluded. Humanoid Ev-
eryday (Zhao et al., 2025b) contributes 10.3K trajectories, over three million frames, and 260 tasks spanning
dexterous manipulation, articulated-object interaction, human–robot interaction, and locomotion-integrated
behaviors. We form training examples from an egocentric image, a language instruction, a 100-frame motion
history, and a 50-frame future action chunk sampled at 30 Hz, converting trajectories to Kimodo’s motion
representation.

taking approximately 60 hours with bfloat16 mixed precision and a global batch size of 1024. During policy
pretraining, hand supervision is represented as a two-dimensional binary vector indicating the open/closed
state of the left and right hands. This shared representation accommodates the different hand morphologies,
degrees of freedom, and control conventions in the source datasets, preserving grasp-and-release semantics
while providing a consistent target for the hand head. We use AdamW with learning rates of 3 × 10−5 for the
ControlNet Transformer layers and 1 × 10−4 for the ControlNet conditioning modules and hand head, together
with a weight decay of 0.01. The learning rate follows a cosine schedule with 1% warm-up and a minimum of
10% of its initial value.

Implementation Details. We fine-tune the Kimodo-Policy variants with bfloat16 precision on four GPUs using
30 Hz motion sequences, a 100-frame history, and a 50-frame prediction horizon. HumanoidArena single-task
policies use a global batch size of 64 for 200k steps, while the joint seven-task policy uses a batch size of
128 for 500k steps; SIMPLE uses a batch size of 64 for 200k steps, and real-world policies use a batch size
of 32 for 50k steps. Variants without and with policy pretraining share the same fine-tuning setup and retain
the same pretrained Kimodo motion backbone; they differ in the initialization of the trainable conditioning
modules and hand head. The four-layer ControlNet detaches the root-control input to body prediction. We
use the objective in Eq. 3, with weights 4, 1, 1, and 0.2 for root motion, body motion, hand control, and
hand transitions, respectively; reconstruction terms use MSE and the hand-transition term uses an ℓ1 loss.
Optimization uses AdamW (weight decay 0.01) and a cosine schedule with 1% warm-up and a 10% minimum
learning-rate ratio. The ControlNet Transformer layers, adapters, and hand head use learning rates of 3×10−5,
1 × 10−4, and 1 × 10−4 for HumanoidArena fine-tuning, and 1 × 10−5, 3 × 10−5, and 3 × 10−5 for SIMPLE
and real-world fine-tuning. SIMPLE training uses color jitter and continuous hand-control targets; real-world
training uses color jitter and random crops, with binary hand commands conditioned on measured hand closure.

We evaluate motion generation as a foundation for humanoid control through benchmark and real-world exper-
iments (Secs. 5.1–5.3), followed by analyses of motion priors, scaling, and adaptation design (Secs. 5.4–5.6).
Unless explicitly ablated, all Kimodo-Policy variants retain the pretrained motion backbone. Kimodo-Policy
denotes direct downstream fine-tuning, while Kimodo-Policy∗additionally initializes the conditioning modules
and hand head through policy pretraining on 419 hours of robot-interaction data.
5.1 Evaluation on the HumanoidArena Benchmark
HumanoidArena (Wang et al., 2026a) comprises seven language-conditioned tasks spanning human–object
interaction (HOI) and human–scene interaction (HSI), with substantial requirements for locomotion, posture
adjustment, and manipulation (Fig. 8). We evaluate both independent task-specific policies and a single policy
jointly trained on all seven tasks. Success rate (SR) measures task completion, while average fall rate (AFR)
characterizes execution stability within the SONIC-based control stack (Luo et al., 2026). Real-time chunking
(RTC) is disabled in our simulation evaluations.

while using approximately 42% fewer trainable parameters. This comparison supports the selected trainable
parameter budget; inference efficiency is not measured in this sweep.
5.6 Ablation Study
We distinguish motion-backbone pretraining from robot-interaction policy pretraining. All variants are trained
jointly on the seven HumanoidArena tasks; Fig. 14 reports mean SR over the three HOI tasks, DoubleDesk,
Football, and P&Pbox. The reference achieves 51.67% with a pretrained, frozen Kimodo backbone and
downstream-trained conditioning modules and HandNet, without additional policy pretraining. Random ini-
tialization (w/o T2M) retains the same Kimodo root–body architecture but randomly initializes and trains its
weights. Unfrozen backbone (w/o Frozen) instead initializes that network from the pretrained checkpoint and
fine-tunes it. Original Kimodo objective (w/o MSE) replaces the root–body MSE objective with component-
weighted Smooth L1 reconstruction and a forward-kinematics consistency term. Without detachment (w/o
Detach) allows body-stage gradients to update the root ControlNet. Each component variant is compared with
the reference, while Kimodo-Policy∗separately evaluates additional policy pretraining. These results charac-
terize HOI performance and are distinct from the seven-task overall scores in Table 2.
Ablation results. Random initialization yields 18.33% SR, compared with 31.67% when the pretrained back-
bone is fine-tuned and 51.67% when it remains frozen. Among the two variants with a trainable motion
backbone, pretrained initialization improves SR by 13.34 percentage points, supporting the value of inher-
ited motion priors in the evaluated setting. Keeping the pretrained backbone frozen provides a further 20.00-
point advantage over fine-tuning it, supporting adaptation through separate conditioning modules. Replacing
the root–body MSE objective with the original Kimodo objective reduces SR to 35.00%, a 16.67-point drop;
this comparison favors the selected reconstruction objective and weighting for downstream policy adaptation.

6 Conclusion
We presented Kinematic Action Models (KAMs), which use a pretrained motion generator as the primary
backbone for action generation, and Kimodo-Policy as an initial instantiation for humanoid loco-manipulation.
Kimodo-Policy retains a frozen motion backbone and learns visual conditioning and hand control around its
root–body generation hierarchy. Motion-history constraints and repeated action-chunk prediction ground this
inherited motion structure in ongoing execution, connecting motion synthesis to closed-loop interaction. Exper-
iments on HumanoidArena, SIMPLE, and eight real-world tasks support the feasibility of this approach with
92M trainable parameters out of 678M total parameters. Ablations support retaining the pretrained motion
backbone, while the motion-prior comparison shows that downstream performance depends on the selected
prior at matched backbone capacity. Additional robot-interaction pretraining benefits joint multi-task train-
ing and real-world deployment, although it does not improve the aggregate single-task HumanoidArena result.
Data and capacity sweeps further show gains from strengthening adaptation around a fixed backbone within
the evaluated range. These findings provide initial evidence for KAM-based humanoid control.
Limitations. A principal limitation of the current framework is its dependence on a specific low-level motion
tracker. Kimodo-Policy is designed and evaluated with SONIC, and its motion-reference interface and deploy-
ment pipeline are tailored to this tracker; other tracking controllers are not currently supported. Consequently,
the reported results establish the effectiveness of the policy within the SONIC-based control stack, while trans-
fer across trackers remains unverified. Although separating high-level motion generation from low-level execu-
tion provides a modular organization, this separation alone does not ensure compatibility with controllers that
differ in reference parameterization, tracking behavior, or executable motion range. Extending the framework
would therefore require adapting the policy–tracker interface and validating closed-loop performance under
the target controller. Developing a more general interface between pretrained motion generation and low-level
whole-body control remains an important direction for broadening its applicability. The present evidence con-
cerns one KAM instantiation; generalization across motion generators and broader interaction settings remains

to be established.
Future Work. A natural extension is to combine the motion-generation backbone with richer semantic and
predictive representations. Vision–language and video models could condition the KAM on task-level context
and anticipated environmental changes while retaining motion generation as the primary action mechanism. A
complementary direction is to improve the motion prior through broader motion coverage and more capable
generators. The current comparison motivates studying this direction, but does not separate the effects of
motion-data size, data composition, and generation quality. Human motion resources offer an additional route
to pretraining, through retargeted robot-space trajectories or transfer from pretrained human motion generators.
Such transfer requires aligning representations across embodiments and accounting for robot-specific actuation,
balance, and contact constraints. Together with a more general policy–tracker interface, these extensions could
broaden the range of closed-loop behaviors supported by KAMs.
