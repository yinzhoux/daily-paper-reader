## Abstract 

Dexterity is, at its core, finger-level control, yet exist-ing dexterous manipulation and human–object interaction (HOI) generation methods rarely let one specify how an interaction is performed: five-fingered hands are often re-duced to grippers, or finger usage is copied from individ-ual human demonstrations. We present FineDex, a frame-work that makes finger-level intent both expressible and ex-ecutable. FineDex learns coordinated human body, hand, and object motions that follow fine-grained instructions on hand selection, individual fingers, and contacts with articu-lated objects. To bridge generation and execution, FineDex leverages generated interactions as actionable priors for an agent that discovers physically feasible contact strategies through practice in a physics-based digital twin. By adapt-ing at test time without per-instance retraining, FineDex generalizes across fine-grained instructions and object con-figurations. Experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art HOI generation quality and fine-grained controllability, substan-tial improvements in dexterous manipulation success, and successful deployment on a real dexterous humanoid robot. 

1arXiv:2611.34884v1 [cs.RO] 25 Nov 2026 1. Introduction 

Dexterity is, at its core, finger-level control: humans press with a fingertip, hook with a specific finger, brace with the palm, and switch hands when one is busy. Yet existing dex-terous manipulation and human–object interaction (HOI) generation methods are evaluated mainly by task comple-tion alone: many effectively use a five-fingered hand as a gripper for pick-and-place [25, 28], while others copy what-ever finger usage appears in a specific human demonstra-tion [7, 24]. Meanwhile, human interaction data offers a scalable source of rich finger-level behaviors. The missing link is a model that can express such intent on demand— which hand, which finger, which contact—and a way to re-alize it on a physical robot. However, existing methods fall short on both fronts. On the expression side, text-conditioned HOI generators syn-thesize coordinated human–object motion [1, 3, 21, 23], but they respond almost only to task-level descriptions: asked to use the left hand or a particular finger, they produce the same interaction regardless. On the realization side, gener-ated motions are not physically exact, and differences in robot kinematics and contact dynamics make direct mo-tion replay unreliable. Existing pipelines bridge this gap by training reference-tracking policies [4, 7], but their re-liance on reference-specific training limits scalability to di-verse fine-grained instructions, object configurations, and robot-specific constraints. Our key insight is to decouple fine-grained intent genera-tion from its physical realization, treating generated HOI as a sketch of the intended interaction. What a human would do is best captured by a generative prior learned from fine-grained human interaction data. How the robot can do it 

is best discovered through test-time practice in a physics-based digital twin. Generation supplies the finger-level in-tent; practice supplies its physical grounding. Building on this observation, we introduce FineDex , a framework that makes finger-level intent both expressible and executable. As shown in Fig. 1, FineDex learns to generate coordinated body, hand, and object motions con-ditioned on fine-grained instructions specifying hand se-lection, individual finger motions, and designated contacts. For execution, FineDex treats generated interactions as ac-tionable priors for an agent that proposes contact programs, practices them under randomized dynamics, and commits to the most reliable behavior, which is then executed on the robot. Because adaptation happens through practice, the same system generalizes to new instructions, robot-specific constraints, and object configurations without retraining. To measure what task success alone cannot, we intro-duce FingerBench, which evaluates whether generated mo-tions follow hand- and finger-level instructions and whether robots realize them in physics-based execution. Experi-ments demonstrate state-of-the-art HOI generation quality and finger-level controllability. In physics-based execu-tion, practice turns generated priors into reliable contact be-haviors where direct replay rarely succeeds while preserv-ing designated finger contacts. On a real Unitree G1 hu-manoid, FineDex completes articulated-object tasks under constraints it was never trained for, such as operating with the left hand only or with a single designated finger. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Finger-level HOI generation. A generator of coordi-nated body, hand, and articulated-object motion that fol-lows fine-grained instructions on hand selection, spe-cific fingers, and designated contacts, with state-of-the-art controllability and quality. • Practice-grounded dexterous execution. An agent that grounds generated interactions through practice, adapt-ing to fine-grained instructions and object configurations without retraining, with deployment on a real humanoid. • FingerBench and fine-grained ARCTIC annotations :a benchmark for evaluating fine-grained instruction fol-lowing in HOI generation and robotic execution, and fine-grained annotations for training instruction-conditioned HOI models, with the latter to be publicly released. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Human–Object Interaction Generation 

Text-conditioned HOI generation synthesizes coordinated hand, body, and object motion. Hand-centric methods gen-erate contact-aware interactions through diffusion [3, 5] or masked autoregressive diffusion [1], while whole-body methods generate human–object motion [13, 14, 21]. HOI-DiNi [23] improves contact realism through diffusion noise optimization, F-HOI [29] aligns fine-grained interaction states with language, and physics-based approaches syn-thesize dexterous grasping and articulated interactions [27, 32, 33]. Despite improved semantic and contact fidelity, instruction-level control over hand selection and individual finger contacts remains underexplored. Our work addresses this gap, evaluated through FingerBench. 

2.2. Learning Dexterity from Human Data 

Human demonstrations provide a rich source of dexterous behavior. Retargeting methods transfer captured or video-derived hand motion to robot hands [15, 19, 20, 22, 24], often refined by residual reinforcement learning [7, 15], and DexMimicGen [12] scales demonstrations through tra-jectory generation. At the humanoid level, motion track-ers reproduce large libraries of reference behaviors [17], and RoboSTAR [31] transfers generated sign-language mo-tion, including fine finger articulation, to humanoid hands without object contact. 2.3. Language-Guided Agentic Manipulation 

Language-model agents plan robot behavior through affordance-grounded skill selection [11], code genera-tion [16], and spatial or keypoint constraints [9, 10]. Simulation-driven approaches synthesize rewards and tasks for policy learning [18, 26], and RAX [8] acquires manip-ulation skills through online exploration with executable robot programs. FineDex connects agentic planning with generative HOI priors.

As shown in Fig. 3, our generator combines language, articulated-object geometry, and anatomical hand features to synthesize coordinated human body, hand, and object motion. We train it on ARCTIC [6] with our fine-grained language annotations, which describe hand roles, finger ac-tions, contact regions, and object-state changes.

Geometry-conditioned diffusion. We learn a conditional diffusion model pϕ(x | ℓ, O, x1: K ), where x1: K are known context frames. Its conditioning memory consists of token-level language features, point features of the sampled ob-ject surface, an object-state token encoding the relative base pose and hinge configuration, and anatomical anchor tokens distributed across the fingers. A Transformer denoiser uses frame queries to attend to these features. At diffusion step 

s, it predicts the clean sequence as a residual to a damped constant-velocity extrapolation of the root, body, and object channels of the context, with finger channels holding the last context pose: 

bx0 = P(x1: K ) + Dϕ(xs, s ; C), (1) where C denotes the conditioning memory. With a sin-gle context frame, P reduces to repeating it. Overlap-ping, context-conditioned windows extend the model to longer interactions. We train with reconstruction, kine-matic, and contact-weighted losses, and sample with DDIM and classifier-free guidance on text, keeping the object and structural controls fixed across guidance branches. 

Hand selection. We separate the task description from structured hand and finger commands. To control the acting hand, we guide each denoising step with low-dimensional, temporally interpolated body-pose offsets. The guidance penalizes displacement of the stationary hand’s anchor cen-troid and wrist, while attracting the acting hand toward an object- and part-dependent contact prototype estimated from training interactions. The offsets are optimized at sparse temporal knots and interpolated across the motion window. The inactive arm joints are additionally fixed to their observed configuration.

3.2. Generation-Guided Agentic Manipulation 

To bridge HOI generation and dexterous robot manipula-tion, FineDex treats generated interactions as actionable pri-ors rather than trajectories to replay. The generated HOI provides hand roles, approach directions, contact configu-rations, and target object articulation, but may not satisfy the robot’s kinematic and physical constraints. We therefore separate instruction constraints from motion guidance: des-ignated hands and fingers restrict admissible contact pro-grams, while the generated HOI guides contact selection and support-hand placement. An agent then adapts contact configurations and execution parameters through test-time practice, without per-instance training. Robot-specific con-straints, such as an unavailable hand, are incorporated into the digital twin. 

Interaction retargeting. We retarget generated hand mo-tion with an interaction mesh over hand and object key-points in the object frame, preserving its Laplacian coor-dinates under joint-limit and non-penetration constraints. Contact programs. We partition the generated object ar-ticulation into S opening or closing segments. A contact program is represented by 

π = ( m, κ 1: S , b, η), (4) where m specifies the manipulating hand and object-motion plan, κs is a part-relative wrist pose and finger configura-tion for segment s, b denotes the supporting-hand behavior, and η contains approach, pressing, timing, and closure pa-rameters. Candidate contacts include those extracted from the generated interaction, geometrically synthesized push or hook contacts, and human grasps from a library built from non-test ARCTIC sequences. Geometric candidates are ranked by their ability to drive the desired hinge mo-tion and their proximity to the generated contact. The HOI prior guides contact selection and support-hand placement without fixing the final contact poses or execution timing. Each program is compiled into wrist and finger targets by smoothly blending free-space motion with object-relative contact configurations initialized from the generated HOI. 

Agent planning and digital-twin practice. We instanti-ate a multimodal planning agent with GPT-6 Astra by de-fault and Claude Fable 5.2 as an alternative (Tab. 3). Given the instruction, object state, rendered HOI keyframes, and structured contact descriptors, the agent proposes candidate contact programs and predicts their outcomes. Each pro-gram is evaluated in parallel digital twins under randomized dynamics. The agent uses task outcomes, contact statistics, and failure renders to diagnose failures and iteratively refine its proposals. Candidates are ranked by empirical success rate, with ties broken by articulation progress, execution penalties, and deviation from the generated HOI prior. The top four goal-reaching candidates undergo additional randomized rehearsals, and the most reliable program π⋆ is selected for execution. The committed program is executed open-loop without online replanning. We evaluate FineDex on floating Wuji hands and a Uni-tree G1 humanoid equipped with Wuji dexterous hands. The selected program is executed using embodiment-specific controllers. 

3.3. FingerBench 

Conventional motion metrics do not assess whether fine-grained instructions are followed. FingerBench evaluates instruction compliance through two tracks. The generation track evaluates hand selection (Switch/Still), finger motion (A1–A5), and finger contact (B1–B4). A1–A5 test single-finger motion, same- and opposite-direction finger motions, bimanual control, and action timing. Success requires the instructed motion while preserving uncommanded fingers. B1–B4 evaluate contact with the reference finger, exclusive contact, counterfactual finger contact, and thumb–index pinching. Success requires the designated contact and cor-responding object articulation, with unintended contacts ex-cluded where specified. The robot track evaluates physical execution using finger-conditioned success rate (FC-SR) and Robo-SR. Both require task completion and sustained contact by the designated finger; FC-SR measures contact dominance by impulse share, while Robo-SR limits the duration of unin-tended contacts by other hand regions.

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented FineDex, a framework that makes finger-level intent both expressible and executable. Trained on our fine-grained annotations, the generator produces coor-dinated human–object motion conditioned on hand, finger, and contact instructions. Rather than directly replaying gen-erated motions, an agent uses them as priors to discover ex-8ecutable contact behaviors through test-time practice. Ex-periments demonstrate state-of-the-art fine-grained control-lability and HOI generation quality, substantial gains in dex-terous manipulation success, and successful execution on a real humanoid robot. We further introduce FingerBench to evaluate instruction following in both generation and phys-ical execution. 

Limitations. Enforcing designated-finger constraints re-duces task success, while limited arm reach and imperfect wrist tracking remain major failure modes on the humanoid. Real-robot behaviors are executed open loop, without on-line feedback; closing this loop is left to future work.
