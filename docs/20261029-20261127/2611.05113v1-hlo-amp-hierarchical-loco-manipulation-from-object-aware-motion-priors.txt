# HLO-AMP: Hierarchical Loco-Manipulation from Object-Aware Motion Priors 

Jean-Pierre Sleiman Bilal Hammoud He Li Jonathan Foster Sylvain Bertrand Jessica K. Hodgins Farbod Farshidian 

Robotics and AI Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA 

Abstract: Hierarchical pipelines for humanoid loco-manipulation typically pair a task-agnostic whole-body controller (WBC) with a downstream task policy. Such WBCs are often trained through manual reward shaping, which yields visibly machine-like motions; more consequentially, they are trained without the manip-ulated object in the scene. Object dynamics are instead approximated through randomized end-effector force disturbances, a proxy that fails to reproduce the dis-tributed multi-contact wrench a real payload applies across the arms and torso, with the gap becoming critical near the platform’s payload limit. In this work, we train the low-level (LL) controller itself as an object-aware motion prior: a single policy trained via motion imitation on retargeted motion-capture (MoCap) references that span the core motion primitives of the downstream task, with the manipulated object physically present in the scene. This single stage inherits natural human-like coordination from the references and absorbs the realistic contact wrench at its source, while exposing the same compact task-space command interface used by command-tracking WBCs. A reference-free high-level (HL) policy is then trained on top, with its action channel anchored to the empirical envelope of the low-level training data so that it generalizes the priors to a long-horizon multi-stage task while structurally preventing queries that would push the low level off-distribution. We instantiate the framework on a Unitree G1 for bimanual box pick-and-place: the same deployed policy generalizes across object masses and dimensions, initial and goal poses, and reactively recovers from intentional drops mid-carry. # 1 Introduction 

General-purpose humanoid platforms are designed to operate in human-centric environments and physically interact with the objects in them. Realizing this promise requires whole-body loco-manipulation, in which a control policy must coordinate locomotion, balance, posture, and contact-rich object handling. When the robot manipulates a payload, especially in dynamic rather than quasi-static tasks where the object’s inertial effects matter, the payload alters the dynamics that govern balance. Locomotion and manipulation are therefore not separable subproblems that can be solved independently and combined afterward. These couplings make whole-body loco-manipulation a central testbed for embodied intelligence on humanoid platforms [1–13]. In this paper, we study one representative and challenging instance: bimanual box transport, in which the robot must approach a box, lift it with two hands, carry it, and place it at a desired location. This task requires the controller to regulate balance under varying inertial loads, sustain bilateral contact over long horizons, and transition smoothly between free locomotion and forceful interaction. Recent learning-based work approaches this problem along two complementary lines: One trains end-to-end whole-body policies to imitate time-indexed retargeted references [4, 7, 14, 15]: the imitation objective implicitly regularizes the policy toward natural-looking motion and removes the need for per-task reward shaping, but behaviors are limited to the reference distribution. The other factorizes the system 

10th Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL 2026), Austin TX, USA. arXiv:2611.05113v1 [cs.RO] 3 Nov 2026 into a task-agnostic command-tracking WBC [8, 11, 16] with a higher-level task policy stacked on top [10, 17, 18]. Such WBCs generalize across a wide command space and admit reuse across tasks, but they are produced through extensive manual reward shaping that yields visibly machine-like motion, and are trained without the manipulated object in the scene. Object dynamics are instead approximated through randomized end-effector force surrogates [11], which cannot accurately capture the distributed multi-contact wrench a real payload applies across the arms and torso. In this work, we present a hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) framework for humanoid loco-manipulation that addresses contact-rich complexity in a bottom-up manner, beginning at the low-level training stage. The LL policy is trained via motion imitation on retargeted MoCap with the object physically in the scene, absorbing the realistic multi-contact wrench at its source and inheriting natural human-like coordination without manual reward shaping. A reference-free HL policy then steers this frozen prior from only a sparse user-supplied goal. We formulate the HL policy’s Markov decision process (MDP) so that its action channel structurally constrains commands to those realizable by the LL policy, avoiding off-distribution inputs that could degrade the frozen prior. We instantiate the framework on a Unitree G1 for bimanual box pick-and-place. A single deployed policy executes the full task with natural-looking whole-body motion, generalizes across box geometries and payload masses up to 7 kg (approaching the platform’s actuation envelope), and reactively recovers from external disturbances such as intentional mid-carry drops. Complementary simulation studies characterize the performance and robustness of our method, compare against end-to-end and hierarchical baselines under similar training conditions, and ablate each core component. We rely on MoCap for object-state feedback and leave on-board perception to future work. The priority here is 

generality, naturalness, and reliability of the whole-body skill before integrating a perception stack. 

# 2 Related Work 

Learning-based whole-body control for humanoids. WBCs for humanoids admit several concrete realizations, differing along two axes: the supervision signal, and whether the controller is holistic or decoupled into upper- and lower-body pairs. A first group is built around imitation of large-scale offline motion datasets. For instance, ExBody [19] adopts a decoupled architecture (upper body tracks reference motion, lower body follows velocity commands), while OmniH2O [20], TWIST2 [21], and CLONE [22] train holistic whole-body trackers over full-body kinematic references through teacher– student distillation. A second group avoids motion data entirely and is trained through manual reward shaping: HOMIE [16] couples height tracking with an upper-body pose curriculum in a decoupled architecture, FALCON [11] factorizes control into a lower-body locomotion agent and an upper-body manipulation agent jointly trained with a force curriculum on randomized end-effector disturbances, and ULC [8] argues against hierarchical decompositions, training a single fine-grained policy that tracks root-level commands, and arm joint positions. Common to these systems, training either ignores the manipulated-object dynamics or uses randomized force disturbances as a surrogate [11]. 

Reusable skill spaces and motion priors. A separate line, originating in physics-based character animation, learns compact latent representations of diverse whole-body behaviors from which downstream policies sample to accelerate task learning, ranging from distilled motor-primitive embeddings [23–25] to adversarially learned skill spaces [26]; the recipe has been carried to real legged platforms as well [27, 28]. As with the WBCs above, the resulting priors do not encode manipulation-induced dynamics, and they additionally introduce a non-trivial and hard-to-interpret latent-to-task conditioning interface that object-aware high-level modules must operate through. 

Learning-based legged loco-manipulation. Legged loco-manipulation has recently been dominated by learning-based approaches, varying along two axes: how the long-horizon objective is shaped, and whether a reusable LL controller is exploited. A first cluster relies on reward shaping: Dao et al. [1] train one policy per stage and stitch them through a behavior graph, while WoCoCo [9] avoids per-stage switching by decomposing long-horizon tasks into sequential contact stages within a single policy. These methods avoid reliance on motion data, but at the cost of meticulous reward engineering, and rarely constrain stylistic quality. A second cluster eliminates much of the reward-engineering burden by tracking pre-computed references from retargeted human MoCap [7, 14], video-captured 2Figure 1: System Overview. A low-level motion-imitation policy πLL is then trained as an object-in-scene prior, conditioned on a noisy task-space command. The frozen πLL is reused by a reference-free high-level policy πHL observing proprioception, box face centers, and a sparse goal, producing commands within the LL dataset envelope and stitching the learned priors into the full behavior for zero-shot deployment. 

human motion [4], or trajectory optimization [15, 29], at the cost of being limited to the reference distribution. A third cluster generalizes beyond reference distributions by stacking a higher-level module on a frozen RL whole-body controller. On humanoids, VIRAL [10] and DoorMan [17] train high-level policies on pretrained WBCs through teacher–student distillation, while DemoHLM [18] relies on demonstration-conditioned behavior cloning; on quadrupedal mobile manipulators, a frozen WBC is reused for large-object non-prehensile manipulation through a sampling-based model-predictive-control (MPC) planner [30] or a constrained-RL high-level policy on top [31]. These hierarchical pipelines can be used broadly but inherit the LL limitations identified above. Bridging work [3, 5, 6] retains motion-prior data while aiming to generalize beyond the reference distribution. PhysHSI [5] relies on adversarial motion priors [32] to encourage stylized motion without strict tracking; VisualMimic [6] employs teacher–student distillation to train a data-driven RL motion tracker with a task-specific downstream motion generator stacked on top; and Pro-HOI [3] adopts an imitation-based low-level policy that uses only the root pose and contact states as reference and relies on a kinematic motion planner to supply the root trajectory required by the task. 

Positioning of our work. Our LL shares the object-tracking objectives of the reference trackers above [4, 7, 14], but is trained as a reusable prior for a reference-free HL policy rather than as a reference tracker, which requires the LL training adaptations of Sec. 3.3. Among the bridging approaches, VisualMimic [6] is the closest, as it also bounds the commands that its downstream motion generator sends to the tracker; however, it relies on a two-stage distilled keypoint tracker, whereas our prior is a single-stage RL policy with a compact WBC-style command. Moreover, beyond bounding the HL commands, we shape the HL objective to keep its behavior within the demonstrated motion manifold (Sec. 3.4), and we target a multi-stage transport task involving large sustained interaction forces rather than individual skills. PhysHSI [5] likewise requires no references at deployment, but its reported results are limited to light boxes and do not include recovery from large disturbances. Our aim is a single deployed policy that combines natural motion, generalization across object masses and dimensions, operation near the platform’s torque envelope, and reactive recovery from large disturbances. 33 Methodology

3.2 Data Collection and Retargeting 

Reference clips are recorded via MoCap of a single human subject manipulating a box, with the box trajectory captured alongside the human motion. The seed library is organized into four primitive families (free locomotion, walking-with-box, box pickup, and box placement). We retarget the clips onto the Unitree G1 via the interaction-mesh formulation [14, 33], which preserves the relative geometry of the agent–object–terrain system rather than matching joints one-to-one. To bridge the human/G1 anthropometry gap, we apply per-segment scaling, chain-rebuild, regrounding, and grasp-contact-preservation corrections. For each manipulation primitive, we augment the dataset to 10 box variants, comprising three base sizes (small, medium, large) and seven rotational variations, each re-computed via the same interaction-preserving optimization to obtain contact-consistent references. Further details can be found in Appendix A. 

3.3 Low-Level Whole-Body Tracking MDP 

Our LL training follows ZEST [34], a proprioception-only motion-imitation recipe that transfers zero-shot to hardware across diverse motion corpora on multiple legged platforms. We adapt it to serve as a frozen LL motion prior for downstream loco-manipulation. Each training episode contains a single primitive from the motion library above, and stitching them into the long-horizon task is deferred entirely to πHL . The adaptations specific to our setting are described below. 

Object-in-scene training. We train πLL to jointly track robot and object references under the resulting interaction forces. Despite the box being in the scene, it is not observed by πLL : the box pose is randomized during training of πHL and at deployment, so feeding it to πLL would push its observation off-distribution. We retain ZEST ’s standard tracking and regularizer terms and add a tracking term and an early-termination predicate on the box state, tying the imitation objective to the manipulated object without requiring it to be observed by the actor. We also adopt the same adaptive-RSI scheme, which biases reset selection toward time bins with higher failure rates; however, the randomization offset around the sampled state is kept small here, both to avoid spawning the robot inside the box and because resets far from the reference would lead to failed episodes.

The box pose representation is given by the relative 3D positions of its six face centers in the torso frame: this avoids orientation singularities, gives the HL policy direct access to contact-relevant geometry, and lets it learn its own low-dimensional representation of object geometry, orientation, proximity, and grasp affordance. The goal g = ( x⋆, y ⋆, z ⋆) is the target box position, expressed in the same torso frame. Both the box face-center positions and the goal vector are hard-clipped per axis at every observation step; distances beyond the clip saturate to the same value, forcing the HL policy to depend on direction and on local geometry once the target enters the reachable regime. As a consequence, the deployed system tolerates arbitrarily large initial robot–box and box–goal offsets despite training under a much narrower randomization range. Because the MoCap system assigns box axes arbitrarily, the six face-center entries are randomly permuted once per episode at reset to decouple input-slot labeling from the physical box pose; orientation randomization would achieve the same invariance but conflicts with the contact-rich RSI states introduced below. 

Phase-conditioned reward. To shape exploration over our long-horizon task, we drive training with a deterministic scheduler over five phases: (0) WALK -TO -BOX , (1) LIFT -BOX , (2) CARRY -TO -GOAL ,(3) PLACE -BOX at g, and an absorbing (4) IDLE . Forward transitions are gated by predicates over physically meaningful quantities (position and heading tolerances, bilateral arm–box contact, and box height). Phase 2 additionally admits a dropped-box fallback that returns the scheduler to phase 0, while the placement predicate sends it to IDLE . Initialization & termination. We apply RSI on the HL side restricted to frames from the box-pickup demonstrations: with some probability the robot and box are jointly reset to a mid-pickup configuration, otherwise the robot is initialized far from the box and must discover the contact configuration through phase 0. This targeted RSI directly exposes the HL to the contact-rich phase-1 states that the staged reward alone is slow to discover; once the value function is well-shaped around these states, the learning signal propagates backward to the approach phase. We retain the LL’s reference-independent early-terminations [34] and add a stage-specific time-out: if the pickup phase is not completed within a fixed time budget, the episode is terminated. This prevents the policy from spending excessive time in uninformative configurations. 

3.5 Training Setup 

Both the LL and HL policies are trained in IsaacLab [35] with 8192 parallel environments at a shared control rate of 50 Hz, and optimized with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [36]. Network archi-tectures and PPO hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix G. Training spans three representative box geometries (small, medium, and large) with masses between 0.5–8.0 kg. Both stages randomize the same set of factors to support sim-to-real transfer (e.g., robot and object mass, friction, PD gains). The HL additionally varies the box’s source and target locations, and the robot’s initial pose relative to the box. Source- and target-side support tables are spawned with asymmetric height and probability ranges; the exact ranges and rationale, together with all domain-randomization details, are given in Appendix H. We adopt an asymmetric actor–critic scheme [37] in both stages: the actor receives only the deployment-time observations described in Sec. 3.3 and Sec. 3.4, while the critic additionally consumes privileged simulator states. Detailed observation lists are provided in Appendix B. 

# 4 Experimental Results 

We evaluate the framework on hardware to demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, generalization across box geometries and masses, recovery from external disturbances, generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, and the natural-looking motion quality inherited from LL training. We then characterize each design choice through baseline comparisons and targeted ablations in simulation, with a full robustness sweep deferred to Appendix L. 

4.1 Hardware Results 

All hardware experiments run on a Unitree G1 with a single policy and no per-trial tuning. The actor receives onboard proprioception and the 6-DoF box pose from an external motion-capture system. Each condition was attempted three times, and all attempts succeeded except at 7 and 8 kg (detailed below). The supplementary video shows one representative take per condition chosen for visual quality rather than as the first successful trial. 

Stress-test pick-and-place. We probe the deployed policy along four dimensions of single-box pick-and-place: Baseline Operation. With a ≈ 0.5 kg box and a single ground goal, the policy completes the full approach–grasp–lift–carry–place sequence; the same transfers to a ≈ 2 kg box. 

Disturbance recovery. We then introduce two disturbances on each box: pushing it mid-carry until it drops from the robot’s grasp, and displacing it from the goal after placement. In both cases, the controller successfully retries and recovers—re-grasping the dropped box in the first case and re-approaching and re-placing it in the second. Heavy payload. 4.2 Simulation-based Evaluation 

We complement the hardware trials with large-scale evaluation in simulation across 10,000 parallel environments, under the same domain randomization applied during training; episodes are 20 s long unless stated, and an episode succeeds if the box is placed within 0.50 m of the goal (the training-time placement predicate, Appendix D). We split success by payload into light (< 4 kg) and heavy (≥ 4 kg), and report the dropped-box rate (fraction of episodes in which bilateral contact is lost at least once) and a naturalness-and-smoothness (N&S) index averaging six lower-is-better motion-quality ratios relative to Ours (Appendix J). 

Performance, recovery, and OOD robustness. Table 1 reports task success as a function of episode budget. The policy completes 80.7 % of episodes at 20 s, with success rising and plateauing past 30 s and heavy boxes consistently trailing light. The dropped-box rate consistently exceeds the failure rate, so most 20 s failures are episodes that would have completed given a longer recovery window; a per-phase breakdown (Appendix K) shows that the carry phase accounts for the largest share of failures and that a 40 s budget more than halves the carry-phase failures. Doubling the initial-pose and goal-placement ranges (last row) costs only completion time, not task success: with a 40 s budget the policy still reaches 82.1 %, within 6 percentage points of the in-distribution result. This follows from the HL observation design, under which OOD box and goal positions saturate onto in-distribution inputs. A complementary three-axis stress test (Appendix L) further characterizes task-success degradation when box mass, external pushes, and box dimensions are each scaled well beyond the training distribution: the policy is least sensitive to external pushes, where stronger pushes mainly increase the dropped-box rate rather than the failure rate; moderately sensitive to payload mass, with the bottleneck being raw payload against the platform’s actuation limits; and most sensitive to box dimensions, since geometry changes the grasp and contact configuration that the face-center observation can only partially absorb. 

Comparison to baselines. We compare against three alternatives (Table 2), all sharing our domain randomization, PPO configuration, compute budget, and task; the WBC baselines also use our command interface and HL training setup, so only the LL differs. Monolithic E2E is a single end-to-end policy without the HL/LL decomposition or motion prior, sharing our HL’s observations and task reward plus upright-torso terms during walking and carrying. The two WBC baselines replace our LL with a command-tracking controller trained on a large human-mocap dataset of walking and reaching with both end-effectors (including the box loco-manipulation trajectories); neither sees the box during training, and the second adds an end-effector force surrogate that randomizes end-effector inertia and applies bounded force/torque disturbances [11]. The monolithic policy effectively fails (3.6 %): it walks to the box (91.6 % reach the lift phase) but never discovers bimanual grasping, and its few “successful” trajectories kick the box along the ground until it reaches the goal. The object-free WBC reaches a moderate overall success, with a striking light/heavy split exposing the cause: a controller that never feels the payload wrench during training destabilizes under load. The force-surrogate variant recovers most of the overall success (77.1 %) but at a 78.0 % dropped-box rate. Component ablations. Table 3 removes one component at a time. Every ablation collapses success from 80.7 % to 3–16 %, confirming no component is redundant. Removing observation noise or the dataset-envelope action clipping breaks the HL →LL interface: the HL emits commands the LL was never trained to track, resulting in the highest OOD-command frequency and N&S index. Removing the object from LL training (even with an EE-force surrogate) or removing the targeted HL RSI leaves locomotion intact but the policy cannot get past the lift phase. Object-in-scene LL training is the single most critical ingredient: the surrogate recovers only to 6.7 %, in stark contrast to the 77.1 % the same surrogate achieves in the comparison above. The reason is that our LL is a compact motion-imitation policy that, without the box, ends up imitating pickup motions with nothing to pick up, whereas the comparison WBC is a broad command-tracking controller that can be driven into a box-holding configuration regardless. Unlike the baselines, the ablation collapses are roughly uniform across light and heavy boxes: these failures are structural rather than load-dependent. 

# 5 Limitations and Future Work 

Three limitations frame the scope of this work and the natural next steps. (i) Perception. The deployed system reads the 6-DoF box pose from an external motion-capture stream, so we have not validated the controller against fully on-board perception. The natural next step is to close the perception loop, for example by using our HL policy as a teacher and distilling it into a depth-based student, while the goal would be tracked in the robot frame via on-board localization. (ii) Motion-library coverage. 

The four primitive families on which the LL motion prior is built limit downstream behaviors: table-to-table transfers beyond the demonstrated lift range, diverse initial grasps, or non-prehensile interactions such as pushing or tumbling, require additional retargeted demonstrations; the HL policy itself cannot extrapolate to them. Porting the framework to other contact-rich loco-manipulation tasks (door opening, non-prehensile object transport) is modest but not automatic: it requires new demonstrations, a phase graph for the new stages, and stage-driven rewards, while the rest of the LL and HL formulation carries over unchanged. (iii) Naturalness. The N&S index is only a proxy for naturalness; a proper user study would be needed to assess it. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We presented a hierarchical RL framework for humanoid loco-manipulation in which a single-stage, object-in-scene LL motion prior is trained and then steered by a reference-free HL policy that requires only a sparse user-supplied goal. This setup absorbs the real distributed multi-contact wrench at its source and inherits natural human-like coordination from retargeted MoCap; the HL MDP is designed to drive the long-horizon task while structurally preserving the LL motion prior. On a Unitree G1, a single deployed policy executes bimanual box transport across box geometries, payloads up to the platform’s actuation envelope, multi-goal and multi-box sequences, and ground-to-table transfers, and reactively recovers from mid-carry pushes and table-edge collisions. 9References

To generate a dataset that generalizes across object sizes, we retarget human MoCap data onto the Unitree G1 humanoid using a custom implementation of the interaction-mesh formulation [14, 33]. The first primitive family of Sec. 3.2 (free locomotion) does not involve the box, so its reference is reused directly from a ZEST [34] retargeted random-walk clip and does not enter the interaction-mesh pipeline. The remaining three primitive families are seeded by three MoCap demonstrations of a subject interacting with a box: • carry: random walk while holding the box at chest height. • pick: bend over and pick up the box from the floor. • place: while carrying the box, bend over and place it on the ground. From these three primitives we produce 30 training clips through a three-stage pipeline: preprocessing the MoCap data to match G1’s anthropometry (Section A.1), running the interaction-mesh solver, and re-solving against varying box geometries to augment the seed set (Section A.2). 

A.1 Mocap Data Scaling 

Since the interaction-mesh formulation preserves relative spatial distances, it is sensitive to several mismatches between source and target. It is sensitive to differences in limb proportions: G1’s limb ratios are not those of the human subject, so no single global scalar can match every segment length at once. It is also sensitive to the coupling between root displacement and leg-swing length: if the pelvis traverses more or less ground than the feet can cover, foot-sliding appears as a pre-retargeting artifact. The last issue is contact preservation at the grasp, where the wrist-to-wrist span shrinks with scaling and pushes the palms inside the object. We account for these by (i) splitting the root scale into a horizontal stature ratio sheight = hr/h h and a vertical leg-chain ratio sleg = ℓr/ℓ h, so the pelvis lands at G1’s natural standing height while horizontal stride remains proportional to overall body size; (ii) rebuilding every kinematic chain as pchild (t) = pparent (t) + Lrobot ˆdhuman (t), with Lrobot 

taken from neutral-pose forward kinematics and ˆdhuman (t) taken from the unscaled mocap so bone directions are not distorted by the per-axis scaling; (iii) regrounding per-frame against the stance toe rather than a global z-min, which pins the supporting foot to the floor through flight phases; and (iv) freezing the hand-box contact points and solving for wrist orientations that minimize the deviation between palm normals and their original grasp axes.

Source- and target-side support tables. Source-side variation is more conservative, with initial heights up to 50 cm and a lower probability of 0.15 : since the HL does not observe the table, a collision with a source-side table edge during the approach or grasp would compromise the rest of the episode, whereas a comparable collision at placement occurs once the box is already near its goal and is therefore far less costly. Such collisions are treated as external disturbances the policy learns to handle. This extension tests whether the HL policy can adapt ground-trained LL primitives to elevated targets without retraining the LL on additional references.

Figure 3 stress-tests the deployed policy along three axes, each scaled well beyond its training range; the y-axis is task success normalized so that the nominal Ours-20 s policy sits at 1. The policy is least 

sensitive to external box pushes: even at 3× the training disturbance magnitude, success holds at 

0.98 of nominal. Stronger pushes mainly translate into more frequent box drops rather than outright failures: the dropped-box rate rises only modestly, from 26.8 % at nominal to 29.1 % at 3×, and the policy could still re-acquire the dropped box and complete the task within the episode budget. It is moderately sensitive to payload mass, degrading smoothly to 0.50 of nominal when the mass range is tripled (mean payload 10.3 kg); the bottleneck here is raw payload against the platform’s actuation limits.
