EffectWAM: Learning Action-Grounded Effects for Robot
Manipulation

Abstract
World–action models connect visual prediction with executable robot control.
We present
EffectWAM, which develops this connection through three complementary components: Effect,
camera positional encoding (CaPE), and Action Grounding. Effect represents visual change as the
difference between independently encoded future observations and a shared current-observation
anchor. CaPE conditions endpoint pooling on relative camera pose when reliable extrinsics are
available, while sharing the same learned parameters with pose-free pooling for uncalibrated trajec-
tories. Action Grounding trains the visual representation to retain measured changes in end-effector
pose and gripper state. An exponential moving average of the observation encoder supplies Effect
targets. A shared generation expert learns to jointly predict Effect and an executable action chunk
through flow matching, and reuses the sampled pair to generate future video on demand. This
allows the controller to execute actions without waiting for video synthesis. We train on more than
20,000 hours of heterogeneous embodied data, and accommodate dataset-specific joint, end-effector,
and hand-pose representations. Our evaluation uses the Small (8B) configuration on all five
benchmarks and additionally evaluates the Large (26B) configuration on RoboDojo. Experiments
examine closed-loop manipulation across complementary benchmarks, and comparisons across
multiple baseline policies show consistent improvements from Action Grounding.

Introduction
Language-conditioned robot manipulation requires a policy to translate a task instruction into a sequence
of actions that brings about the intended change in the environment. In closed-loop execution, the policy
repeatedly observes the scene and adjusts its actions as the task progresses. Anticipating how the scene may
evolve over an action horizon can help the policy connect its immediate commands to the intended outcome.
The choice of what to predict about the future is therefore central to learning visual dynamics for robot
control.
Vision–language–action (VLA) models learn to map observations and instructions to robot actions [1], [2].
Their action supervision leaves the visual consequences of those commands implicit. World–action models
(WAMs) add an explicit objective for predicting visual dynamics [3], [4]. Future video provides a rich training

signal, but synthesizing it at every decision adds latency to closed-loop control. Fast-WAM avoids this cost
by using future video only during training [5]. In matched experiments, LAWA found that this approach
generalized less well than joint video and action prediction, especially with limited robot demonstrations, and
showed that predicting a compact latent alongside actions could achieve comparable success to joint video and
action prediction on RoboCasa at lower latency [6]. This motivates a policy that retains a compact prediction
of action consequences at test time to support manipulation performance while keeping video synthesis off the
control path.
The choice of target determines which changes the policy learns to anticipate. La-JEPA, for example, uses
a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder to supervise future prediction alongside robot actions [7], [8]. Frozen video
features inherit the distinctions learned during pretraining. A generic video prediction objective provides
no direct supervision for retaining small changes in gripper opening or end-effector pose, even when those
changes matter for control. Robot demonstrations provide that signal through synchronized images and
measured state changes. We use these measurements to shape the representation that supplies the policy’s
future prediction targets.
To this end, we introduce Effect, a compact representation of visual change over the action horizon that learns
from paired observations and robot measurements in the same demonstration. A shared encoder processes the
current and selected future observations independently. We subtract the current feature from each future
feature so that changes at different horizons are expressed relative to the state from which the robot acts. An
auxiliary head reads out measured changes in end-effector pose and gripper state from the feature difference,
encouraging the encoder to preserve information about the executed motion. An exponential moving average
(EMA) of the encoder supplies the resulting Effect targets during policy training.
Across robot embodiments and camera setups, similar manipulation motions can yield different image changes
because cameras are mounted and move differently. For example, as a wrist camera approaches a drawer, the
handle can shift in the image before the drawer moves. Qwen-RobotManip uses explicit camera geometry to
align visual observations and end-effector motion across heterogeneous robot data [9]. To support transfer
across embodiments and camera setups, we condition Effect’s attention pooling on the camera pose relative to
the current observation when reliable extrinsics are available. This gives the encoder geometric context for
distinguishing changes due to camera motion from changes in the scene. When extrinsics are unavailable, the
same encoder uses pose-free pooling with shared weights, allowing both types of trajectory to contribute to
training.
EffectWAM integrates Effect, CaPE, and Action Grounding through two complementary flow-matching
objectives. The first jointly models Effect and the demonstrated action chunk given the current observation
and instruction. The second models future video latents given the same context, the clean Effect target, and
the demonstrated action. At inference, the policy jointly samples Effect and the action chunk, retaining a
prediction of visual change in each control decision. The controller can execute the sampled chunk as soon as
joint sampling finishes. When a visual forecast is requested, the video mode uses the same Effect–action pair.
We train with more than 20,000 hours of embodied data spanning heterogeneous action representations.
We evaluate the Small (8B) configuration on LIBERO [10], LIBERO-Plus [11], RoboTwin 2.0 [12], Robo-
Casa365 [13], and RoboDojo [14], and additionally evaluate the Large (26B) configuration on RoboDojo. The
experimental comparisons distinguish these configurations and compare Small (8B) with existing policies. We
also examine Action Grounding across multiple baseline models, where it consistently improves performance,
supporting its value beyond a single architecture.
Our contributions are as follows.
• Effect: a compact prediction of visual change. We represent future observations relative to a shared
current-observation anchor and jointly predict the resulting Effect sequence with executable action
chunks. The same Effect–action pair conditions optional video generation, connecting control to visual
forecasting without requiring video synthesis at each decision.
• CaPE: geometry-aware visual transition encoding. We condition endpoint pooling on relative camera pose
to account for viewpoint changes across robot and camera configurations. Calibrated and uncalibrated

trajectories share the same learned pooling parameters, supporting joint learning from heterogeneous
demonstrations.
• Action Grounding: supervision from measured motion. We make end-effector and gripper changes
recoverable from Effect, shaping the visual representation around motion relevant to robot control.
Evaluations across multiple baseline policies show consistent improvements from this objective, indicating
that its benefit extends beyond our complete model.

Related Work
Predictive targets for world-action models. Future video provides a learning signal for control, but different
models use that signal at different points in the policy. Video-first policies infer controls from predicted
frames [15], while DreamZero jointly generates video and actions [3]. Fast-WAM retains video prediction
during training and skips it during action inference [5]. Predicting in a compact feature space offers another
way to use future observations. V-JEPA 2.1 learns dense spatiotemporal features through self-supervised video
pretraining [7]. La-JEPA uses a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 encoder to define the future features that its policy
predicts alongside actions during training [8]. InternW0-∆uses changes in video features more explicitly. Its
Causal Imprint regresses differences between adjacent video latents and aligns intermediate features with
future-video features before conditioning a separate action expert [4]. In each case, the feature space used to
construct the prediction targets shapes which visual changes the policy is trained to anticipate.
Learning representations of visual transitions. Latent-action models learn a representation of the transition
itself. LAPA compresses changes between frame pairs into discrete codes that support future-frame recon-
struction and uses those codes for policy pretraining [16]. Subsequent work uses additional signals to capture
manipulation-relevant information. WALA learns latent actions by predicting current-anchored changes in
pretrained semantic features and estimated depth, then uses its pretrained latent-action encoder to supervise
the policy [17]. LAWA uses a tokenizer trained with next-frame reconstruction and manipulation-region masks,
then jointly denoises a continuous latent and executable actions [6]. Robot measurements can also shape the
representation. WLA3 encodes synchronized views together with available embodiment-state changes and
learns through multimodal reconstruction and consistency across overlapping windows [18]. In our method,
end-effector and gripper measurements supervise an auxiliary readout from visual feature differences. The
observation encoder learns during policy training, and its EMA copy supplies the Effect targets. The resulting
Effect is jointly predicted with actions and can also condition a video forecast together with the sampled
action chunk. We treat Action Grounding as a distinct supervision mechanism and evaluate its contribution
across multiple baseline policies, in addition to evaluating the complete model.
Camera geometry in visual transition encoding. A visual transition can reflect both physical interaction
and a change in viewpoint. EscherNet introduces camera positional encoding (CaPE) to express relative
camera pose in attention, and PRoPE extends relative camera encoding to include camera intrinsics and
extrinsics [19], [20]. We use CaPE to provide the Effect encoder with explicit viewpoint information when it
pools each endpoint. We express available camera poses relative to the current observation and use the same
pooling weights for trajectories with and without reliable extrinsics.

3.2
Architecture Overview
EffectWAM builds a common observation–language context for both factors in Eq. (1), linking joint Effect–
action prediction with conditional video generation (Fig. 1). The task instruction is embedded as text tokens,
and a trainable vision transformer (ViT) maps the available current camera views to merged image tokens.
SEM receives both sets of tokens, with intermediate visual features injected into early SEM blocks. In parallel,
the frozen video VAE encodes the current observation alone. A learned projection maps its latent to visual
tokens for GEN.
The mixture-of-transformers (MoT) uses masked self-attention to couple SEM and GEN. GEN shares its
transformer backbone across Effect, action, and video, with modality-specific input and output projections. It
first samples the Effect and action streams jointly. The optional video pass reuses the sampled pair and the
original observation context, as detailed in Sec. 3.5. During training, the ViT also supplies pre-merger patch
features to the observation endpoint encoder in Sec. 3.3. The component parameter counts for the Small (8B)
and Large (26B) configurations are reported in Table 3.

3.5
Closed-Loop Inference and On-Demand Video Generation
At each decision time, the policy constructs C from the latest RGB observations, the task instruction, the
action interface, and the requested prediction horizons. The ViT-derived semantic features and the frozen
VAE latent of the current observation provide the visual context for sampling. The observation endpoint
encoder, physical readout, and EMA target encoder are used during training. At inference, GEN samples the
Effect sequence and action chunk jointly from the current context, as shown in Fig. 1.
In the joint Effect–action mode, we initialize the Effect and action variables with independent Gaussian
noise and treat them as one state sσ = (Eσ, Aσ) on a shared flow time grid. At each solver evaluation, the
generation expert reads both noisy streams and predicts their velocities together,

For closed-loop control, we denormalize the valid coordinates of ˆA and convert them to the command

for execution as soon as its integration completes.

starts from an independent video noise sample. The same generation expert integrates the video velocity
to obtain ˆY , and the frozen VAE decodes the current and predicted future latents into RGB frames. The
forecast describes the sampled action chunk from its original observation, including the planned suffix if the
controller executes only a prefix before replanning. This procedure keeps video generation optional and ties
the forecast to the same action proposal used for control.

4.2
Training Process
Training follows the objectives in Sec. 3.4, combining the supervision available in each trajectory. The current
camera observations, task instruction, action interface, and prediction horizons define the conditioning context.
The trainable ViT supplies visual features to the semantic expert, while the frozen VAE separately encodes
the current observation to provide the observed visual latents.
The prediction targets are constructed from the same demonstration. The EMA branch encodes the current
and selected future observations independently through the pre-merger ViT and observation endpoint encoder.
Future endpoints are compared with their shared current endpoint to form detached Effect targets, as defined
in Eq. (4). The frozen VAE encodes the demonstration clip to supply future-video targets. The current visual
context remains distinct from these future prediction targets.
The shared generation expert is trained through two forward modes within the same optimization window.
Joint Effect–action training uses a shared flow time and independent noise for Effect and actions. Video
training noises the future-video latent while conditioning on the clean EMA Effect target and demonstrated
action chunk. Both modes share generation parameters and optimize their corresponding velocity-prediction
objectives.
Each mode requires the annotations and conditioning variables specified in Table 1. Available physical
measurements supervise Action Grounding through changes in end-effector pose and gripper state over the
prediction interval. Reliable camera extrinsics enable CaPE-conditioned endpoint pooling; uncalibrated
trajectories use the pose-free route with the same learned pooling parameters. Observation consistency
and intermediate Effect alignment provide the additional supervision defined in Sec. 3.4. Terms requiring
unavailable annotations are excluded. Visual-only recordings contribute the applicable visual objectives
without supplying robot-action labels.
Following training on the multi-source data, the model adapts to each benchmark’s demonstrations using
the corresponding action interface. This preserves the source-specific meaning of action annotations while
applying the shared training objectives.

Experiments
We evaluate EffectWAM on five manipulation benchmarks to study task execution, robustness, compositional
generalization, and capability-specific behavior. The comparisons examine task success relative to InternW0-
∆[4], robustness to environmental perturbations, and generalization to unfamiliar task combinations.
5.1
Experimental Setup
We evaluate two configurations, Small (8B) and Large (26B), with component sizes listed in Table 3. Small
is evaluated on all five benchmarks, and Large is additionally evaluated on RoboDojo. The two RoboDojo
configurations are compared with baselines of similar model size in separate tables.
Section 5.2 evaluates standard task execution and robustness to changes in observations, robot initial states,
and scene configuration. LIBERO measures task performance relative to InternW0-∆, while LIBERO-Plus and
the Clean/Randomized comparison on RoboTwin 2.0 assess performance under distribution shifts. Section 5.3
examines whether the gains extend beyond atomic tasks to seen and unseen combinations on RoboCasa365.
RoboDojo complements these comparisons with a capability-level analysis, using success rates and partial-credit
task scores to characterize full-task completion and intermediate progress across five capability dimensions.
On LIBERO and RoboCasa365, we evaluate InternW0-∆(8B) and EffectWAM using the same task sets and
episode budgets. EffectWAM uses only Clean demonstrations for RoboTwin 2.0 and only Human300 for
RoboCasa365, without Target training data. The RoboTwin comparison is restricted to policies adapted using
only Clean demonstrations from this benchmark. Model sizes are approximate and include frozen conditioning
encoders but exclude VAEs.
We follow each benchmark’s aggregation protocol. LIBERO-Plus and RoboCasa365 aggregate success over
evaluation episodes, RoboTwin 2.0 weights Clean and Randomized success equally, and RoboDojo weights its
five capability dimensions equally. Success rates are reported as percentages; RoboDojo additionally reports
partial-credit task scores; Small is evaluated with one seed, while Large reports variation across three seeds.
5.2
Task Performance and Robustness
Standard task execution. We use standard LIBERO to establish task execution performance before introducing
distribution shifts. We evaluate EffectWAM and InternW0-∆across four suites with 10 tasks per suite and
50 episodes per task, totaling 2,000 episodes per model. EffectWAM Small achieves 99.20% overall success,
compared with 99.00% for InternW0-∆(Table 4). The comparison spans supervised policies and methods
with additional post-training, providing a broader reference for standard task execution.

480 Composite-Seen, and 480 Composite-Unseen episodes. InternW0-∆uses the same task grouping and

not used. Overall success is weighted by the number of episodes in each split. Table 7 compares performance
on the three splits and their episode-weighted overall success.
EffectWAM improves success across all three splits, with 739 successful episodes compared with 725 for

6
Limitations and Conclusion
Limitations. Performance remains uneven across evaluation conditions. The decrease under RoboTwin
randomization, the low success on unseen Robocasa365 compositions, and the difficulty of RoboDojo’s Open
tasks identify robustness and generalization as continuing challenges. On long-horizon RoboDojo tasks, the
gap between partial-credit scores and full-task success further highlights the need to sustain useful behavior
through task completion.
The method also depends on the availability of geometric and physical supervision. CaPE requires reliable
camera extrinsics; uncalibrated trajectories use the shared pose-free route. Action Grounding supervises only
available, consistently aligned measurements, limiting its coverage of physical quantities across heterogeneous
data sources. At inference, optional video generation keeps video synthesis outside the control path, but joint
Effect–action sampling still incurs computational cost. Prediction horizons remain configured inputs and do
not automatically adapt to subgoal duration.
Conclusion. We presented EffectWAM, a world–action model that connects visual change prediction with
executable robot control through Effect, CaPE, and Action Grounding. Effect encodes visual change as
endpoint differences relative to a shared current-observation anchor. CaPE incorporates relative camera
geometry into endpoint pooling when calibration is available, while Action Grounding aligns the representation
with measured motion. A shared generation expert jointly predicts Effect and actions through flow matching
and reuses the sampled pair for optional video forecasting. Training on more than 20,000 hours of heterogeneous
embodied data brings these components together across robot and human demonstrations with different action
representations.
Evaluation across five manipulation benchmarks demonstrates strong standard-task performance and improved
success over the original policy on Robocasa365, with the largest gain on unseen compositions. Cross-baseline
evaluations further indicate that Action Grounding improves performance across the evaluated baseline
policies, supporting its role as a transferable training signal. The remaining sensitivity to environmental
variation and difficulty in completing composite tasks motivate further work on robust physical grounding,
adaptive action horizons, and more efficient joint sampling.
