Abstract. Whole-body humanoid manipulation remains bottlenecked by high-dimensional state-action spaces, contact-
rich environments, and long task horizons. This trilemma is a challenge that state-of-the-art reinforcement learning
(RL) methods are yet to resolve reliably. We address it with three contributions that add just enough structure to
guide exploration: Sparse Interaction Edges (SIE) for whole-body manipulation, Interaction Gated Transport (IGT) for
sustained transport over long horizons, and a Hinted Object Path (HOP) that resolves collision and search complexity
bottlenecks. Across all three, the policy remains free to discover its own manipulation strategy. The method generalises
from rigid object to human transport. As a flagship demonstration, we show, to our knowledge, the first bed-to-wheelchair
transfer of a medical mannequin by a humanoid robot completed with no human intervention during execution. In
the domain of human transfer, we show that a single policy can adapt across substantial variations in body mass,
size, and random force perturbations, all while training on a single GPU in under an hour. Our approach offers a
pathway to fully RL-driven humanoid manipulation, enabling assistive robots that can help humans. Website: https:
//elle-miller.github.io/into_my_arms/
Introduction
A robotic agent trained via reinforcement learning (RL) in sim-
ulation is free to exploit an embodiment that differs markedly
from our own, discover non-obvious strategies, and acquire
closed-loop policies that remain robust under substantial scene
variation and perturbation. Within this paradigm, RL agents
have achieved superhuman feats in high-speed drone racing [2],
quadrupedal parkour [3], and blind in-hand manipulation [4].
Yet, fully RL-driven humanoid manipulation has not seen the
same gains. Most humanoid manipulation work instead inte-
grates RL with motion priors or imitation learning, enabling
humanoids to lift medium-sized rigid objects [5–8]. Under
pure RL, results remain restricted to bi-manual handovers of
small rigid objects [9] or end-effector-driven box-transfer [10].
We attribute this gap to three intersecting bottlenecks. (1)
High state-action dimensionality from coupled robot-object
dynamics: While locomotive agents (drones, quadrupeds) oper-
ate with 6–12 degrees of freedom (DoFs) to move themselves,
humanoids designed for manipulation often possess 30 or more
DoFs and must additionally control the DoFs of an external
object. Each DoF exponentially expands the search space and
the exploration burden. (2) Discontinuous optimisation land-
scapes from contact-rich interaction: Standard entropy-based
exploration relies on isotropic Gaussian sampling in joint space.
In free space, this yields continuous feedback; under tight inter-
action bounds, the same perturbations instead cause collisions
or invalid configurations, making the reward landscape difficult
to optimise. (3) Long, acyclic task horizons: Cyclic tasks (e.g.,
locomotion or in-hand rotation) permit rapid credit assignment
over repeated motion primitives. Longer-horizon tasks can
instead require non-repetitive sequences of distinct phases (e.g.,
lifting, transporting, placing), each demanding different skills.
State-of-the-art RL approaches can sometimes address one
or two of these challenges in isolation, but can fail when con-
fronted with this trilemma simultaneously. Closing the gap
requires methods that remain general-purpose, rather than be-
ing engineered for a single task. We ground our contribution
in a task that embodies all three bottlenecks at once and which
carries substantial real-world value: autonomous human bed-
to-wheelchair transfer (Figure 1). Physical caregiving is an
increasingly urgent application for robotics. More urgent, we
argue, than the household automation that dominates the field’s
current attention [11]. Human mobility is inherently fragile,
and demand for care is accelerating. By 2100, nearly a quarter
of the global population is projected to be aged 65 or older,
exceeding 35% in nations like Japan [12]. Relying solely on
human caregivers to absorb this demand compromises recip-
ient autonomy because independence in daily mobility then
depends on another person’s availability, while further strain-
ing an overwhelmed workforce [13]. Bed-to-chair transfers are
essential for well-being and preventing complications such as
pressure injuries [14], yet remain among the most physically
hazardous tasks for care staff, persisting despite known risks
due to staffing shortages and time pressure [15]. Automating
this burden does not dehumanise care: it frees caregivers to
focus on social and emotional connection, and restores au-
tonomy to recipients [16]. That hope is voiced clearly by
Henry Evans, left mute and quadriplegic by a 2002 stroke,
who, with Jane Evans, pioneered assistive robotics for disabil-
ity through Robots for Humanity: “for the severely disabled,
for whom miraculous medical breakthroughs don’t seem fea-
sible in our lifetimes, robots are the best hope for significant
independence” [17].

30 Hz, the search space spans tens of thousands of dimensions.
Robot-human, robot-environment, and human-environment
contact discontinuities further complicate the search space.
We address these bottlenecks through three generalisable
ideas to guide exploration (Figure 3). First, standard RL formu-
lations feed raw joint configurations [5], and sometimes end-
effector poses [9,22], into the MDP, creating a mismatch with
task-space rewards that we hypothesise contributes to poor ex-
ploration. We resolve this with Sparse Interaction Edges (SIE),
unifying state and reward through keypoints connected across
the robot and object, aligning the optimisation gradient with the
policy’s own observations. Second, standard transport rewards
measure only object-to-goal displacement, permitting uncon-
strained solutions such as throwing an object when sustained
proximity is what is actually desired; our Interaction Gated
Transport (IGT) softly gates the transport reward on this same
SIE structure. Third, jointly discovering robot and object tra-
jectories from scratch creates a prohibitively high-dimensional
search, and standard exploration fails wherever agents must
manoeuvre around obstacles; our Hinted Object Path (HOP) in-
stead guides exploration with an explicit object path. Across all
three, we give RL just enough task structure to guide learning
(which spatial relationships matter, how closely the robot must
stay engaged, and where the object should travel), while leav-
ing the precise manipulation strategy for the policy to discover,
refine, or abandon on its own.
Together, these contributions solve three whole-body,
contact-rich, long-horizon tasks where an ablated RL baseline
fails, from transporting a ball into a box to transferring humans.
As a flagship demonstration, we show, to our knowledge, the
first bed-to-wheelchair transfer of a medical mannequin by a
humanoid robot completed with no human intervention (Fig-
ure 1). Within the human transfer task, we show that a single
policy can adapt across substantial variation in human body
mass and size. (115–180 cm, 11–33 kg). Training is highly ef-
ficient, reaching competitive performance in under an hour on
a single consumer GPU across 2048 parallel environments. By
addressing core bottlenecks in high-dimensional robot learn-
ing, this work offers a pathway to fully RL-driven humanoid
manipulation, enabling assistive robots that can help humans.

Results
Here we describe our key findings. As is common in RL,
we report the mean and variance of evaluation returns across
different seeds (Figure 2). However, to fully illustrate the effec-
tiveness of our learned policies, we provide per-seed videos on
our project website (https://elle-miller.github.io/
into_my_arms/).
Setup.
We train our agent using clipped PPO [23] to control
AIREC, a 35-DoF mobile humanoid (see Methods). We evalu-
ate three novel environments: Ball, a rigid sphere transported
from a table into a raised box 2.5 m away; Human (near), trans-
ferring an articulated human model from a bed into a nearby
wheelchair; and Human (far), the same transfer task but over
a longer distance with an obstacle in the way. Full agent, en-
vironment, and training details are provided in Methods and
Appendix.
Ablated RL Baseline.
To our knowledge, no published pure-
RL method targets this combination of whole-body control,
complex object contact, and long-horizon transport, and prior
whole-body approaches typically rely on motion priors or im-
itation [5–8]. We therefore compare against an ablated RL
baseline that mirrors common design choices from that liter-
ature while removing our contributions: the agent observes
joint configurations [5] and end-effector relations [9,22], SIE
is restricted to the two end-effectors, giving two robot-object
edges for the observation and reward, and HOP gated by IGT
is replaced with a standard object-to-goal distance reward. Se-
quential ablations in Figure 6 isolate each component’s contri-
bution.
Compute.
On a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090, Ball
trains in about 30 minutes, while competitive Human perfor-
mance appears within about 60 minutes. The longer Human
wall-clock is dominated by articulated-body, contact-rich simu-
lation. Throughput is essentially identical for our method and
the RL baseline in each environment, so the extra guidance
structure does not measurably increase training cost.
Sparse Interaction Edges enable and guide whole-
body manipulation
Sparse Interaction Edges (SIE) establish spatial correspondence
between robot and object keypoints, aiding initial grasping and
later guiding transport via the interaction gate. In Ball, the
single object keypoint sits at the centre, so our SIE formulation
leaves the grasp location unguided, giving the policy flexi-
bility in its manipulation strategy. The Human environments
are designed differently: we deliberately choose object-robot
keypoint pairs that result in a more comfortable transfer.

Structured reinforcement learning for object transport. We propose three generalisable methods to guide learning in whole-body,
contact-rich, and long-horizon manipulation: Sparse Interaction Edges, Interaction Gated Transport, and Hinted Object Path. These ideas can be
integrated into the MDP’s observation and reward function without changes to the RL algorithm or training process.
Figure 2Figure 6 shows the effect of enabling whole-body
SIE in Ball, comparing the first and second panels. The RL
baseline agent (first panel) uses its right hand to push the ball
behind itself toward the goal, lifting its left arm out of the way;
it fails to lift or carry the ball across all five seeds (Figure 2).
We attribute this directly to the MDP formulation: the agent can
only “see” the end-effector-to-object distance vectors, and is
rewarded only for closing those distances, so the optimisation
aligns with end-effector motion alone, leaving the rest of the
robot’s embodiment unused. When we instead let the agent
observe additional robot-object keypoint pairs, and reward
minimising these edges, it learns to encircle the object with
both arms and lift it off the table (second panel). The agent still
fails the task, but the shift in strategy is the important result:
rather than exploiting a single end-effector, it now manipulates
the object using its whole body.
We observe a similar phenomenon in the human environ-
ments, with wider variation across seeds: the baseline agent
typically throws the human, drops it into its other hand, or
lifts by a single thigh or the head, rather than supporting it
appropriately (Human (near) in Figure 2; further variation in
Human (far) is discussed below). These results suggest two
primary benefits of SIE. First, it facilitates whole-body manip-
ulation via observation-reward alignment. By perceiving and
directly acting on robot-object spatial relationships, the agent
no longer has to discover these relationships implicitly in the
joint space. Second, it biases the solution toward desirable
behaviour through robot-object keypoint design alone, without
additional reward engineering.
Interaction Gated Transport enables consistent, but
not constraining, long-horizon strategies
Standard transportation rewards typically measure only object-
to-goal displacement, permitting unconstrained solutions such
as throwing or pushing. For long-horizon transport tasks, how-
ever, sustained proximity to the object is usually what is ac-
tually desired. After learning to grasp and lift an object via
SIE, we apply Interaction Gated Transport, which acts like
an activation function on the transport reward, based on the
lengths of a chosen subset of SIE edges.
We see the direct effect of IGT in Ball by comparing the
second and third panels of Figure 6. The initial grasp and lift
are nearly identical, but instead of throwing the ball, the IGT-
enabled agent maintains its grasp and carries the ball to the
box. The same pattern appears, with greater variation, in the
human environments. For the RL baseline in Human (near), the
transport strategy differs in every seed, since the unconstrained
reward permits any path to the goal. This variation is magnified
under the longer horizon of Human (far): seed 1 transports
the human upside-down by the ankles; seeds 2 and 4 launch
the human into the air, overhead and behind; seed 3 comes
closer, but ends up flipping the human 360◦; and only seed
5 succeeds. In contrast, our method transports the human in
the same style across every seed in both human environments,
with only minor variation, e.g., in Human (near) seed 2, the
agent’s right hand wraps around the left thigh. In contrast, for
other seeds it wraps around the upper left calf. By zeroing
the transport reward whenever robot-object keypoint distances
exceed a set threshold, IGT prevents early convergence to these
degenerate strategies and enables stable, repeatable behaviour
over long horizons.
Hinted Object Path resolves collision and search-
complexity bottlenecks
Standard object-to-goal displacement rewards do not scale to
contact-rich, high-dimensional transport problems. The gradi-
ent of fastest reward accumulation points directly at the goal,
which in the presence of obstacles points into a collision bound-
ary rather than around it, trapping exploration. The Hinted
Object Path instead defines a reward over a full path to the
goal, redirecting this gradient around such boundaries. We see
this directly in Ball, comparing the third and fourth panels of
Figure 6: SIE and IGT alone already enable the agent to lift
and carry the ball with its whole body, but with an object-to-
goal reward the gradient of fastest accumulation points into the
box itself, and the agent cannot explore around this collision
boundary. With HOP, the object path and thus reward gradient
is instead directed up and over the box, leading to task success.
HOP’s second benefit, reducing search complexity, grows
with object complexity and is clearest in the human environ-
ments, where jointly discovering a robot and object trajec-
tory across many object keypoints is itself a high-dimensional
search problem, independent of any obstacle. Without HOP, the
RL baseline’s human keypoints follow wildly different paths

Immediate generalisation across diverse human
masses and sizes
An implicit benefit of using RL for object transport, rather than
task-specific engineered control, is generalisability: a closed-
loop policy should adapt to variation in the object itself, not
just the robot’s own state. Here, we test whether our proposed
method retains this benefit after being trained with our addi-
tional task structure. We study this using the human transfer
task, both because articulated bodies present a harder gener-
alisation challenge than a rigid object, and because human
bodies vary substantially in practice: a real-world necessity
for any caregiving system. We train different policies under
independent variations in human mass (11–33 kg) and height
(115–180 cm) (joint torque limits constrain our physical plat-
form to a maximum payload of ∼35 kg).
In Figure 5(a), across 1,000 rollouts spanning this mass
range, we can see that the policy largely guides the human
along a common path regardless of mass, with some variation:
lighter bodies follow a slightly elevated trajectory and corre-
spondingly sharper robot joint inflexions, indicating tighter
trajectory tracking. A trained policy is also able to adapt across
human height, since representing the body and task goals via
five keypoints endows the observation space with structural
geometric invariance. The main challenges instead came from
physical scene alignment: the workspace and initial robot con-
figuration were tailored to the 155 cm mannequin, leaving sub-
optimal starting placements at extreme heights (e.g., the 115 cm
mannequin’s feet resting on the robot’s hand), and the compact
Japanese wheelchair frame struggled to accommodate indi-
viduals near 180 cm. Despite these alignment and workspace
constraints, the agent recovers and completes the transfer task
in most cases, demonstrating generalisation across substantial
variation in human morphology without any additional training.
Robust closed-loop behaviour to unpredictable ob-
ject motion
Real-world transport rarely goes exactly to plan: objects can
shift, slip, or be jostled mid-transport, and a robust policy must
react rather than blindly follow a fixed trajectory. This is espe-
cially true for human transfer, where care-recipients with motor
impairments may experience involuntary spasms or tremors.
To evaluate the agent’s capacity to react to such disturbances,
we apply random external forces to the target object in both the
Ball and Human environments: at each timestep, a force sam-
pled uniformly from [300,500] N is applied with probability
p = 0.1 to the centre of the ball or the chest of the human.
Introducing these perturbations prompted the agent to dis-
cover a distinct, more robust manipulation strategy while pre-
serving task success. In Ball (final panel, Figure 6), the unper-
turbed agent executes a symmetrical “hug” grasp, placing both
hands together and supporting the ball atop its forearms; under
perturbation, it instead learns an asymmetrical grasp (right hand
underneath for primary support, left hand around the centre for
stabilisation) maintained throughout transport. We observe an
analogous adaptation in Human. Whereas unperturbed rollouts
rest the left hand at the centre of the back with the right arm
at a right angle (Figure 1), the perturbed policy wraps the left
hand over the human’s left shoulder, drawing the right hand
much closer to the chest and securing the human closer to its
torso (Figure 5(b)).

training prompt the emergent discovery of tighter, more secure
grasps without any explicit reward, demonstrating RL’s capac-

First bed-to-chair transfer of a medical mannequin
without intervention
As a flagship demonstration, we replayed trajectories from our
trained policy, open-loop, on the physical AIREC robot in a
replica bedroom with an adjustable-height hospital bed and
standard wheelchair (Figure 1), using a medical training man-
nequin (155 cm, 15 kg) with realistic joint motion. Despite sev-
eral sim-to-real challenges (Appendix A), AIREC successfully
lifts the mannequin from the bed, carries it to the wheelchair,
places it down, and disengages. We test two wheelchair place-
ments, facing the head of the bed and facing away at the foot,
both requiring a 90◦rotation in addition to translation (Fig-
ures 1). To our knowledge, this is the first bed-to-wheelchair
transfer of a medical mannequin by a humanoid completed
without a caregiver in the loop.
Closing the sim-to-real gap required addressing four chal-
lenges, detailed in Appendix A: an unmodelled real-world
impedance controller diverging from our idealised simulated
PD law, matching the real robot’s strict stability monitoring,
which halts execution if the Zero-Moment Point exits its sup-
port polygon; eliminating large contact forces on the wheelchair
that would tip it in reality but were invisible to a fixed simulated
chair; and precisely calibrating the simulated scene, including
the mannequin’s shape, to match the physical setup. Even so,
replaying the same trajectory did not always yield the same out-
come, and we observed failures alongside successful rollouts.
This demonstration shows that the learned strategy is physically
realisable; it is not a full closed-loop hardware validation study.
Discussion
SIE, IGT, and HOP supply just enough structure for whole-
body, contact-rich, long-horizon transport where an ablated
RL baseline fails. Applying our method to a new task requires
a small set of designer choices: robot, object, and goal key-
points; interaction edges that gate transport; a coarse, hinted
object path; and completion tolerances. We see this as a light
design burden compared with gathering teleoperated or human
demonstrations for every new object, scene, and goal. Criti-
cally, the guidance is a hint instead of a rule: the SIE/IGT/HOP
reward terms contribute only a small fraction of total return
(e.g., 5-10%, Figure 2), so the policy remains free to adopt an
alternative strategy for the guidance terms are zero. In practice,
it often approximately follows the suggested engagement and
path early on, then departs when beneficial.
One may ask why a general-purpose humanoid controlled
by a learned RL policy is needed, rather than alternatives such
as a robotic hoist or bed-mounted transfer system. We see a
general-purpose humanoid as one attractive path alongside spe-
cialised devices such as robotic wheelchairs and exoskeletons.
One mobile platform can share sensing and control advances
across many caregiving tasks, so hardware and software effort
need not be rebuilt for each new assistive function. In addition,
a free-moving humanoid can, in principle, accompany a person
beyond the bedside into settings that remain largely inaccessi-
6


ble today, such as rugged terrain or commercial air travel [24].
Thus, we build on a long line of general-purpose humanoid
caregiving robots. Between 2006 and 2015, a series of Japanese
humanoids demonstrated the first bed-to-chair transfers of hu-
mans (RI-MAN [25], RIBA [1], TWENDY-ONE [26]). Those
systems were semi-autonomous: execution relied on a pre-
defined trajectory, with a caregiver in the loop to perform key
sub-actions (e.g., lifting the legs) and make fine adjustments
for individual human properties (e.g., height).
Achieving full autonomy raises a related question of learn-
ing paradigm. More recently, model-based approaches have
generated desired contact forces for autonomous assistance via
whole-body quadratic programming, for sit-to-stand [27] and
arm repositioning [28], and via motion planning for supine-to-
lateral repositioning [29]. These approaches offer valuable
safety guarantees, but their reliance on an accurate model
makes them harder to apply when the human, an unpredictable
agent rather than a passive object, is difficult to plan for [30].
Imitation learning (IL) trades this modelling burden for a differ-
ent one: recent approaches replicate teleoperated repositioning
manoeuvres, such as sitting up in bed [31], offering a simpler
route to competent behaviour, though by construction repro-
ducing only a fixed demonstrated trajectory limits their ability
to react to unpredictable human behaviour at test time. RL
instead offers closed-loop, dynamically adaptive policies well
suited to this unpredictability, while also exploiting a robotic
caregiver’s high-torque actuators, non-biological ranges of mo-
tion, and superhuman stability, rather than mimicking human
caregiver technique constrained by spinal vulnerability [14].
We see these paradigms as complementary rather than compet-
ing: combining RL’s adaptability with the safety guarantees
of model-based control, or the sample efficiency of imitation
learning, is a promising future direction.
Having motivated both choices, a further question is how
close this brings us to deploying with an actual human rather
than a mannequin (Figure 5(c)). Two barriers remain: motor
strength and safety, and resolving them defines our main direc-
tions for future work. Holding a 50 kg person (and own 25 kg
arm) at 0.5 m requires each shoulder motor to supply approxi-
mately 375 Nm, above AIREC’s current rated 220 Nm; stronger
motors exist, so the limit is engineering rather than physics.
Safety is more fundamental as a typical neural-network policy
offers no certificate that small shifts in state will not produce
unsafe actions. Progress on certifiable robustness for neural
control in safety-critical settings [32] is one path toward clos-
ing that gap. More fundamentally, we view whole-body tactile
sensing as imperative for future iterations, both to relax the
framework’s dependence on precise object keypoint percep-
tion and to ensure human safety, comfort, and robustness to
dynamic motion, slippage, and occlusion during real physical
contact. Compliant, force-limited whole-body control [33,34]
is a complementary path toward safer interaction even before
such sensing is mature. Resolving the RL trilemma on this
caregiving testbed is a step toward assistive autonomy; human
deployment still requires the barriers above to be addressed.

Sparse Interaction Edges (SIE)
Standard RL baselines typically observe joint configurations [5]
and sometimes end-effector poses [9, 22], paired with task-
space rewards such as object-to-goal or end-effector-to-object
distance. We hypothesise that this representation is insufficient
for whole-body manipulation, leading to poor exploration. Sev-
eral recent works build explicit spatial representations between
robot and object, but for purposes distinct from resolving this
exploration problem directly. OmniRetarget [7] constructs an
interaction mesh over robot, object, and terrain keypoints, dat-
ing back over a decade to early interaction-mesh-based motion
adaptation [35], but uses it only to improve motion-retargeting
quality. The RL policy itself remains blind to this structure, ob-
serving basic proprioception and tracking a reference trajectory
precisely. AnyBody [36] similarly guides whole-body control
via a sparse set of robot keypoints, but for motion tracking
alone, with no object or manipulation task. Yuan et al. [37]
instead represent robot-human interaction through a topology-
based Writhe matrix, demonstrating sim-to-real transfer of
a dual-arm holding policy for swimmer rescue. In contrast,
Zheng et al. [6] perceive robot-object interaction through a
latent neural signed-distance representation computed by a pre-
trained network, relying on motion priors to accelerate learning
in long-horizon, contact-rich tasks.
None of these representations is used to directly shape both
what the policy observes and what it is rewarded for. We in-
stead resolve this representation mismatch by unifying state
and reward through Sparse Interaction Edges: a lightweight,
hand-specified set of keypoints on the robot and manipulated
object, connected by 3D spatial edge vectors, requiring no
reference motion, motion priors, or latent perception. For-
mulating the reward as a scalar field over these edge lengths
aligns the optimisation gradient with the policy’s observation
features, accelerating convergence in whole-body tasks. The re-
sulting low-dimensional topology also acts as a compact map,
projecting the high-dimensional configuration space onto a
task-relevant manifold.

Interaction Gated Transport (IGT)
The primary objective of pick-and-place is to translate each
object keypoint to a unique goal keypoint, and standard trans-
portation rewards accordingly measure only object-to-goal dis-
8


placement (e.g., [5, 9, 22]). We found that ungated object-
to-goal rewards produced degenerate policies that used only
part of the robot, threw the object, or pushed it. Instead, we
wanted the robot to stay close to the object throughout trans-
port. We therefore introduce Interaction Gated Transport (IGT),
which applies a soft gate to the transport reward using the same
SIE-based formulation, contingent on proximity rather than
contact. This keeps the observation and reward spaces aligned,
requires no reference motion, and makes transport contingent
on sustained whole-body interaction rather than on object dis-
placement alone.
A related line of work constrains whole-body manipulation
via explicit contact goals, rather than leaving the manner of con-
tact unconstrained as above. WoCoCo [10] decomposes loco-
manipulation into sequential contact stages with pre-defined
contact goals (e.g., the side of a box); Lin et al. [9] similarly
specify desired contact locations for dexterous fingertips, with
an associated contact reward; and MaskedManipulator [38]
identifies which body parts should be in contact and rewards
proximity to a reference motion’s contact configuration. Each
of these requires a well-defined notion of contact, e.g., a loca-
tion on the object surface, which is unavailable in tasks like
Ball, where the sole object keypoint sits at an uncontactable
centre.

Hinted Object Path (HOP)
Jointly discovering robot and object trajectories is high-
dimensional, and endpoint-only rewards often drive the agent
into obstacles. We hypothesise this is a local-minima problem:
an endpoint-based reward directs the agent along the direction
of steepest reward accumulation, which in free space coincides
with the direct path to the goal, but in contact-rich settings with
obstacles, or narrow openings, this direction frequently points
into a collision boundary rather than around it.
Nearly every RL method for whole-body manipulation that
uses a reference, whether joint states, robot keypoints, or end-
effector waypoints, guides the robot’s own motion with it [5,
7,8,36,38,39]. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work
in the domain of RL-driven humanoid manipulation provides
a standalone object’s trajectory as a learning reference. We
propose the Hinted Object Path, an explicit hint trajectory for
the object itself from start to goal, for each object-goal pair
(Figure 4). The reward is defined over this full path to the goal
rather than the object’s final pose alone, redirecting the gradient
of fastest accumulation around collision boundaries rather than
into them. Any trajectory generation method is compatible
with HOP.

Analytical Bandwidth Computation (ABC)
For all dense reward components computed via the hyperbolic
tangent transform D, we normalise distances and control gradi-
ent steepness via a bandwidth parameter kD. The disadvantage
of this formulation is that kD requires careful tuning to ensure
the reward is meaningfully above zero at the initial distance –
too large a kD yields a vanishing gradient at onset, while too
small saturates the reward prematurely. This is typically tuned
empirically, which is time-consuming. Recent work proposes
bi-level optimisation to find the optimal bandwidth [40]. In-
stead, we propose Analytical Bandwidth Computation (ABC):
rather than hand-tuning kD, we specify a desired initial re-
ward value r0 at a known initial (offset) distance δ, such that

Experimental setup
Environments.
We evaluate our agent in three environments,
Ball, Human (near), and Human (far), built in Isaac Lab [41],
with the control policy running at 30 Hz and the physics en-
gine at 120 Hz. In Ball, the object is a rigid sphere, 50 cm
in diameter, with randomised mass between 2.5–7.5 kg, trans-
ported from a tabletop surface into a raised box more than
9


2.5 m away. In Human (near) and Human (far), the object is an
articulated human model composed of spheres and cylinders,
with 15 revolute joints (head, neck, chest, shoulder pitch and
roll, elbows, hip pitch and roll, knees), a height of 155 cm,
and randomised mass between 11–33 kg, transferred from a
bed into a wheelchair; Human (far) additionally places the
wheelchair at a greater distance, with an obstacle in the way.
Agent.
Observations combine proprioception (joints, torques,
command errors, centre of mass, planar ZMP) with SIE, IGT,
and HOP features (Table 1 in Appendix D). Actions are 35-
DoF joint-position deltas for AIREC (head, torso, arms, hands,
omnidirectional base), slew-limited and smoothed, then tracked
by a low-level PD controller (gains and limits in Appendix).
Episodes use environment-specific warm-start poses with ran-
domised base, object, mass, and goal; they terminate on tip-
over, dropped object, excessive displacement, or numerical
failure.

A. Sim-to-real transfer
To successfully transfer rollouts from trained policies to the
physical AIREC robot, we had to overcome several sim-to-real
challenges.
1) Controller mismatch. Simulation relied on an ideal PD
torque law (τ = Kp(θ cmd −θ) −Kd ˙θ), whereas the physical
robot operates under an unmodelled impedance controller. This
mismatch produced divergent torque and joint position signals
that escalated with joint velocity (Figure 7). We mitigated the
mismatch by replaying the trajectories at a reduced frequency
of 10 Hz (1/3× the training frequency).
2) Stability monitoring. The real robot has strict stability
safety monitoring to prevent tipping over. An emergency stop
triggers if the Zero-Moment Point (ZMP) exits a 12×12 cm2
support polygon. We match this behaviour in simulation via
a termination condition and a −1000 penalty, and add the
planar ZMP, centre-of-mass position, and acceleration to the
observation so the agent can implicitly learn this constraint.
3) Wheelchair collision avoidance. Fixing the wheelchair po-
sition in simulation allowed policies that exerted large forces on
the chair to aid placement, which would tip the physical chair
in the real world. We eliminated this behaviour by inflating
the wheelchair’s collision meshes, which removed robot-chair
contact in the real world.
4) Scene alignment. We calibrated the simulated environ-
ment (bed, wheelchair) to mirror the physical objects. While
we trained with position randomisations, the object positions
for open-loop replay had to match the physical scene. For the
mannequin, we measured each link and approximated its shape
with cylinders.
5) Safety stops from torque and joint-position limits. The real
robot enforces hard safety stops when commanded torques or
joint position errors exceed rated limits. These stops occurred
frequently during human placement, where the combination of
the human’s weight and the precision required to lower them
into the wheelchair pushed several joints close to their limits.
We could not overcome this limitation directly.

D. Observations and actions
Observation.
The agent receives proprioception plus SIE,
IGT, and HOP features (Table 1). Proprioception is identical
across tasks: normalised joint positions, velocities, and torques;
joint position and torque command errors; centre-of-mass posi-
tion and acceleration; and planar ZMP,
px = xcom −zcom ¨xcom
¨zcom +g ,

¨zcom +g ,
(17)
with g = 9.81m/s2. Including the ZMP and terminating if it
leaves the support polygon discourages unstable whole-body
configurations adopted only to accelerate task completion.
Robot and object keypoint states are expressed relative to the
robot base, with SIE and object-to-goal edges. Robot keypoints
carry Cartesian position, velocity, and a 6D continuous rota-
tion. Object keypoints carry Cartesian position and velocity.
Goal keypoints carry Cartesian position (Ng = No). Each SIE
(and object-to-goal) edge contributes a distance vector, scalar
distance, relative velocity, and normalised scalar distance. Ob-
servation size therefore scales with keypoint count.
Actions.
AIREC has 35 DoF (head 3, torso 3, dual arms
14, hands 12, omnidirectional base 3). The policy outputs a
joint-position delta a ∈R35. We slew-limit the raw delta to
˙θslew = 1 rad/s on revolute joints, then apply an exponential
moving average with α = 0.1. The result is integrated to a
target joint position and tracked by a low-level PD controller,
with commanded torque clipped to the actuator limit and joint
velocity clipped to 50% of the hardware limit in simulation.
E. Simulation details
Scene modelling.
The bed and wheelchair are modelled to
match their real-world counterparts (Figure 1). The bed com-
prises three prisms: a rigid base, a compliant mattress, and a
compliant pillow. The wheelchair matches a standard Japanese
model with a compact frame (40 cm seat width, versus the
46 cm Western standard), so insertion tolerance is tight. Place-
ment into a wheelchair is harder than into an ordinary chair
because armrests, side bars, and footrests restrict the viable
placement space. For open-loop physical replay, evaluation
episodes begin with the robot entirely behind the human, rather
than with the mannequin pre-aligned with the robot’s hands as
during training.
Initialisation, randomisation, and termination.
In Ball,
the robot starts 0.3 m from the surface with outstretched arms.
In the Human environments, it starts 0.6 m away in a lifting
configuration. We randomise robot base pose, object pose,
object joints (when applicable), object mass, and goal pose.
Episodes terminate when (i) the robot base tilts beyond stability
thresholds, (ii) the manipulated object falls below a height
threshold, (iii) the object exceeds a distance from the origin, or
(iv) a numerical instability is detected.
F. RL training details
We use clipped PPO [23]. Parallelism is 4096 environments
for Ball and 2048 for both Human settings. Observations and
values are scaled by running normalisers. The policy and
critic are MLPs with hidden layers [512,256,128] and ELU
activations. The policy outputs a Gaussian with a tanh mean
head and a state-dependent logσ head. We use KL-adaptive
learning-rate scheduling. Table 2 lists the shared PPO recipe;

G. Teleoperation and imitation attempt
Imitation offers two routes: mimicking a human caregiver, or
teleoperating the robot. We argue that copying human caregiver
technique is a limited path for physical assistance, since the
technique is constrained by human mobility. We attempted the
task via teleoperation with a high-fidelity robotic puppet, but
an expert demonstrator failed to lift the mannequin robustly.
Under the mannequin’s weight, it kept slipping, faster than the
operator could react (Figure 8).
