Abstract
Robot reward modeling faces two challenges: generalizing across tasks and accurately tracking progress in
long-horizon manipulation. General-purpose reward models demonstrate broad task transfer, but transfer
from short-task pretraining to long-horizon tasks remains underexplored. Long-horizon progress models often
rely on task-specific subtask classifiers or fixed action vocabularies, limiting the reuse of learned progress
knowledge when adapting to new tasks. To address these challenges, we introduce Grana, a vision-language
robot reward model that learns to identify unfinished subtasks and estimate their continuous progress using
open-vocabulary subtask descriptions. We train a language-conditioned model to estimate progress in short-
horizon tasks and individual subtasks of long-horizon tasks. For long-horizon tasks, we combine the selected
subtask’s predicted progress with credit for preceding completed subtasks to estimate whole-task progress.
General progress prediction requires training the model across diverse tasks with progress supervision that
accounts for imperfect execution. Elapsed-time proxies can misrepresent progress when relative subtask
durations vary, or failures reverse local progress. We therefore introduce an annotation protocol that converts
sparse subtask boundaries and action-quality annotations into dense progress targets, without requiring
annotators to assign numerical progress scores. Pretraining with this supervision on 200 diverse, mostly
short-horizon manipulation tasks enables zero-shot reward prediction on unseen tasks and improves progress
prediction after adaptation to multi-minute, long-horizon tasks. Experiments demonstrate (i) generalizable
reward prediction, including adaptation to five long-horizon tasks, (ii) benefits from pretraining and composing
subtask progress estimates, and (iii) improved downstream policy performance.

1. Introduction
Progress-based reward models estimate how much of a task has been completed, providing learning
signals for robot policies [1, 2]. For long-horizon manipulation, these models should reuse progress
knowledge learned from shorter tasks while tracking failed attempts and recovery.
Consider assembling a model airplane by placing a wing, inserting a screw, and tightening it with a
drill. Completing wing placement should receive the same progress credit across executions, even
when the time spent on each subtask varies. During insertion, repeated misalignment should slow
progress, while dropping the screw should reset insertion progress without erasing credit for the
placed wing. Furthermore, reaching for the drill should not advance progress while insertion remains
unfinished. These distinctions motivate us to compose whole-task progress from subtask progress,
and to combine subtask boundaries with action-quality annotations to capture setbacks and recovery.
General-purpose reward models learn from diverse robot videos [2--4] or use pretrained vision-
language models directly [5] to provide reward signals across diverse tasks. We ask whether learning
progress at the subtask level improves whole-task progress tracking and supports transfer from
short tasks to long-horizon tasks. Some long-horizon progress models use task-specific subtask
classifiers [1] or a fixed action vocabulary shared across tasks [6]. These choices limit reuse of learned
progress knowledge when subtasks change in a new long-horizon task. We seek a model that learns
subtask selection and progress prediction through language descriptions, so that knowledge from
short tasks can be reused in longer compositions.
Progress supervision poses a second challenge. Subtask completion keyframes alone do not reflect
failed attempts or recovery. Local execution judgments can describe setbacks [6, 7], but a progress
curve must also show how much work is lost. A brief failure may undo the current attempt without
undoing earlier subtasks.
We introduce Grana, a vision-language robot reward model that learns to select the unfinished subtask
and predict its local progress from open-vocabulary descriptions (Fig. 1). We combine this prediction
with credit for preceding completed subtasks to estimate whole-task progress. Incorrect subtask
choices can distort this whole-task estimate, so training considers subtask order to define a local-
progress target for each selected subtask. The same language interface lets us reuse the pretrained
model on new tasks and longer compositions without replacing model components.
To train this predictor, we combine subtask boundaries with action-quality annotations to construct a
dense progress curve without direct numerical scoring. Subtask boundaries mark when subtasks are
completed, while action-quality annotations describe nominal, suboptimal, and regressed execution
within each subtask. The constructed progress curve advances more slowly during suboptimal
execution and drops when regressed execution loses local progress. It rises again during recovery
while preserving credit for earlier completed subtasks. Using this supervision, we pretrain Grana on
200 mostly short manipulation tasks.
We evaluate how these design choices affect progress prediction on new tasks and a different
embodiment, as well as downstream policy learning. We also show that structured supervision
improves progress predictions from a prior vision-language model (VLM)- based reward model. We
further show Grana ’s generalization capabilities, achieving 20.7% lower progress RMSE than the
strongest evaluated baseline without target-task reward labels or fine-tuning on 50 held-out tasks.
Across five long-horizon tasks with up to 18 subtasks, Grana adapted with 15 labeled trajectories per
task achieves the lowest progress RMSE among the evaluated models. On the two 3--5-minute airplane
and red car assembly tasks, it also outperforms baselines adapted with 100 trajectories; matched
comparisons show that pretraining improves prediction on both tasks at both adaptation budgets.
The reward model’s zero-shot progress prediction also improves offline reinforcement learning (RL) on

∙Compositional progress prediction. We train the model to select subtasks and predict local

∙Structured progress supervision. We combine subtask boundaries and action-quality annota-

∙Evidence for reward model generalization and policy improvement. We show that short-task
pretraining improves long-task adaptation and that predicted progress supports downstream
policy learning.
2. Related Work
General-purpose robot reward models
Pretrained visual and vision-language representations can be adapted to provide reusable reward
signals from image goals, text, or demonstration videos [8--11]. Recent approaches use pretrained
VLMs to estimate task progress without further fine-tuning [5, 12] or learn reward and value
predictors from diverse video data [2--4, 13]. However, the benefits of short-task pretraining for
long-horizon progress prediction remain underexplored, so we study whether subtask supervision
improves long-task adaptation.
Progress supervision
Video-derived supervision for reward learning includes temporal ordering and transformations [3,
14, 15], signed frame distances or remaining-time targets [13, 16], and successful-transition predic-
tion [17]. Other supervision uses subtask boundaries, interval-level progress and completion labels, or
simulated failures [7, 18, 19]. To capture local setbacks and recovery while preserving completed work,
we deterministically convert sparse subtask boundaries and action-quality judgments into dense
progress curves without direct numerical scoring. The constructed curves reset local progress on
failure and rebuild it according to action quality during retries, while retaining credit for completed
subtasks.
Progress estimation through task decomposition
REDS infers subtasks through video--language alignment and learns subtask-conditioned rewards
from segmented demonstrations [20]. SARM reports that REDS’s predictions remain largely step-
shaped despite monotonicity regularization, limiting fine-grained progress estimation. By separately
predicting subtask identity and continuous local progress, SARM achieves lower progress-estimation
error on T-shirt folding [1]. SARM2 extends subtask-aware reward modeling to multiple tasks [6].
PRM4RL composes subtask indices and local progress through generated simulator-state functions,
while RoboMeter advances through VLM-proposed subtasks using success predictions during online
RL [2, 21]. Grana studies how short-task pretraining supports longer compositions by combining
predicted local progress with credit for preceding completed subtasks. Its supervision explicitly
captures local failure resets and recovery, while task order defines progress targets even for incorrect
subtasks.
Reward-guided policy learning
Learned visual rewards already support robot policy improvement through RL [2, 14, 22]. Offline
RL and advantage-weighted learning provide complementary policy-improvement tools [23--25]. We

3.1. Predicting Progress through Subtasks
Given camera observations, a task description, and the task’s subtask sequence, Grana selects the
unfinished subtask and predicts its local progress (Fig. 2). The supplied sequence specifies the user’s
preferred execution order and evaluation granularity. Subtask descriptions are language inputs to a
shared predictor; their order and number are not hard-coded into the architecture. The same task
can therefore be queried with different valid orders or coarser or finer decompositions. Training or
adaptation uses supervision defined under the chosen specification, while inference conditions on the
supplied list. Whole-task progress measures completion relative to this user-specified procedure.

We implement the shared predictor with Qwen3.5-4B [26] and LoRA [27], training the adapters,

retain the same architecture and language interface. At inference, the model receives observations,
the description, and the subtask list, without annotation inputs.

4. Experiments
Our experiments address four questions:
1. Supervision: Can structured subtask and quality annotations provide practical and useful
progress supervision?
2. Generalization: How well does Grana generalize across tasks, horizons, embodiments, and
datasets?
3. Pretraining and composition: How does reward-model pretraining improve long-horizon adap-
tation, and does subtask composition improve progress prediction in both Grana and a frontier
VLM such as GPT-6 Astra [28]?
4. Policy learning: Do the resulting progress estimates improve downstream policy learning?

Zero-shot evaluation uses 50 held-out tasks. Long-horizon reward prediction covers five tasks: circuit

4.2.2. Progress Consistency under Different Supervision
We evaluate the official RoboMeter checkpoint and RoboMeter trained with its original or our
constructed targets using two metrics (Appendix A.4). Q-Rank measures how often a high-quality
keyframe scores above a low-quality keyframe of the same subtask from another episode; a random
ordering of each pair is correct half the time on average (50% accuracy). Down--Up measures how
often scores around an annotated failure satisfy the drop-and-recovery criterion in the appendix. We
report both because random scores can attain high Down--Up scores without consistently ranking
execution quality, whereas scores that never decrease always give Down--Up = 0. Constructed-target
finetuning achieves the highest scores on both tasks (Table 2).

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce Grana, a generalizable reward model for robotics. It combines structured
progress supervision with a shared predictor conditioned on the subtask that should be the focus
of evaluation. Subtask boundaries and action-chunk judgments determine targets that capture
failure resets and recovery through retries, and these targets also improve the existing RoboMeter
architecture. Pretraining on short tasks supports zero-shot reward prediction, long-task adaptation,
and public OOD ranking after adaptation. At the same time, the resulting progress estimates
improve policies through offline RL and progress-weighted behavior cloning. The observed agreement
between rankings by held-out progress RMSE and policy success supports using RMSE to prioritize
promising reward models for downstream evaluation. The long-horizon comparisons show benefits
from subtask-based inference in both GPT-6 and base Qwen3.5-4B, and from pretrained initialization
of the 4B predictor under matched target-task adaptation.

A.4. Progress-Consistency Metrics
Table 2 scores the airplane and car model test episodes with whole-task progress (accumulated
subtask progress divided by 𝐾, averaged over the four camera views) at about 1 Hz. Both metrics use
the human annotations of these episodes.
Q-Rank.
Annotators mark keyframes of high- and low-quality execution within each subtask. For every pair
of a high-quality and a low-quality keyframe of the same subtask from different episodes, we take
the score at the evaluation point nearest to each keyframe (within 0.5 s). Q-Rank is the percentage
of pairs in which the high-quality keyframe scores strictly higher. Independent random scores drawn
from the same continuous distribution rank either frame higher with equal probability, giving Q-Rank
18


Grana: Compositional Reward Modeling for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation
50 on average. The pairs involve 12 low-quality keyframes per task.
Down--Up (D--U).
Each event is an annotated failure interval followed by a high-quality keyframe of the same subtask.
Let 𝑠−be the last score strictly before the failure onset. We search the first 3 s after that onset for
the minimum score 𝑠min; this is the search window, not the duration of the annotated failure. Let 𝑠+
be the score nearest to the later high-quality keyframe (within 1 s). We count the score response to
an annotated failure as correct when the window minimum is below both the pre-failure and recovery
scores: 𝑠−> 𝑠min and 𝑠+ > 𝑠min, with strict comparisons and no threshold. Down--Up is the percentage
of these annotated failure events with a correct score response. Each event is counted once, even if
the score drops repeatedly.
Reference values.
A predictor whose scores never decrease cannot produce 𝑠min < 𝑠−, so its Down--Up is 0. A predictor
whose score increases only with elapsed time is one such case. Scores drawn independently from
the same continuous distribution give Q-Rank 50 on average. If 𝑠−, 𝑠+, and the 𝑘window scores are
distinct samples, the window minimum lies below both comparison scores with probability 𝑘/(𝑘+ 2),
so random scores can satisfy the Down--Up criterion for many events. A high Down--Up score alone
therefore does not establish that score decreases are specific to failures. We report Q-Rank alongside
Down--Up to assess whether the scores also rank execution quality.

E. Test-Time Model Predictive Control with Grana
Besides policy learning (Sec. 4.5), predicted progress can guide a fixed policy at test time. A world
action model (WAM) predicts the outcome of an action chunk before executing it, so the robot can
compare candidate actions before committing to one. Model predictive control (MPC) with the WAM
is costly, however: at each planning step the robot pauses while the WAM imagines candidate futures.
We therefore use Grana in two roles at inference: it decides when to switch MPC on and off, and, while
MPC is on, it selects among the imagined futures. Both roles combine the selected subtask with its
local progress, without additional training.
E.1. Switching MPC On and Off
While the policy runs, Grana scores the real camera stream every ∆𝑡seconds and returns the selected
subtask index 𝑘𝑡and the composed whole-task progress ˆ𝑃𝑡(Eq. 1). A single misclassified frame should
not trigger MPC, so we smooth the subtask index: the smoothed index ¯𝑘𝑡changes only after a new
subtask is selected on 𝑚consecutive estimates. MPC switches on when either signal indicates that
the policy is in trouble:
∙Progress stall. Whole-task progress has not grown over the last 𝑊seconds, ˆ𝑃𝑡−ˆ𝑃𝑡−𝑊< 𝛿: the
policy is stuck, or repeated attempts are failing.
∙Subtask jump. The smoothed subtask index skips ahead, ¯𝑘𝑡−¯𝑘𝑡−Δ𝑡> 1: the policy has moved to a
later step before completing the current one, for example reaching for the drill before mounting
a wheel.
MPC switches off once the robot has clearly moved past the difficult step: ¯𝑘𝑡advances to the next
subtask and its local progress exceeds a margin 𝜖, or the final subtask’s local progress approaches
completion. A cap of 𝑀seconds of MPC keeps the robot from staying in planning mode when neither
condition is met. Control then returns to the policy alone until the next trigger.
