Training humanoid vision-language-action (VLA) models relies heavily on costly real-robot teleopera-
tion. Recent efforts convert egocentric human demonstrations into humanoid training data. However,
most methods still require wearable motion trackers and leave important hand–object interaction cues
implicit. Many also mix in real-robot teleoperation data for VLA post-training. These dependencies
limit scalable data collection, while implicit interaction cues make precise contact harder to learn. To
overcome these limitations, we propose EgoLoAF, the first paradigm to derive all training data for
humanoid loco-manipulation directly from egocentric demonstrations without wearable motion trackers.
We post-train the VLA on the converted data alone, without real-robot teleoperation demonstrations,
and use explicit hand–object interaction cues to guide action generation. Specifically, to obtain
humanoid action targets, we estimate torso motion from recorded camera poses and reconstruct both
hand trajectories from video. From the same recording, we use each hand’s current pose relative to
the object to represent hand–object affordances. During post-training, we inject these affordances
into the action expert through cross-attention while keeping the vision-language backbone frozen.
Additionally, we release 128,400 egocentric demonstration episodes spanning 1,000 hours and 1,284
tasks, together with converted humanoid action trajectories and rendered robot-view observations.
Across four real-world tasks, EgoLoAF nearly achieves teleoperation performance (73.1 vs. 74.4 in
mean Task Progress Score), while achieving a mean 4.03× improvement in data-collection efficiency.

Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models and related policies have made progress in robot manipulation and
humanoid loco-manipulation (Jiang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026; Lambda-humanoid Team, 2026; Na et al.,
2026; Han et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). However, training humanoid VLAs still relies
heavily on real-robot teleoperation. Each demonstration requires a skilled operator to teleoperate a physical
humanoid. Resetting the robot and workspace between trials further increases collection time. This makes it
costly to collect demonstrations across many tasks and environments. To address this challenge, egocentric
human data offers an alternative.
Recently, there have been efforts to convert egocentric human demonstrations into training data for humanoid
loco-manipulation (Shi et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Lambda-humanoid Team, 2026; Zhou et al., 2026).
However, existing approaches still face two limitations. First, most of these methods use wearable motion
trackers to obtain humanoid action labels.
Each recording session involves fitting the trackers to the
demonstrator and synchronizing their motion measurements with the video. These setup requirements make
data collection more difficult to scale. Second, most of these methods use converted human trajectories as
action targets and leave the current hand–object relationship implicit. This makes precise contact harder
because the policy must infer where the robot’s hands are relative to the object before deciding how to move.
If the robot misjudges the hand–object relationship, it will reach the right object but still miss the correct
contact required by the task.

Related work
Humanoid vision–language–action models.
Humanoid VLAs learn language-guided loco-manipulation through
transferable representations and structured action generation. WholeBodyVLA (Jiang et al., 2025) and Ψ0
(Wei et al., 2026) learn from human videos before adaptation with humanoid trajectories. OpenHLM (Hu
et al., 2026) studies whole-body teleoperation, VLA adaptation, and heterogeneous co-training. Action designs
include body-part tokenization, hierarchical generation, and sparse-pose interfaces (Shao et al., 2026; Gu
et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). These interfaces differ in how much whole-body motion the policy predicts
and how much the controller supplies from compact task-space goals. HEX (Bai et al., 2026) conditions
actions with dynamics experts, while RISE (Kee et al., 2026) learns behavioral experts and their transitions.
Interaction-aware policies add whole-body tactile observations (Wang et al., 2026b) or persistent 3D object
states for action generation and geometric verification (Ren et al., 2026). EgoLoAF constructs humanoid
action supervision directly from egocentric recordings.
Humanoid learning from egocentric demonstrations.
Egocentric transfer requires aligned observations and
executable humanoid motion targets. HAT (Qiu et al., 2025) and In-N-On (Cai et al., 2025) use shared
representations and retargeting for human–robot manipulation co-training. EgoVLA (Yang et al., 2025) adapts
human hand actions to simulated humanoids using robot demonstrations and inverse kinematics. EgoHumanoid
(Shi et al., 2026) aligns observations and actions for loco-manipulation co-training. EgoHumanoid-V2 (Chen
et al., 2026) and EgoAlign (Jiang et al., 2026) use wearable tracking and humanoid motion refinement to enable
human-only target-task training. KODA (Zhou et al., 2026) combines tracker-free motion reconstruction,
contact-aware hand refinement, and robot-view rendering, but mixes real-robot demonstrations into policy
training. Other interfaces address active head–hand coordination (Zhao et al., 2026) and whole-body contact
goals (Xu et al., 2026). λ0 (Lambda-humanoid Team, 2026) scales aligned whole-body human supervision
through HumanVerse and humanoid mid-training. Transfer methods also differ in where they use geometry.
Refining a demonstrated trajectory improves the supervision available during training. Explicit affordance
conditioning provides complementary information: the current hand–object relationship from which the robot
must act during deployment. Motion refinement and affordance conditioning therefore affect different parts of
the learning pipeline. KODA’s evaluated recordings include phone depth (Zhou et al., 2026); our annotation
uses RGB and recorded camera poses, with depth estimated from images. EgoLoAF combines tracker-free
motion annotation with post-training on converted egocentric data alone. Each recording pairs future torso
and hand action targets with current object-relative hand poses as affordance conditions.

Rendered observations. We fit robot-hand meshes to the reconstructed hands, erase visible human hands and
forearms, and fill the removed regions with LaMa (Suvorov et al., 2022). Visibility labels from the original
hand and object masks are transferred to the robot meshes before compositing, preserving hand–object

3.2
Hand–object affordance construction
The action targets specify the motion to execute; hand–object affordances describe the configuration from
which that motion starts. We encode these affordances with current entity poses and the position and
orientation of each hand relative to each entity (Wang et al., 2026a). For current entity Ek and hand h, the
relative pose is
EkTHh = (BoTEk)−1BoTHh.
(3)
These relative poses are invariant to a common rigid change of reference. We use four entity slots: the two hand
TCPs, the manipulated object, and an optional second object. Each entity’s 29-dimensional vector contains
its type, its pose in Bo, both hand poses in the entity frame, and an opening indicator. The four vectors form
a 4 × 29 affordance input for the current observation. The opening indicator stores the open/closed state for
hand rows and −1 for object rows. Entity and feature masks mark missing or invalid measurements. The
supplementary material gives the complete feature definition.
Estimation from human demonstrations. We reuse the reconstructed hand poses and opening states. The
metric variant of Depth Anything V2 (Yang et al., 2024) provides monocular depth, but its scale prior can
differ from that of the hand estimator. We align the depth map using the median ratio of reconstructed
hand-surface depth to predicted depth at corresponding visible pixels, with temporal smoothing. F2G-Pose
(Zhang et al., 2026a) then estimates object poses from RGB, object masks, and the aligned depth. Camera
poses map the objects into Bo alongside the hands, allowing us to compute Eq. 3. All geometry is estimated
from the original recordings; inpainted pixels are used only in rendered policy observations.
3.3
Affordance-conditioned post-training and deployment
Affordance conditioning. We post-train π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025) with rendered images, task
instructions, current pose and opening states, and current affordances. An encoder projects each entity
row and its feature-validity mask into an affordance token; we collect these tokens in Z. Before selected
action-expert layers, the incoming hidden states Hℓreceive a residual cross-attention update:
eHℓ= Hℓ+ CAℓ(Hℓ, Z; m).
(4)
Here m masks invalid entities, and CAℓincludes query normalization and learned projections. Expert hidden
states provide the queries; affordance tokens provide the keys and values. Its output is zero when no entities
are valid. The updated states enter the action-expert layer, giving action generation direct access to current
hand–object relations while the vision-language backbone remains frozen.
Post-training. We update the action expert, action input/output projections, and affordance-conditioning
modules. The action flow-matching loss supervises the 30-target chunks in Eq. 2, excluding invalid target
components. Affordances enter as current conditions; only future actions are prediction targets.
Online affordances and execution. At deployment, robot forward kinematics and hand feedback provide
TCP poses and opening states. F2G-Pose uses head-camera RGB-D to estimate object poses. We encode
these measurements using the same affordance representation as during post-training. The policy uses robot
RGB, task instructions, current state, and these affordances to predict the next action chunk. Fixed tool
transforms convert predicted TCP poses to wrist targets. A frozen ScaleBFM controller (Zeng et al., 2026),
fine-tuned by KODA (Zhou et al., 2026), tracks these targets together with the torso motion. Hand opening is
commanded separately. Real-time chunking (Black et al., 2025) and segment blending connect asynchronous
policy predictions to continuous control. We apply spatial feedback to compensate for torso-tracking errors
during execution. The supplementary material details this correction, tool transforms, execution timing, and
the remaining implementation settings.

4
The EgoLoAF dataset
We release the EgoLoAF dataset for humanoid loco-manipulation, comprising 128,400 complete, independently
recorded demonstrations across 1,284 tasks. Each task has 100 demonstrations. After quality filtering, the
recordings total 1,000 hours.
4.1
Collection and task coverage
We collected demonstrations with a chest-mounted iPhone, without wearable motion trackers. Camera height
and pitch approximately match those of the G1 head camera. The Stera application from MobileEgo Anywhere
(Palanisamy et al., 2026) records RGB images, timestamps, camera intrinsics, and camera trajectories. This
setup leaves both hands free and requires no robot during collection.
Tasks span different manipulation goals and objects. Examples include object placement, pouring, and
interactions with containers and cabinets. The collection includes coordinated two-hand manipulation and
body movements such as walking, turning, and bending. Recordings preserve any transitions between body
repositioning and object manipulation. Figure 3 shows representative tasks and paired original and rendered
observations.
4.2
Annotations and data organization
Each demonstration includes the original RGB observations, a task instruction, estimated torso and bilateral
hand trajectories, and binary hand-opening states. The torso and hand trajectories include both position
and orientation over time. Object poses and precomputed hand–object affordances are provided for all
demonstrations. The affordances encode the current hand poses relative to the objects, following Sec. 3.2.
We also provide rendered observations that replace human arms and hands with G1 arms and O6 dexterous
hands, matching the configuration used in our real-world experiments. Original and rendered frames share
timestamps, motion targets, and affordance annotations. All motion and affordance annotations are computed
from the original recordings.
The task instruction applies to the complete demonstration, while poses, opening states, and affordances
are associated with individual observations. Training windows pair the current observation and hand–object
affordances with subsequent torso and hand motion as action supervision. Continuous trajectories support
sampling action targets at different prediction horizons without repeating motion reconstruction.
We checked each demonstration for recording completeness, camera-trajectory quality, arm and hand rendering
quality, and task completion. Entire demonstrations that failed these checks were discarded.
For each of the four experimental tasks, post-training uses 100 demonstrations from the released dataset and
50 collected separately (Sec. 5). Checkpoint selection uses separate held-out recordings. All windows from

5
Experiments
We evaluate whether EgoLoAF learns effective humanoid loco-manipulation from egocentric human task
demonstrations. We compare it with a teleoperation-based baseline under matched training-frame budgets
(Sec. 5.2), then examine how hand–object affordance conditioning benefits policies trained from human and
robot demonstrations (Sec. 5.3).
5.1
Experimental setup
Robot platform. We use a Unitree G1 humanoid with LinkerHand O6 dexterous hands and a head-mounted
Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera. We calibrate the camera-to-torso transform and construct online
affordances following Secs. 3.2 and 3.3. Grounding DINO (Liu et al., 2023) initializes or recovers object regions,
while mask propagation and SAM refinement (Kirillov et al., 2023) support subsequent pose estimates.
Execution settings. All policy comparisons share the same frozen whole-body controller and execution settings,
including spatial feedback. Only the explicitly labeled feedback ablations in the supplementary material
disable this additional correction.
Real-world tasks. Following the evaluation practice of prior studies (Shi et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026), we
select four real-world tasks to evaluate increasingly complex combinations of hand manipulation and body
motion. These tasks range from precise placement to bimanual manipulation and long-horizon sequences
involving turning, walking, bending, and squatting (Fig. 4):
1. Tissue-to-Box. This precision task requires the robot to grasp a tissue pack on the table, turn, and place
it precisely into a box.
2. Tissue-to-Bin. The robot grasps a tissue pack, then turns, squats, and bends to place it into a low bin,
combining fine hand manipulation with whole-body motion.
3. Pick-Turn-Place. The robot sequentially grasps two plush toys on the table, one in each hand, then turns
and places both into a tray, combining coordinated two-hand manipulation with torso motion.
4. Tissue-to-Cabinet. The robot grasps a tissue pack from the table, turns and walks toward a cabinet, then

5.2
Comparison with teleoperation
We first assess whether a policy post-trained solely on egocentric human task demonstrations can learn
effective robot actions. The teleoperation baseline is trained on demonstrations collected with the same G1,
recording head-camera images and body and hand states, with the same number of training frames as the ego
data for each task. Both methods use the action representation and policy/controller architecture in Secs. 3.1
and 3.3, with matched optimization budgets. EgoLoAF additionally uses affordances on all four tasks, while
the teleoperation baseline uses no affordance input.
EgoLoAF achieves a four-task mean TPS of 73.1, compared with 74.4 for teleoperation (Fig. 5a). It trails the
baseline by 0.4 points on box and 5.2 points on bin, and exceeds it by 0.6 points on plush. On the long-horizon
Tissue-to-Cabinet task, it achieves 65.8 TPS compared with 66.0 for teleoperation. These results show that
egocentric human task demonstrations provide effective supervision for humanoid loco-manipulation, including
a sequence that combines grasping, turning, walking, squatting, placement, and door closing. The comparison
evaluates the complete pipelines; Sec. 5.3 isolates the effect of affordance conditioning within each data source.
We also evaluate data-collection efficiency using person-hours (Chi et al., 2024), defined as the total working
time, in hours, summed across all participants. For matched training-frame budgets, we count the time spent
on setup, demonstration collection, failed attempts, and resets. Automated processing time that requires no
human involvement is excluded from person-hours. Egocentric collection uses one person, while teleoperation
uses two.
Figure 5b shows a mean 4.03× improvement in acquisition efficiency. Among the four tasks, Tissue-to-Cabinet
shows the largest gain (8.00×). Resetting the humanoid and task scene is time-consuming during teleoperation,
especially for long-horizon tasks, which contributes to the larger efficiency gap on this task. Collection details
are provided in the supplementary material.
5.3
Ablation: effect of hand–object affordances
We next isolate the effect of hand–object affordances within each data source, using policies trained from
human and robot demonstrations.

5.3.1
Human-data policies
Policies with and without affordance inputs use identical training frames, targets, masks, optimization budgets,
and execution settings. Figure 6 compares these policies on all four tasks, in the same order as the main
comparison.
Adding affordances raises the four-task mean TPS from 53.2 to 73.1, a gain of 19.9 points. TPS increases from
64.2 to 81.0 on Tissue-to-Box and from 55.8 to 70.0 on Tissue-to-Bin. Larger gains occur on Pick-Turn-Place,
from 50.2 to 75.4 (+25.2), and Tissue-to-Cabinet, from 42.5 to 65.8 (+23.3). The improvement narrows the
gap to the affordance-free teleoperation baseline (74.4 TPS) from 21.2 to 1.3 points.
Pick-Turn-Place and Tissue-to-Cabinet require multiple object interactions combined with body repositioning.
In observed executions, affordance conditioning helps the robot adjust reaching and placement to the current
hand–object relationship. This improves contact and coordination between hand actions and body motion
across successive task stages. These observations explain why pairing converted motion targets with current
affordance conditions makes egocentric supervision more effective for robot manipulation.
5.3.2
Robot-data policies
Affordances also improve policies trained from robot demonstrations (Table 2). With robot training data,
optimization budgets, and execution settings held fixed, the four-task mean TPS increases from 74.4 to 86.3,
a gain of 11.9 points. TPS rises from 81.4 to 90.0 on Tissue-to-Box, from 75.2 to 80.0 on Tissue-to-Bin, and
from 66.0 to 75.0 on Tissue-to-Cabinet. The largest gain again occurs on Pick-Turn-Place, from 74.8 to 100.0
(+25.2).
The improvement on robot data shows that HOA remains useful when training and deployment use the same
embodiment. Even with robot motion targets, the policy must determine how its hands should approach and
contact objects from the current observation. Providing the hand–object relationship explicitly helps guide
these actions, complementing trajectory supervision for both human-data and robot-data policies.

Conclusion
We presented EgoLoAF, a paradigm that converts egocentric human demonstrations into humanoid loco-
manipulation training data without wearable motion trackers. Each recording provides coordinated torso and
hand motion as action supervision, rendered robot-view observations, and current hand–object affordances. We
post-train the VLA entirely on the converted data, without real-robot teleoperation demonstrations. We inject
the affordances into the action expert through cross-attention while keeping the vision-language backbone
frozen. We also release 128,400 egocentric demonstration episodes spanning 1,000 hours and 1,284 tasks,
together with their converted humanoid training data. Across four real-world tasks, EgoLoAF nearly matches
the teleoperation-trained baseline (73.1 vs. 74.4 mean TPS), while more than quadrupling data-collection
efficiency on average (4.03×).

A.4
Rendered robot observations
We generate robot-view observations by replacing human hands and forearms with O6 hands and G1 arms.
We fit the robot-hand meshes to the reconstructed MANO geometry through landmark alignment and pose
optimization under the robot’s joint constraints. The fitted configurations determine the robot appearance in
each frame.
To preserve hand–object occlusions, we transfer surface visibility from the reconstructed human hands to
the robot meshes. MobileSAM (Zhang et al., 2023) provides visible hand and object masks from the original
RGB frame. We project the MANO meshes into the image and render their depth to identify the frontmost
hand surfaces. Frontmost surfaces supported by the hand masks receive visible labels, while projected hand
surfaces covered by the visible object mask receive object-occlusion labels. We establish correspondence
between MANO and O6 surfaces using the local structure of the palm and individual fingers. Each rendered
robot-hand fragment then retrieves its visibility label from the corresponding location on the posed MANO

surface. A fragment is hidden when it carries an object-occlusion label and its image projection overlaps the
visible object mask. A Z-buffer resolves occlusions within and between the rendered robot hands.
For image compositing, we remove the visible human hands and forearms and use LaMa (Suvorov et al., 2022)
to complete the exposed regions. We then composite the robot rendering onto the completed image according
to the transferred visibility labels, retaining the object in front of the occluded robot-hand regions. Action
targets and affordances are computed from the original recording and paired with the rendered observations
through their timestamps.

C
Real-robot deployment
We deploy on a Unitree G1 with LinkerHand O6 hands and a head-mounted Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D
camera. We use an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU for inference.
C.1
Online perception and affordance construction
Grounding DINO (Liu et al., 2023) initializes task-object regions and reacquires lost targets. Optical-flow
mask propagation and periodic SAM refinement (Kirillov et al., 2023) update these regions between detections.
F2G-Pose (Zhang et al., 2026a) takes RGB, measured depth, object masks, and camera intrinsics to estimate
camera-frame object poses. The calibrated camera-to-torso transform maps them into the observation-time
torso frame Bo.
Robot joint feedback and forward kinematics provide wrist poses, which the fixed tool transforms map to
TCP poses; hand feedback provides opening states. We associate these measurements with RGB-D frames
using nearby host receive timestamps. The resulting hand and object measurements populate the four-slot
affordance representation in Sec. B.2, with masks for unavailable features. Policy inference consumes the RGB
image, instruction, current state, and these affordances.

E
Dataset coverage and organization
The EgoLoAF dataset contains 128,400 independently recorded demonstrations across 1,284 tasks, with 100
demonstrations per task and 1,000 hours of video after quality filtering. This section details the task taxonomy,
representative task sequences, and the organization of observations and annotations.
E.1
Task taxonomy and coverage
The task inventory organizes manipulation into six families and 30 finer-grained operation types. These
cover object placement and organization, opening and closing, control actuation, alignment and insertion,
material transfer, and tool use on surfaces. Table S5 lists the operation types and their coverage under
three body-motion settings. The complete inventory of 1,284 tasks is provided in task_catalog.xlsx in the
supplementary material, with task IDs linking the catalogue to the examples below.
Each task specifies the objects, initial arrangement, action sequence, and completion criterion. The count of
1,284 refers to these concrete task configurations, including configurations that combine a shared manipulation
goal with different body-motion requirements. For example, placing a cup on a coaster appears as an in-place
operation, an operation requiring a turn toward a side workspace, and an operation requiring the cup to be
carried to another workspace. Each configuration has its own independent demonstrations.
The three settings distinguish how the body participates in manipulation. In-place manipulation contains
600 tasks performed within reach at one standing workspace. Posture-coordinated manipulation contains 400
tasks requiring turning, bending, squatting, or combinations of these movements within the same work area.
Loco-manipulation contains 284 tasks that connect spatially separated workspaces through walking, such as
carrying an object to a destination or retrieving a tool before using it elsewhere. This organization covers
both local hand–object interactions and their coordination with torso movement.
